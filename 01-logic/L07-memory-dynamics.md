## Statement

Retention decays over time on a forgetting curve. Effortful retrieval, spaced repetition, and
interleaved practice counteract the decay and strengthen the trace. Encoding quality and
retrieval demand are inversely related — the better the encoding, the less retrieval work is
needed ("two sides of the same coin") — and the required retrieval effort never reaches zero.

## Core claims

- Forgetting is the default: unreinforced material decays predictably; revision timing works
  against this curve, not against a calendar.
- Retrieval practice (producing from memory) strengthens retention more than re-exposure;
  difficulty during retrieval is productive, not a malfunction (testing effect, desirable
  difficulty).
- Finding many gaps in early retrieval sessions is the system working: retrieval's job is gap
  discovery as much as strengthening (the good-retrieval reframe).
- Spacing: distributing touches beats massing them. Canonical cadence: same-day → 1 day →
  1 week → 1 month, targeting 80-90% end-of-week retention at roughly 6 touches per month,
  tuned per learner and per encoding quality.
- Interleaving: mixing topics/forms within practice strengthens discrimination and
  reconstruction; at its most sophisticated it is reframing and reconstructing knowledge
  across contexts, not merely a schedule (the corpus's own corrective).
- Encoding-retrieval inverse: strong schemas (L02) decay slower and need fewer, lighter
  retrieval touches; weak encoding shows up downstream as unsustainable revision demand (the
  "mathematically unsustainable" rote-at-volume argument).
- Fluency is not retention: recognising material (rereading, mindmap-completion satisfaction)
  produces an illusion of knowing that only retrieval testing can audit (see L09).

## Independence

Without L07, the system has no time axis: nothing decays, so spacing schedules, revision
strategy, SIR, and the entire retrieval half of the curriculum have no reason to exist.
