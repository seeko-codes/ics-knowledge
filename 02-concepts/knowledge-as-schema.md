## Memory as network

Information is stored in the brain as a network, not as a list of items. The brain determines
whether information is worth retaining based on how connected it is to other information:

- If a piece of information is part of a bigger network, the brain judges it probably more
  important, and worth keeping.
- If information is isolated and not well connected, the brain judges it probably less relevant
  and prunes it.

This is framed through **cave theory**: the brain is unchanged since prehistoric times, and
modern formal-education learning is frequently disconnected from how the brain actually prefers
to learn. Aligning method with the brain's native preference — including its preference for
connected over isolated information — is presented as the reason techniques built on network
formation are more effective, engaging, and enjoyable than techniques that are not.

Isolated facts and unconnected concepts are forgotten regardless of whether they were, at the
time, "understood." A fact memorised by itself, unrelated to anything else, will still be
forgotten; a concept that is understood but not related to any other concept will still be
forgotten. Groups and relationships create the structure and organisation that produce durable
memory.

## Two relationship types

Two types of relationship-forming activity build the network:

- **Analogous relationships** — connections between new information and existing prior
  knowledge. Methods: creating accurate and comprehensive analogies; relating new information to
  knowledge already held; simplifying new terminology by renaming it something more intuitive.
- **In-context relationships** — connections between new information and other new information.
  Methods: comparing new information with other new information to see how they relate; grouping
  new information into chunks by shared similarity; drawing arrows between chunks based on how
  they affect each other; positioning chunks spatially in notes according to which other concepts
  they connect to.
- A method such as immediately applying information to solve a problem can build either type of
  relationship, or both, depending on the problem.

Both relationship types should be pursued whenever possible; many methods serve both at once. The
goal is not certainty about which category a given relationship falls into, but a standing
reminder to look for both kinds of connection.

**Priming relationships before learning.** Doing problem-solving or practice questions *before*
learning a topic helps prime the brain toward potential relationships (both types). Correct
prestudy that builds big-picture understanding increases the points of relevance available for
analogous relationships, and makes in-context relationships easier to perceive and hypothesise.

**The zero-relationship case.** If no relationship of either type can be formed with new
material, this is diagnostic: the material is too detailed for the learner's current knowledge
level. The remedy is to rebuild a broader, shallower foundation of surface-level knowledge via
prestudy before returning to the detailed material.

## Item + context = schema

A live-clinic deep dive on chunking (LC73) frames what it means for information to "make sense"
with the formula **item + context = schema**. Information needs a schema before it can enter
long-term memory; each piece of terminology comes bundled with its own mini network of
information. Having more prior patterns and knowledge makes learning new material easier — the
power of prior knowledge.

### Schema-quality spectrum

Schema quality runs from worst to best across three points:

1. **Relevance** — items are grouped for a reason.
2. **Internal connection** — the relations within the group are made explicit.
3. **Intuitiveness** — the structure leverages prior knowledge so that it "feels logical" and
   requires minimal deliberate memorisation.

**Mnemonics sit at the bottom of this spectrum.** The example given is TEPID as a mnemonic for
the causes of chest pain in medicine: a mnemonic groups items by an arbitrary surface feature
(shared first letters), not by relevance, internal connection, or intuitiveness, making it one of
the worst kinds of schema despite being memorable in the short term.

A related property named in this material is **derivability**: a well-formed chunk hints at what
the other chunks in the structure should be. (The mechanism by which derivability is assessed or
built is not elaborated in the captured text — see gaps below.)

### Confusion as signal, not obstacle

When generating chunks, confusion should be examined rather than escaped — it is treated as a
marker of real (as opposed to bypassed) learning. Conversely, feeling that a structure is
"obvious" without having first passed through confusion may indicate that learning was bypassed
rather than genuinely performed. Chunking skill level is determined by comfort with confusion and
experience generating questions — not by raw subject knowledge.

### 4 levels of chunking skill

1. **Level 1** — low comfort with confusion; low experience generating questions.
2. **Levels 2–3** — taking action, and going through self-correction loops.
3. **Level 4** — chunks become intuitive and accurate on the first pass.

### 2-4 rule

A chunk should hold 2–4 items (named in LC73 as an exact rule; the fuller rationale and
operational criteria for it sit with importance-based chunking, outside this entry's scope).

## Relation to higher-order-learning

Chunking is itself a higher-order-learning act: it requires both analysing (finding similarities
and differences) and evaluating (judging which similarities are important enough, at a
big-picture level, to justify grouping). See `02-concepts/higher-order-learning.md` for the
analyse/evaluate distinction that underlies why a schema built on evaluated importance
outperforms one built on surface similarity alone.
