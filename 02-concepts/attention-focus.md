## Statement

Attention is a trainable cognitive skill (L08) that is built, not forced: it is conditioned through cue-response-reward loops (L10) and operates inside a finite, manageable load budget (L05). The canonical skill set for self-management of attention has two independent components — a training method that increases the brain's underlying capacity to recenter itself (focus training), and a timing method that allocates work and rest around the state of that capacity as it fluctuates through a session (OFF-rest timing). Both replace an earlier, simpler technique: focus training and OFF-rest timing together replace "maintaining focus" and the fixed-interval Pomodoro technique respectively (per the Base Camp replacement table).

## Focus training

Focus training is a mindfulness-meditation-based technique for improving the brain's ability to let go of intrusive thoughts and feelings and return attention to a chosen point, rather than forcing concentration directly.

- **Mechanism**: emotional self-regulation and sustained focus are reframed as *preventing* the brain from being hijacked by thoughts and emotions, not as forcing it to concentrate. Training consists of (1) placing oneself in a situation where attention naturally drifts (typically distraction-free), and (2) practising letting go of intrusive thoughts and returning attention to a chosen anchor (the breath). The skill trained is the "letting go and returning" cycle itself, not suppression of distracting thoughts.
- **Dosing**: the therapeutic dose for significant improvement is 20 minutes/day for 30 days; a minimum-effective dose of 10 minutes/day has shown effects in program experience. Consistency is emphasized over duration.
- **Baseline expectation**: the mind wanders roughly every 3-4 seconds even in trained practitioners; after years of practice, no more than ~10 seconds of "mental silence" is typical — but this represents roughly a tripling of baseline focus ability.
- Full procedure, warnings (past trauma can resurface during practice), and progression notes: see `03-details/techniques/focus-training.md`.

## OFF-rest timing

OFF-rest timing ("Optimised-For-Focus rest timing") is a personalised replacement for the fixed-interval Pomodoro technique, designed to keep efficiency and focus high across as many work-rest cycles as possible in a day.

- **Core parameter**: rest duration = one-third of the immediately preceding focus duration (an explicit ratio, not a fixed number of minutes).
- **Trigger**: rest begins at the *first sign of mental dulling*, not at exhaustion. Waiting until noticeably tired before resting produces a much longer refocus/restoration time and lower overall productivity (analogy: pushing a boulder that is still rolling costs far less energy than restarting one that has fully stopped).
- **Break-type selection**: if relatively fresh, take a topical/miscellaneous break; if low on energy, take an active-relaxation break (focus training or mindful meditation suggested). Regardless of type, focus duration is measured with a timer and rest is set to one-third of it. For breaks under 10 minutes, Wim-Hof breathing is suggested for rapid re-energising.
- **Non-canonical variants**: Flowmodoro and the "Rule of 52/17" are cited as related but non-canonical alternatives that resemble the same underlying guidelines.
- Full procedure and bibliography note: see `03-details/techniques/off-rest-timing.md`.

## Attention management (Camp II system): flow-first vs. time-boxing

Attention management is the advanced Camp II system for optimising flow: rather than prioritising the time of day, it prioritises maintaining flow states as long as possible, bridging flow between tasks. It is an advanced skill with an explicit prerequisite of significant experience with conventional time- and task-management principles.

Key principles (from the Camp II lesson):
1. Train keen self-awareness of where attention is versus where intention is; self-regulate to prevent drifting into unintended activities.
2. Maintain flow at all times — do not manage task start/end times; operate in deep flow, trusting that flow produces the fastest high-quality output.
3. Remove decisional delays throughout the day (every decisional delay is a flow-breaker); execution should follow pre-programmed instructions.
4. Optimise environments ahead of time so no environmental decisions arise mid-day.
5. Use work-rest timing (OFF-rest) with active relaxation/recovery breathing for as many rests as possible.

Operational rules:
- **Scheduling**: use generic time blocks ("study") rather than per-task blocks; on achieving a task's quantity/depth goal, move directly to the next priority item. Small free pockets absorb non-urgent-important or urgent-unimportant tasks, or microlearning.
- **Mandatory disruptions** (classes, meetings) unavoidably break flow; plan small reprioritisation sessions before/after them.
- **Success measurement**: achievement of the planned task (e.g. "unit 3 to 80% depth") plus duration of maintained flow — not clock-time adherence. Flow-state performance is treated as near-maximum pace, so task achievement dominates elapsed time.
- **When inappropriate**: whenever bounding time spent matters more than output magnitude (classic time-boxing situations); this is why the technique is gated on conventional time-management mastery.
- **Parkinson's-law tactic**: optionally set an estimated completion time and a timer to create a deadline effect; finish early → next task; finish late → the deadline pressure wasn't the binding factor. Flagged as a push tactic, not a regular practice — timing every session creates unnecessary stress.
- **Claimed long-term benefits**: earlier noticing of procrastination/drift (faster self-correction); easier refocusing (functions as extended focus training); easier entry into efficient deep-flow states.

Tradeoff versus fixed time-boxing: fixed blocks (e.g. 25-minute Pomodoro) are simple but unoptimised on both ends — they cut off productive flow or hold a learner past focus lapse, and under-specify rest. Flow-first timing matches the brain's variable capacity but requires active self-monitoring and timer discipline.

## Decisional delays and multitasking

- **Decisional delays** — time spent deciding what to do next — are flow-breakers: each break from flow costs an average of 10-15 minutes of re-entry/cognitive disruption, easily exceeding an hour daily; they also spend limited cognitive resources on low-value planning. Identification and mitigation procedure (time-tracking audit, batch planning, distraction blocking, explicit self-instructions): see the decisional-delays section of `time-priority-management.md`.
- **Cognitive switching penalty**: multitasking degrades focus quality — the brain can only switch rapidly between two or three things, and each switch costs refocusing time. Efficient practice is strictly one task at a time; this claim underlies both flow maintenance and distraction handling.

## Distraction-handling ladder

A two-step escalation for managing in-session distraction, drawn from the BEDS-M framework's Distraction cheat sheet and Environment components (see `03-details/techniques/beds-m.md` for the full technique):

1. **Distraction cheat sheet**: a running paper log kept during a focus session. Each time attention breaks to a distraction, the distraction is written down rather than acted upon. This step alone increases general focus self-regulation by raising awareness of what actually pulls attention away.
2. **Environment**: the logged distractions are then used to drive concrete changes to the physical/digital environment (removing triggers, adding friction to competing behaviours) so the same distraction cannot recur. Environment optimisation is flagged as potentially the most powerful lever of the two — in many cases sufficient on its own to produce durable behaviour change — but the cheat sheet is what tells the learner *which* environment changes are actually worth making, rather than guessing.

The ladder is deliberately sequential: log first, then modify environment based on what was logged, rather than attempting to redesign the environment from assumptions alone.

## Competence targets

At the Base Camp checkpoint, the target competency for focus training is: consistent daily practice with easy tolerance of feelings of "boredom" or "restlessness"; a general (not just immediately post-practice) increase in focus; and intermittent use of focus training throughout the day. The target for OFF-rest timing is dynamic, near-automatic adjustment of rest timing and rest-activity type to optimise productivity.

## ADHD/ASD adjustment

Learners with hyper-focus/hyper-distractibility profiles (diagnosed or not) apply a 9-step adjustment protocol on top of the standard attention/focus techniques rather than a separate system. See `03-details/techniques/adhd-adjustments.md` for the full protocol and its specialist-deference disclaimer.

## Independence

Without L05, focus training and OFF-rest timing have no resource to manage (why rest matters, why load fluctuates). Without L10, attention training reduces to willpower ("just concentrate harder") rather than a conditioned letting-go response. Without L08, focus would not be treated as a skill that progresses through stages and needs dosed, staged practice — it would be either present or absent.
