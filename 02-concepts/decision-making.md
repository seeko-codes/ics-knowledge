## Mechanism

Decision-making is treated as an application of importance-evaluation (L03 — allocating attention/effort by
consequence-of-failure rather than comfort or urgency) and self-correction (L09 — closing the loop on one's
own choices via reflection) to the specific problem of choosing under uncertainty. The corpus locates this
material in a dedicated "Higher Ground Decision-Making" stage with four planned lessons — basic, complex,
when-changing-decisions-is-best, and a summary — of which only a thin layer of principles survives capture.
What follows is the complete surviving content; every gap is recorded explicitly rather than filled in.

## Basic decision-making — names only, locked

The basic-decision-making lesson frames most daily decisions (e.g. what to eat, what to wear) as low
consequence but still worth deciding well, and introduces two named frameworks for reducing decisional delay:
**"Heck Yes and Heck No"** and **"Choice throttling."** Neither framework's content survives: both sub-lesson
pages are client-side pages with no DOM capture, so only the names exist in the corpus, with a one-line gloss
that they help increase decisiveness and reduce decisional delays. No mechanism, procedure, or example
for either framework is recoverable from this source.

## Complex decision-making — 6 principles, 5 locked sub-lessons, Kahneman citation

The complex-decision-making overview page states six key principles directly (not as a sub-lesson, but as the
page's own content):

1. The future is unknown and all decisions are uncertain; expecting certainty in complex decisions is
   unrealistic.
2. Good decisions are about managing probability and information.
3. Indecision is itself a decision, and deciding based on comfort may reflect status quo bias, which may
   cause harm.
4. Opportunity, uncertainty, and information all change over time and are related to each other.
5. Holding off on a decision to gain more information is acceptable, but the consequences of holding off
   should be consciously weighed.
6. Information and risk are modifiable through one's own actions; gaining information earlier in the process
   is preferable.

The page cites **Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler (1991)** on status quo bias and loss aversion in decision-making,
and links a general reference on status quo bias, as further reading.

Five named sub-lessons were planned to elaborate these principles into a fuller "increasingly uncertain
situations" treatment, and **all five are NOT CAPTURED** (client-side pages, no DOM capture):

- Protecting your biggest downsides
- What defines a good decision?
- Positional decisions and expected value
- Perfectionism and overthinking
- Applying theory to reality

No procedure, worked example, or operational method for any of the six principles above survives beyond the
one-line statements quoted here.

## When changing decisions is best — principles + Annie Duke reframe; no decision rule given

This lesson survives in full (single page, fully captured), but its content is persuasive/reframing, not
procedural. Stated principles:

- Changing decisions is affected by several cognitive biases: status quo bias, sunk cost bias, and loss
  aversion bias.
- "Probability concentration" shows that changing decisions can be advantageous (the mechanism of probability
  concentration itself is asserted, not explained, in the source).
- Decisions made later, with more information, can be more appropriate than persisting with the original
  decision.

The lesson's central reframe: persisting with a decision is often conflated with resilience or grit, but
resilience is only the right response when the decision still aligns with the learner's goals, values, and
priorities — otherwise, changing a decision quickly is a sign of adaptive, critical thinking rather than
weakness. It quotes decision-making consultant Annie Duke: **"Quitting isn't always a sign of defeat. Highly
successful people often know exactly when to give up."**

**Despite its title, the lesson gives no decision rule, threshold, or procedure for identifying the moment
when changing a decision is actually best.** This is recorded explicitly as a gap: the operational content
implied by the title lives only in the page's uncaptured video, if anywhere.

## Positional decision-making (cross-reference)

"Positional decision-making" is named within the complex-decision-making sub-lesson list above
("Positional decisions and expected value" — NOT CAPTURED) and recurs independently in a live clinic as a
framework for triage under irreducible overload, accepting that a choice will be locally bad ("is going to
suck, and that's okay") in order to preserve a better overall position. That clinic-sourced material is
carried in full under time-priority-management (self-management cluster), not here, since it was captured as
part of a scheduling/overload discussion rather than as part of this stage. This file records the
cross-reference only; no duplicate content is kept in both places.

## Higher-order-thinking-for-decisions application (LC55)

A live clinic (LC55, "Using iCanStudy skills in other contexts beyond studying") applies higher-order thinking
explicitly to decision-making and problem-solving as one of its topics, with a guest (Aaron) contributing
personal experience of applying higher-order thinking to decision-making, and a claim that this way of
thinking increases the quality of conversations and contributions. The clinic file itself survives only as an
abstract paragraph and a chapter-title timestamp table (no transcript prose captured, consistent with the
rest of the live-clinics format in this range): the relevant chapters are "Decision making: how higher-order
thinking skills can also apply to variables that pose uncertainties" (00:09:19) and "Aaron's personal
experiences with applying higher-order thinking to decision making" (00:12:39). The clinic also points
explicitly to the Higher Ground Decision-Making module (this file) as the place for more detail on decision
making — i.e. the corpus's own cross-reference confirms this is the intended home for decision-making content,
even though that module's own operational content is what is missing (see above).
