## Mechanism

Exam execution is treated as a distinct layer on top of the encoding/retrieval system: even with a complete
learning and self-management system in place, performance under pressure is further shaped by appraisal
regulation (L11 — stress/anxiety gating retrieval), automatized in-exam procedure (L08 — technique executed
under time and pressure constraints), and schema quality under compression (L02 — cramming and essay-writing
both depend on a chunk structure that can be traversed fast). The corpus separates this into: a standing
exam-technique checklist, an in-class technique checklist (both High-Yield phase), cramming principles and
their canonical implementation (Multipass), and a cluster of exam-specific optimisations at Summit
(ReCOVer, MR FIG, breaching questions, silly-mistake diagnosis, best-attempt encoding under volume).

## Exam-technique checklist (fundamentals-2/01) and in-class technique

**Exam execution guidelines** (High-Yield, standing checklist for assessment days):

1. Get good sleep, especially before exams — at least 9 hours in bed per night. Sleep is essential for exam
   execution due to sleep-dependent memory consolidation.
2. Use priority-checking: double-check answers that are unsure while maintaining an optimum pace elsewhere.
3. Think like an examiner: evaluate knowledge and consider how to use it to solve challenging problems,
   especially in ways not yet confident about (cross-refers to the interleaving table for methods).
4. Use slow, deep breathing to activate the parasympathetic response, mitigate anxiety spirals, and — over
   time — train out of habitual anxiety pathways.

For learners who only get nervous in high-pressure exams specifically: **graduated exposure therapy** —
placing oneself in increasingly similar scenarios to what triggers nervousness (practising under strict time
pressure, exam conditions, and involving friends to increase competition). For pre-exam forgetting of these
tips under stress: build a pre-exam checklist and schedule its review, potentially weeks ahead.

**In-class technique** (live, un-pausable learning events — lectures, workshops, webinars) is flagged as a
high-yield lesson: learners applying it are reported to see dramatic improvements, and learners skipping it
show significant issues. Guidelines:

1. Always prestudy — prestudy improves retention, engagement, and enjoyment in class.
2. Focus prestudy on big-picture concepts, not details.
3. Always paraphrase and process information before writing notes.
4. Don't avoid feeling challenged — good learning feels uncomfortable because the brain is actively working.
5. Write non-linear notes — mindmaps and spatial representations are more effective than linear notes.
6. Limit word-count — more writing means less processing, since learning happens in the brain, not on paper.

Prestudy is stated to be more valuable than reviewing previous material when time-constrained ("if the bucket
leaks, it's more important to plug the holes than to keep filling it"), because effective prestudy reduces
future forgetting and prevents continual overwhelm. Correct prestudy execution should produce: less
overwhelm, better consolidation, general awareness of main ideas, and the ability to ask deeper or more
interconnected questions; absence of these signals a technique-execution problem, not a fundamentally wrong
approach. Developing sufficient prestudy skill takes approximately 1-2 weeks of daily practice (approximate,
learner-paced). Struggling to process before writing indicates a deep-processing weakness, remedied by
deliberately delaying note-taking longer over time while relating information to the big picture.

## Cramming principles and when

**Cramming** is defined as taking a large block of knowledge and processing it in a short span of time,
usually before an exam — and is stated to be "very useful if done correctly." The naive failure mode is
grabbing a large volume of content and learning it with normal-pace techniques compressed into a few days or
weeks, which is risky and stressful specifically when the underlying techniques have low retention or are
inefficient at building mastery. An efficient cramming system must satisfy three principles:

1. It must allow strategic content-skipping to make the most of available learning time.
2. It must achieve a retention rate and mastery depth sufficient for the best possible outcome.
3. It must be efficient enough not to sacrifice sleep (protecting sleep-dependent memory consolidation and
   avoiding cognitive-performance decline from sleep deprivation).

**Cram early** is named as one of the easiest and most helpful changes available: completing the
course/curriculum well ahead of the deadline frees the remaining time to be spent stress-free, with only
periodic SIR needed to catch gaps. Learners operating at this level are reported to complete a typical
one-year curriculum in "just a couple of months" of focused work. The canonical cramming system is
**Multipass** — full specification at ../03-details/techniques/multipass.md.

## Multipass (summary; full spec: ../03-details/techniques/multipass.md)

Multipass is the strategy layer for *when* and in what order to learn dense material across multiple
sessions, implemented on top of the Bear Hunter System (BHS supplies the per-session mechanism; Multipass
supplies the session-dividing strategy and high-level order control). It requires prior mastery of order
control, inquiry-based learning, and VPReFRE note-writing. Two variants — inquiry-led and objectives-led —
trade naturalness/engagement against performance-directness; both prioritise logic and concepts layers before
details, the opposite of naive detail-first cramming, because most exam questions are derivable from
logic/concepts once those layers are solid. Comparative table and use-cases: see the technique file.

## Best-attempt encoding and 4-tier confidence prioritisation (LC66)

For ultra-high-volume exams (the corpus cites competitive exams such as UPSC or medical entrance tests), rote
repetition and flashcards alone are stated to be **mathematically unsustainable** at volume. The clinic
frames the response as two parallel processes:

- **Process 1: frequent, structured self-testing** — high-volume testing strategies to spot gaps early,
  targeted retrieval, and effective use of AI/flashcards for the lower-order layer.
- **Process 2: best-attempt encoding and mind-map organisation** — encoding to the best standard achievable
  given available time, explicitly avoiding overly rigid or overly vague chunk structures (contrasted via
  medicine/history examples of intuitive vs poor structure), with gaps subsequently diagnosed as
  higher-order, lower-order, or procedural in nature.

Because full coverage at full depth is not achievable under extreme volume, confidence is deliberately
triaged across a **4-tier layered prioritisation, in order: core > extended > examples > fine details**.
Accepting deliberate gaps at the lower tiers is treated as part of the mechanism working correctly, not a
failure of it — the clinic frames this as "accepting uncertainty and being intentional about what to skip"
rather than attempting uniform coverage. Encoding skill should be matched to available study time, and
conceptual relevance plus test trends are used to guide prioritisation. This clinic file survives only as an
abstract paragraph and a chapter-title timestamp table (no transcript prose was captured); the mechanism
above is reconstructed from those titles and is not further elaborated in the source text.

## Exam-imminent strategy (formal-education/97)

Distinct from the in-exam techniques above, this is program-navigation strategy for someone approaching an
assessment soon: **"best possible result" is not the same as "best result"** — large jumps (e.g. a predicted
60-70% turning into 95% within weeks) are explicitly called unrealistic, since change takes time. The
governing rule is to gain mastery over current techniques and fill immediate gaps *without destabilising the
existing system* — full detail, including the expected-improvement table and the new-member/existing-member
split, is in technique-progression's "when-to-not-advance rules" section, since this is fundamentally a
progression-pacing rule rather than an in-exam procedure.

## Silly-mistakes diagnosis (pointer)

"Silly mistakes" are rarely pure exam-technique errors; the corpus's root-cause table (5 causes, each with
symptoms and a matched solution) is the diagnostic layer that determines which of the above techniques
