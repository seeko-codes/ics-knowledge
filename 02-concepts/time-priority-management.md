## Statement

Time, like attention and study effort, is a finite resource allocated by an explicit judgment of importance — never by presented order, urgency, or comfort (L03). Urgency-driven allocation is the canonical failure mode. Once the important, non-urgent work is identified, executing on it reliably is a conditioning problem (L10): schedules, buffers, and protected blocks work because they remove the need for willpower-driven decisions in the moment, not because they are motivating.

## Urgency trapping

**Importance** is defined precisely, decoupled from urgency: a task is important if and only if the consequence of failure is **unacceptable and unmanageable**. Urgency trapping is the failure of prioritising urgent-but-unimportant tasks over important-but-non-urgent ones, which perpetually consumes available time on the urgent while the important-but-neglected gap (e.g., a skill deficit) keeps widening — by the time the neglected item is felt as urgent, it is often too late to close the gap in time. The fix is not to schedule urgent tasks later in the day, but to schedule important-and-non-urgent tasks *first* and fit urgent tasks around them (e.g., a fixed 20-minute evening block for a non-urgent priority, with the rest of the day filled around it).

## Priority 0+1 system (canonical)

An intermediate time-management and prioritisation technique combining a vision-level layer (Priority 0) with a modified Eisenhower-Matrix daily/weekly task layer (Priority 1).

- **Priority 0** — core values and long-term **vision** (not a SMART goal): an open, abstract idea of the type of person one wants to be and the type of life one wants to live. A vision is deliberately broader than a specific goal because personality and priorities are near-certain to change over time (especially, in modern society, before age 30); a narrow long-term goal risks tunnel vision, while a vision serves as a broad calibration checklist for medium-term goals. Reviewed **once every 6 months**, evaluated open-mindedly rather than assumed static — the review should actively look for evidence that the vision has changed, not merely confirm it hasn't.
- **Priority 1** — daily-or-weekly task prioritisation, using a modified Eisenhower Matrix (importance × urgency quadrants). Tasks are prioritised and scheduled the day before; on the day, newly emergent tasks are captured in a list and fed into the matrix for the next day's scheduling. Repeated daily for busier/more variable schedules, weekly for more stable ones.
- **Procedure**: (1) identify the top priority via reverse goal setting; (2) block time for the top priority; (3) fill the remaining timetable with lower priorities; (4) on unforeseen events, shuffle lower-priority items to protect the top-priority block.
- **Confidence to delete tasks**: if a task is truly important, it will resurface on its own. Barring ample free time and willingness to spend it on that task over relaxation, sleep, or other non-urgent-important work, deleting it is not a loss.

Full procedure including the P0-identification FAQ content (how often P0/P1 change, vision-vs-goal distinction, task-deletion confidence): `03-details/techniques/priority-0-1.md`.

### Task-management extensions (Camp II)

Used in addition to Priority 0+1 once it is established:
1. **2-minute rule**: tasks finishable within 2 minutes are completed immediately.
2. **Eat the Frog**: do the most-avoided tasks first.
3. **Manage dependencies**: identify tasks that future tasks depend on (skill development, coordination with others) and clear those bottlenecks even when they do not look high-priority in isolation.

## Decisional delays

Decisional delays — time spent deciding what to do next — can cost over an hour per day: each break in flow requires roughly 10-15 minutes to re-enter, consumes limited cognitive resources on planning, and confers no meaningful advantage.

Identification (one-week audit): (1) track all time with a time-tracking app for ~1 week; (2) end task-tracking each time a deep-work/flow state breaks, restart on re-entering focus; (3) compare tracked time to the planned schedule; (4) identify unplanned between-task time and count flow breaks (each ≈10-15 min of disruption); (5) estimate total delay/disruption. Awareness via tracking is itself the first-line remedy.

Mitigation: (1) batch planning/prioritisation at the start or end of each day — no mid-day planning; (2) block distractions from focus zones so new decisions cannot enter; (3) write instructions to oneself during planning so execution requires no decisions.

## Protecting time

A 4-step procedure applied after important tasks are scheduled: (1) schedule the important tasks first; (2) identify factors that put those scheduled tasks at risk; (3) take steps to protect the task from those risks, or — if it cannot be protected — move it to a safer time; (4) repeat for lower-priority tasks. The underlying rule: do not schedule important work into a block that cannot realistically be protected from risk.

## Scheduling parameters (scaffold, retained)

A checklist-style set of concrete scheduling guidelines, sequenced after weekly priorities are set:

1. Schedule the top two priority items first.
2. Use a digital app for most people; pen-and-paper is sufficient for low task volume.
3. Be specific in scheduling entries; break study time into **30-minute blocks**, with breaks/gaps left in.
4. **Overestimate time required per task by 25-50%.**
5. Leave a **15-minute buffer** between tasks.
6. Reserve **at least 1 hour per day unscheduled** as emergency time.
7. Schedule time at the end of every day for reflection and next-day priority-setting.

## Reverse goal setting

Any method that dissects a desired outcome into its determinants and traces them back to the current position, used both for identifying Priority 0/1 targets and for broader planning. Two directions, each with its own feasibility check:

- **Top-down**: start at the end and work backwards. Best when a goal is complex/multi-component, or current skill/attribute level is hard to assess. Feasibility check: does the *true* current state match the *supposed* current state arrived at by tracing backward from the goal? A mismatch (needing to already be in a better state than one actually is) means the plan is infeasible as designed.
- **Bottom-up**: start at the current state and work forwards. Best when current skill/attribute level is clear, or later-stage requirements are unclear. Feasibility check: can the step-wise projected development reach the goal within the deadline, given the anticipated pace of development? If not, the plan must be reconsidered.

Both directions still count as reverse goal setting as long as the required skills/attributes are identified by analysing the desired goal itself. Many plans benefit from using both directions to cross-check for gaps. Full method: `03-details/techniques/reverse-goal-setting.md`.

## Performance goals and goal-setting principles

- **Performance goals** are process- and attribute-based (not outcome-based), giving earlier corrective feedback than waiting on results. Candidate attributes: time management, focus/procrastination, learning efficiency, learning consistency, stress management — a learner selects just a few to avoid overloading and failing at all of them simultaneously.
- **Goal-setting principles**: don't sacrifice medium/long-term success for short-term outcomes (avoid chronic catch-up); hard work alone does not produce uncommon results — method and direction of effort matter; long-term direction is more important than any specific goal, since circumstances and information change; goals themselves should be specific and measurable (SMART framing is appropriate at this level, distinct from the broader, non-SMART Priority-0 vision).
- **Self-assessment caution**: the brain is poor at self-assessing ability; this risk compounds for high achievers, who can often safely ignore general advice but who also carry the highest risk of struggling with the program specifically because they tend to rush fundamentals ("this doesn't apply to me" / "I'm different" thinking).

## Execution / Sharpening / Life allocation

Discretionary time (weekends, off-days) is allocated across three categories depending on which pressure currently dominates:

- **Execution** — direct output toward current obligations.
- **Sharpening** — developing the skills/processes that make future execution more efficient.
- **Life** — rest, relationships, and wellbeing maintenance.

**Three horsemen of pressure**: debt, timescale, sustainability. Neglecting one category accrues **category-specific debt** in the others:

- Over-investing in Execution accrues both **Life debt** and **Sharpening debt** (skills remain undeveloped while problems keep accumulating in a different form).
- Investing in Sharpening while under-executing accrues **Execution debt** (problems still accumulate even while skill develops).
- Over-prioritising Life (feeling good) carries its own consequences for the other two categories.

If the pace of demands (timescale) is accelerating, maintaining the same speed of work means falling further behind in relative terms, even without any decline in absolute output. It takes longer to fix a problem than to understand how to fix it — but understanding first is still faster overall than attempting fixes without it.

## Positional decision-making under overload

Under genuinely irreducible overload (multiple competing life commitments with no slack remaining), the applicable framework is **positional decision-making**: the available choice is accepted as one that "is going to suck" rather than searched for as an optimal one, in order to escape or prevent a "deathly spiral." The operative move is to regain hold of the controls — take the locally-worse action that restores some control over the trajectory, rather than continuing to search for a locally-comfortable option while control is lost. This generalises beyond study-specific triage to broader distraction/environment management under overload, and connects to the same short-term-loss-for-positional-gain logic used in identity change (see `mindset-emotion.md`).

## Calendar philosophy

- **Overscheduling is a distinct failure mode**, independent of task selection quality: a full schedule that cannot be followed creates an illusion of productivity, makes true workload capacity impossible to assess, degrades time-estimation accuracy, and normalises falling behind (with a corrosive effect on wellbeing). The remedy is to start with relaxed, manageable timing and add tasks only as time-management skill improves — not to start ambitious and cut back after failing.
- **Relaxation and fun must be scheduled**, not left to "whatever time is left." Skipping this under time pressure produces more unintentional procrastination-driven breaks, which causes falling further behind and contributes to burnout over time.
- **Theory-time budget**: 1-3 hours/week of program theory is recommended as a default; learners already studying more than 20 hours/week may be able to manage up to 5 hours/week of theory before risking overload. This is the same 5:1 practice:theory ceiling elaborated below, applied at the calendar-planning stage.

## Pace adjustments (practice-time ceiling)

Correct practice **replaces** existing study techniques within the hours already being spent, rather than adding extra time on top of current methods — treating new-technique practice as "extra" work is a primary cause of technique abandonment.

| Correct way to practise | Incorrect way to practise |
|---|---|
| Study similar hours as before, but replace current techniques with new ones | Study similar hours as before using current techniques, then spend extra time practising new techniques |

**Ceiling**: never exceed a **5:1 ratio of practice to theory** time, to prevent overload (e.g., 25 hours/week of study caps theory at 5 hours/week — and even that may be excessive for some learners). Progress up to a 40:1 ratio has been observed. On entering the Growth phase specifically, a slowdown in visible progress is expected and normal (cognitive retraining proceeds progressively and cannot be rushed) rather than a signal that something has gone wrong.

## Independence

Without L03, none of the above has a selection criterion: urgency trapping loses its importance/urgency distinction, Priority 0/1 loses its ranking logic, and positional decision-making loses the basis for accepting one "bad" option over another. Without L10, protecting time, scheduling parameters, and the practice-replaces-theory ceiling would have no behavioural mechanism — they would be advice rather than antecedent-level design that removes reliance on in-the-moment willpower.
