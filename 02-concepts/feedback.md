## Definition and function

Feedback is information about performance or understanding that guides improvement. Three
functions: (1) make sense of progress achieved, (2) identify new areas of improvement toward a
learning goal, (3) guide future decisions about learning behaviour and strategy. Feedback is
described metaphorically as a GPS: it shows current position, helps assess progress, and guides
recalibration.

## Process-oriented vs. outcome-oriented goals

Not all learning goals are equally valuable for long-term growth. **Process-oriented goals** —
goals that improve *how* learning happens rather than *what* is learned (asking better questions,
mastering a technique like mind mapping, improving how reflection is done, building habits that
make learning a regular part of life) — are flagged as the most valuable category for long-term
growth, in implicit contrast to goals defined purely by outcome.

## Sources of feedback

| Source | Reliability / use case |
|---|---|
| **Finder AI search** | In-platform AI search; ~90% accuracy on the most common questions; used for questions about taught concepts/techniques; recommended before seeking coach feedback |
| **Coach feedback** | Highly reliable information about performance; requires acting on the advice received to produce improvement; can be time-consuming to obtain |
| **Self-feedback** | Reliability depends on the skill of the self-evaluator; once developed, the single most overall valuable source, since it enables rapid improvement cycles independent of any external source; recommended as the first response in all situations |

## The CLEAR framework

Full specification (Context, Learning Goal, Evaluate Your Work, Ask Targeted Questions, Review &
Follow-up): ../03-details/techniques/clear-feedback.md. Purpose: balance enough situational
context against tight enough focus that a feedback request produces clear, actionable, goal-
relevant guidance rather than either precise-but-vague or broad-and-unfocused responses.

## Closing the feedback loop

Best learning outcomes occur when learners both actively engage with feedback *and* use it to
produce new work — "closing the feedback loop": receive → analyse → implement → produce new work
→ receive more feedback (cyclical). Full 3-step procedure and worked example:
../03-details/techniques/clear-feedback.md.

## Region-beta boundary for feedback-giving

Applied specifically to *giving* feedback to others: a region-beta boundary is the balance between
how much discomfort a piece of advice will cause the recipient and how much discomfort that
recipient is currently willing/able to tolerate. Readiness to receive feedback varies by person
and by time — rejection of feedback at one point is not permanent; the same advice may land later.
Personal pride and ego are named as factors affecting feedback quality on both the giving and
receiving side.

## Learning by giving feedback

Giving feedback to others develops the *declarative* aspect of one's own skill, since it exposes
the giver to new perspectives on the same material or technique. Three principles for effective
feedback-giving:
1. Assume there is a gap in one's own judgement; do not fixate on a first analysis.
2. Explore and discover the other person's process first, before critiquing it.
3. Ask oneself: "How much of that feedback would someone else give to me?"

The "compliment sandwich" is critiqued as needing to be more than a generic statement to be
useful. Building rapport and avoiding a confrontational tone are named as part of communicating
feedback effectively, alongside the underlying content of the feedback itself.

## Effective question framing

Reroute from an ineffective to an effective question form (also documented as a fixed-mindset
symptom in ../02-concepts/self-diagnosis.md and ../02-concepts/mindset-emotion.md):

- **Ineffective**: "What if I make this mistake?" — a low-probability hypothetical that wastes
  clarity-seeking effort on a scenario that may never occur.
- **Effective**: "I tried this and found I made this mistake. How could I overcome it?" — rooted
  in real, already-occurred experience.

Structured experiment-framing question, used after an attempt: **what I did → what I found → what
I think the issue is → what I propose as the next experiment.**

| Question type | When to ask | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Calibration ("How do I know if I've done this correctly?", "What does correct feel like?") | Before/during attempts | May not always be answerable in words; some things are only known through experience |
| Unnecessary-risk-avoidance ("Are there common mistakes I should be aware of?", "Are there situations where this method is inappropriate?") | Before/during attempts | Open-ended, not tied to a specific hypothetical; not every mistake needs to be made personally |
| Experiment-framing (what I did / found / think / propose) | After attempts | Directly rooted in real experience rather than speculation |
