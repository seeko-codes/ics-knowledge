## The cycle

Practice does not automatically improve skill — only *correct* practice does. Kolb's Experiential
Learning Cycle is the framework used to make practice productive: **Experience → Reflection →
Abstraction → Experimentation**, cyclical (the experiment becomes the next cycle's experience).
Full operational procedure, per-stage guidance, and the common-mistakes table:
../03-details/techniques/kolbs-procedure.md. Worked instances: ../03-details/examples/kolbs-exemplars.md.

**Correction marker.** What is taught is explicitly a modification of the standard Kolb's cycle,
arising from years of teaching experience and observation of the original cycle's practical
limitations. Learners are directed away from learning the technique from other sources, since the
version taught differs from how Kolb's is typically presented elsewhere.

## Why a guided, structured reflection tool exists

Objective self-assessment of one's own knowledge gaps is inherently difficult — a captured
learner remark ("I think it's really hard for a lot of people, myself included, to analyze your
mistakes/gaps in your knowledge that objectively. I suppose that's why Kolb's is so important")
frames this directly as the rationale for using a *structured* reflection tool rather than
free-form self-review. This is consistent with the cue-utilisation account of metacognition (see
../02-concepts/metacognition.md): a guided form exists because unguided reflection is where
learners are least able to self-correct. A `/guided-kolbs` form tool is provided specifically to
produce consistent cycles and make feedback on them easier to give.

## Reflection should outweigh abstraction

Reflection is required to be longer than abstraction. The rationale for this ordering rule is
posed directly by a learner ("Why does the reflection in a Kolb's cycle need to be longer than
the abstraction? ... what does it indicate?") but the answer is not present in the captured
record — this is a taught criterion whose mechanistic justification is not stated in the
available text. A related, unresolved process concern is reflection and abstraction "blending
together" (an experimental idea arriving immediately, without the reflection stage doing its
separating work first) — raised as a possible bad tendency, also without a captured resolution.

## Scope: no more than 3 topics

An explicit numeric ceiling is named: "we shouldn't do Kolb's about more than 3 topics." This
creates a live tension when problems found during a reflection dedicated to one topic turn out
to be much larger elsewhere — whether it is acceptable to switch the cycle's topic mid-reflection
when a bigger, unrelated issue surfaces is raised by a learner and not resolved in the captured
text.

## Time calibration

A full, high-quality cycle should take around 30 minutes or less for most people; longer sessions
are reserved for very complex issues. Because Kolb's is sequential, a limitation in an earlier
stage degrades every later stage's quality — so capping session length is a deliberate response
to that dependency, not just a time-management convenience.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Resolution |
|---|---|---|
| Reflecting is tedious or confusing | Limited self-awareness/insight into meta-learning, making it hard to know what is meaningful enough to reflect on | Develop this at high priority, since it limits all skill development; improves with insight over **4–12 weeks** |
| Kolb's sessions take too long | Limited self-awareness, metacognition, or critical-reflection skill; no clear focus | A high-quality session should be completable within **30 minutes**; because the stages are sequential, it is appropriate to cap sessions at 30 minutes and skip completing all steps until efficiency and speed improve naturally with repeated sessions |

## Transitional Kolb's (week-anchored variant)

A learner-proposed and clinic-discussed variant anchors a full week of Kolb's cycles to a single
chosen marginal gain rather than to a fresh experience each time:
1. Generate marginal gains.
2. Pick one marginal gain to work on for the upcoming week.
3. Every Kolb's cycle that week focuses on experimenting with and gaining experience on that one
   marginal gain.
4. At the end of the week, review progress; if little progress was made, continue for another
   week and reassess.

The named risk of this variant is neglecting unrelated issues that arise mid-week, which might
otherwise warrant their own cycle (example given: the week's chosen gain is importance-based
chunking, but a sudden increase in cognitive load shows up unrelated to that gain). A possible
mitigation raised is running more than one Kolb's cycle per day, though the same learner reports
already struggling to complete even one cycle daily. A second named risk is losing sight of the
week's chosen gain and instead reverting to reflecting on whatever issue came up that particular
day.

## Rumination contrast

Not all reflection is productive. Reflecting on problems and feelings without processing them
into meaning or change is **rumination** — a pattern characteristic of anxiety and affective
disorders. The specific question series taught for Kolb's is designed to reduce cognitive biases
and common reflection mistakes observed over years of coaching, precisely to keep reflection from
collapsing into rumination.

## Relation to marginal gains

Marginal gains and Kolb's are complementary, not redundant: marginal gains is the perspective of
progress as small compounding steps (the "road" — what progress looked like); Kolb's is the
mechanism for producing each step (the "engine" or vehicle — how progress was made). Marginal
gains does not specify *how* to gain the next 1%; Kolb's fills that role. Full comparison:
../02-concepts/marginal-gains.md.

Confidence to experiment is described as coming from trusting the Kolb's process itself: success
is effectively guaranteed either by executing correctly or by extracting valuable learning from a
failed attempt. Early cycles at the conscious-incompetence stage typically do not fix a problem
at all — most early gains come from discovering more types of mistakes, even before a first
success.

## Frequency

A cycle is recommended at the end of every day where possible, or at minimum twice per week.
Waiting too long before reflecting loses detail and introduces confounding variables — reflecting
on an entire week of studying, for example, conflates what were really several separate
experiences into one artificially large unit.
