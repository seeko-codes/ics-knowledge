Layers of learning is a structural framework for sequencing what to learn and how much of it to
process at each stage, flagged in the source material as one of the program's core pillar
concepts (equal status to importance-based chunking). It specifies four layers, moving from the
innermost (most global) to the outermost (most granular).

## The four layers

- **Logic (layer 1, innermost)** — the "backbone": the biggest concepts, chunks, and most
  important relationships. Built by asking "Why is it important?" and "How does it relate?" and
  seeing how everything logically connects. People often skip this step entirely — likened to
  rushing to build a house while neglecting the foundation.
- **Concepts (layer 2)** — the main subject-specific details and ideas that make up the bulk of
  understanding. Built on top of layer 1 by asking "How does it happen?" and "What is it?".
  Students who ignore layer 1 will often struggle with layer 2.
- **Details, important (layer 3)** — specific information that often needs to be rote-learned,
  but which is *important* because it helps the concept it is attached to be understood more
  concretely (as opposed to *arbitrary* detail). Identified by the elicitation question: "Does
  knowing this detail help me understand the concept it is attached to?" Yes → important; no →
  arbitrary.
- **Details, arbitrary (layer 4, outermost)** — disconnected pieces of information that may seem
  random or unnecessary but remain in the curriculum or exam. Learned only because assessments
  require them; must be rote-learned, at least until enough later learning makes them naturally
  make sense.

## Volume split

Of the total volume of information in a topic: ~10% is conceptual (layer 2), ~20% is important
details (layer 3), and the remaining ~70% is arbitrary details (layer 4). Consequence: studying
straight through from beginning to end (no layering) means most study time is spent on the
unconnected 70%, producing poor retention because most of what's being memorised has no
significance or meaning yet. Tree analogy: a tree may have 10,000 leaves but only 5–6 main
branches; spending time on the branches lets the leaves arise more naturally.

## Layers-are-dynamic rule

Layers are not static. How relevant a detail *seems* depends on whether layers 1–2 give it
structure: errors in building an importance-based logic and concepts layer mean more details
appear "arbitrary" than actually are. The lever for reducing perceived layer-4 volume is
therefore not memorising harder — it is fixing layers 1–2. Starting with deep, foundational
information dramatically increases overall studying speed because it converts what would
otherwise be arbitrary, unconnected details into relevant, connected ones.

## Overinvest-in-1-2 rule

"As a general guide, it is usually best to overinvest in developing clear layers 1 and 2, as
this makes a dramatic difference to the time it takes to cover layers 3 and 4, where the
majority of time can sometimes be wasted."

## Revisability schedule

The logic and concepts layers remain hypothetical and can be refined or adjusted at any point in
the learning process. Initial chunk-structure hypotheses are rarely correct on the first attempt
— they exist mainly to offload cognitive load into a structure that can then be refined as
understanding deepens. The likelihood of needing to change the structure again once inside the
details layers is "generally low (but not impossible)" — contingent on whether the structure was
continually updated during the earlier logic/concepts layers. In short: revisability is full
through logic and concepts, and declines but never reaches zero once detail layers are reached,
and that decline is earned by prior-layer iteration quality, not automatic. It is explicitly a
mistake to judge information as an arbitrary detail and commit it to rote memorisation before
completing the proper inquiry process on it.

## Operationalisation via Aim-Shoot (no separate procedure)

There is no dedicated procedure for identifying the four layers. "The Aim-Shoot cycle is useful
for identifying all layers." Order control and layers of learning are related but distinct:
order control decides *which order* to rearrange information consumption into; layers of
learning specify *how* to structure it once reordered.

## Important-vs-arbitrary detail test question

"Does knowing this detail help me understand the concept it is attached to?" — yes places the
detail in layer 3 (important); no places it in layer 4 (arbitrary).

## Layering ↔ cognitive-load mechanism

(Source: support article "How does layering improve learning and cognitive load?", gated to
Ascent 2+ readers as its natural prerequisite level.)

- Early in learning a new topic, the brain needs a big-picture frame of relevance before it can
  usefully process detail.
- Prestudy is the predecessor mechanism: it splits cognitive load across multiple focused
  sessions to build the big picture and relevance ahead of the main learning event (figure:
  "Learning with prestudying" — cognitive load on creating knowledge structures spread over
  time).
- Layering extends this by giving order control and inquiry a clearer focus, via
  *self-regulated scaffolding*: deconstructing a topic and reconstructing it in a form that is
  easier and more manageable to understand and develop expertise in (cites Instructional
  Scaffolding).
- True expertise and knowledge is inherently structured in layers — organised, multi-directional,
  non-linear, grouped (figure: "How knowledge and expertise is structured" — a mindmap showing
  logic/concepts/details branching outward and connecting non-linearly). This structure normally
  forms only after years of learning; layering deliberately compresses that formation.
- Source material presents knowledge in **mixed layers**: details and concepts interspersed with
  infrequent hints of logic, and logic/concepts are often not represented in headings at all
  (figure: "How knowledge is presented" — mixed layers in textbook pages and lecture slides).
  Learning in that presented order, without order control, produces cognitive overload because
  relevance keeps fluctuating.
- Layer contents are usually not given explicitly by the source; they must be constructed by the
  learner using the chunking methods already trained (figure: "How knowledge is split into
  layers using cognitive effort"). Sometimes the apparent "logic" in a resource is not the best
  chunk to use once independently evaluated through importance-based chunking.
- Conventional (unlayered) learning encounters high-detail information very early; relevance
  fluctuates, so difficulty stays high throughout — tiring and inefficient (figure: "How people
  normally learn" — fluctuating relevance/detail causing high difficulty throughout).
- Layered learning instead follows a 3-step investment: (1) spend more effort upfront identifying
  the logic, establishing a foundation of relevance; (2) work through increasing layers of
  detail, continuously moving to the next most relevant piece; (3) over time, difficulty reduces
  as the knowledge network becomes more robust (figure: "How people should learn in layers" —
  relevance stays high, difficulty fluctuates within manageable levels, level of detail starts
  low and increases layer-by-layer).

## Backbone-crowding failure mode

(Source: Live Clinic 27, an Ascent 2 student submission titled "LOGIC BACKBONE DISAPPEARS,"
Justin's video response not captured.)

A student reported a recurring failure where the logic layer becomes visually illegible as soon
as the concepts layer is added on top of it: "the logic layer completely disappears when I add
the concepts that dictate the logic ... a logic is made by multiple concepts, when I add the
concepts e.g in relation A -> B, the subconcepts are f,g,h,i,j. When I add them, the clear shape
of the logic is gone. This is repeating. When I keep the thick arrow from A to B, it just crowds
the mindmap further leading to Spiderwebbing. Arrows cross over one another making it messy."
The problem reportedly worsens with scale ("the more topics I add, the more the backbone
disappears"), and the student notes that the logic layer, viewed in isolation, "is clear, crisp
and memorable" — the failure is specifically the concepts layer visually destroying the logic
layer's legibility once superimposed, not a defect in the logic layer itself. This is distinct
from, but compounds into, spiderwebbing. No resolution is captured in text (the clinic answer
exists only in DRM-locked video). GRINDE's Reflective and Emphasised letters (layer separation
by size/colour/font; see 03-details/techniques/grinde-checklist.md) are the general-purpose
prescription for keeping layers visually distinct, but this specific failure — legibility
collapsing as scale increases even when layer separation is attempted — is not resolved against
those letters anywhere in the captured corpus. See 03-details/examples/layering-examples.md for
the full example.
