## Definition

Expertise means being able to answer any question and solve any problem within a domain. What
distinguishes an expert from a novice is not the quantity of facts held but the **knowledge
structure** — how information is organised in the brain. Higher-order learning is the process
that builds expert-like structure; lower-order learning does not.

- **Higher-order knowledge** is integrated in a network. Formed by two operations: *analysis*
  (comparing information to find similarities and differences) and *evaluation* (making
  judgments on the relative importance of those similarities and differences).
- **Lower-order knowledge** is isolated. It is the outcome of repetition, memorisation, and
  understanding pursued without comparison to anything else.
- Higher-order learning is beneficial for retention and depth of understanding. Lower-order
  learning is forgotten quickly and easily.

## Process vs. outcome, and why order is asymmetric

Orders of learning are both processes (what the learner does) and outcomes (what can later be
retrieved). Higher-order learning *processes* produce higher-order learning *outcomes* — but not
the reverse.

- If someone compares ideas to group information (analyse) and then judges the relative
  importance of each group to map them (evaluate), they create higher-order knowledge. With
  retrieval practice to refine and consolidate it, they can retrieve at higher orders because the
  information is already organised correctly.
- If someone uses flashcards to memorise lists of facts (a lower-order process), they can
  retrieve their knowledge only at that lower order, and only with sufficient repetition.

Three consequences follow:

1. **Higher-order learning methods produce both lower-order and higher-order knowledge.** It is
   impossible to compare ideas without first understanding them. Lower-order methods produce
   only lower-order knowledge.
2. **Starting with a higher-order method is more efficient than starting with a lower-order
   method.** Higher-order methods build networks of knowledge first, which makes information
   more memorable to begin with and reduces how much must subsequently be memorised by
   lower-order means (the **lower-order burden**).
3. **Multiple orders of learning are always required.** Even though higher-order learning
   creates more robust knowledge, forgetting is a brain default (see cognitive-load-management
   and the memory-dynamics basis). Retrieval practice is needed regardless of which order was
   used to encode, to ensure performance at every order that will later be required.

## The analyse/evaluate distinction

Forming a chunk is not the same act as noticing a similarity. Identifying that two items share a
property is *analysis*. Deciding that the shared property is important enough, at a big-picture
level, to justify grouping the items together is *evaluation* — a distinct, higher cognitive
act. This extra step is what separates real chunking from mere similarity-finding: chunks formed
by evaluation are formed through value judgments, not just because commonalities exist. A chunk
built on analysis alone (grouped because items are merely similar) is a weaker structure than one
built through evaluation (grouped because the similarity was judged to matter).

## Critique and replacement of linear Bloom's

Bloom's revised taxonomy is commonly read as a hierarchy: memorising and understanding sit at the
bottom ("lower-order"), with analysis, evaluation, and creation above them, implying a
bottom-to-top mastery sequence. This linear reading is criticised by researchers and is treated
in this corpus as inaccurate.

The corpus's alternative framing: memory and understanding are **outcomes** of engaging in
higher-order thinking, not foundational base tiers that must be secured before higher-order work
can begin. The brain adapts to solve problems and learns along the way; good memory and depth of
understanding are symptoms of the brain building good knowledge structures, not something
achieved by directly intending to remember or understand. Authentic learning is not a linear
bottom-to-top climb; it involves constant back-and-forth movement between levels within one
encoding pass.

Dr Justin Sung's **practical encoding cycle** (2023) is presented as the named replacement model
for the linear reading: rather than a base-to-summit hierarchy, memory and understanding result
from a cycle of higher-order thinking operations. This is stated to be an oversimplification of
a genuinely complex process, but a more accurate depiction of learning and expertise-building
than the linear hierarchy reading. Full operational treatment of the cycle (how to run it as a
technique) is deferred elsewhere in the corpus and is not captured in the files reviewed for this
entry.

## What higher-order learning feels like

Because learning cannot be observed directly, monitoring the *feel* of the process is used as
diagnostic data (see cognitive-load-management and the self-correction-loop basis):

| | Higher-order learning | Lower-order learning |
|---|---|---|
| Involves | Comparing, contrasting, evaluating, and forming networks and relationships. | Rote-memorising, repeating, and trying to understand information in isolation (without comparing and creating networks). |
| Feels | Challenging, mentally effortful, and potentially confusing. Learners often go back and forth between their material and spend lots of time thinking deeply and asking questions. | Tedious, time-consuming, mentally straightforward, and potentially boring or sleep-inducing. |

(A fuller What/How/Examples table for higher- vs. lower-order learning, with the example
self-questioning lists, is reproduced in full in
`03-details/tables/higher-lower-order-table.md`.)

## Related attribute: the processing-levels ladder

Deep processing unlocks higher-order learning. A 7-level processing ladder (memorise → try to
understand → rate importance → group by similarity → find alternative groupings → judge the best
grouping → connect groups) exists as a calibration device for identifying and advancing one's
habitual processing level; its full form and calibration procedure are treated as an attribute of
skill development rather than of the higher-order-learning construct itself, and are recorded in
`02-concepts/deep-processing-development.md` and `03-details/tables/processing-levels-ladder.md`.
