## Definition

SIR (Spaced, Interleaved Retrieval) is one of the core foundational techniques of a learning
system and is presented as persisting through the entire program: even as later stages add more
techniques, SIR remains in place underneath them.

SIR is **not a single technique — it is a method**. The difficulty of practising SIR is entirely
determined by the difficulty of whichever interleaved retrieval techniques are chosen to compose
it (see interleaving table). Retrieval techniques are generally easier to master than encoding
techniques: each can typically be expected to reach mastery with roughly 3 to 5 hours of
experience, and retrieval technique competence forms a foundation to work on encoding skill over
time. At least one week of practice is recommended before moving on to the next stage of learning
SIR; less than a week is explicitly not advised.

As encoding skill increases, the retrieval requirement never reaches zero, because the brain is
never leak-proof — some level of retrieval is always needed (see spacing; retrieval-practice).

## Snowballing: schedule integration with prestudy

Snowballing is the practice of learning a topic superficially but broadly during prestudy, then
progressively adding more depth on each subsequent pass over the same material. It extends
prestudy into the SIR schedule rather than treating prestudy as a one-off event: prestudy the
week's material broadly (concepts and their relationships only, no memorisation), then deepen it
across the week's remaining sessions (build detail, create flashcards, elaborate the mind map),
then consolidate at the end-of-week and end-of-month revision passes.

A basic scheduling template built around this idea (Sunday prestudy → daily post-class review →
end-of-week interleaved retrieval, detail memorisation starting here → ongoing flashcards in spare
pockets of time → end-of-month revision, made easy by the accumulated flashcard work) is offered as
a starting point, explicitly flagged as simplified relative to a more advanced schedule taught
later for prestudying an entire topic (4–6 weeks' worth) in one sitting. Struggling to follow a
schedule traces to one of two causes: basic time-management skill gaps, or task-prioritisation
errors (such as prioritising retrieval sessions over good-quality prestudy) — in either case, the
correct response is to revisit the relevant self-management material rather than push forward.

## Prestudy-quality dependency (LC46)

Live Clinic 46 ("Why SIR may feel overwhelming, and how effective prestudy helps") diagnoses a
common presenting complaint — SIR volume feels unmanageable, or "not enough SIR variety" — as
frequently rooted upstream, in prestudy quality and scope, rather than downstream in the retrieval
stage itself. Documented claims: a good SIR strategy must combine multiple techniques to cover
multiple levels of knowledge mastery; consistency of practice alone is not sufficient to cover all
gaps; the low-hanging-fruit fix for SIR overload is typically to work further ahead of class and
prestudy a larger scope of material, not to add more retrieval technique variety; prestudy scope
decisions should be reasoned about explicitly in terms of cognitive load. The session frames this
as a diagnostic redirect — treating a retrieval-stage symptom by fixing an encoding-stage root
cause. The mindmap brain dump technique is named as having "the biggest weakness" among
interleaving options in this context, and a general SIR practice strategy plus a full SIR spacing
timeline are named as session deliverables; the substance of all three is
*[video-only: not captured]* (ics-course/04-supplementary/01-live-clinics/30-lc46.md).

## Time-constrained variants (LC38)

Live Clinic 38 ("SIR yes SIR — maximising limited study time") addresses how to run SIR effectively
under tight schedules, relevant to early-stage learners and to more advanced learners who struggle
to locate their own weaknesses. Documented topic structure: a breakdown of SIR itself; using
Bloom's Taxonomy to differentiate lower-order from higher-order learning; using SIR to overcome
weaknesses in encoding specifically; strategies and timing of interleaving sessions; the claim that
higher-order learning makes lower-order learning easier; subject-specific recommendations for
higher-order techniques as an initial interleaving choice, including for procedural-knowledge-heavy
subjects; teaching and mindmap brain dumps as interleaving techniques; a group-study technique of
exchanging created practice exams and answer sheets (compare advanced group method); and a
discussion of balancing pushing through revision struggle against recognising a re-encoding need. A
named "3 essential parts to maximising" SIR under time pressure, and a discussion of memory-palace
scaling limits, are both flagged in-session but their content is
*[video-only: not captured]* (ics-course/04-supplementary/01-live-clinics/38-lc38.md).
