## Mechanism: why the program teaches simplified versions first

Techniques are themselves skills, not information (L08): they pass through competence stages toward
automaticity, consume cognitive load until automatized, require far more practice than theory, and can only
be acquired a few at a time. Two constraints from the basis jointly explain the entire scaffold-then-replace
architecture used throughout the corpus:

- **The parallel-acquisition cap (L08).** At most 2-3 techniques or processes can be trained at once;
  complexity compounds exponentially, not additively, when more are introduced together. The pacing guide
  states this as a hard rule: "Max 3 techniques learned at a time (at most) — even if each is individually
  simple, due to multiplicative system complexity," illustrated with a spinning-top analogy (a single top is
  easy to predict; two interacting tops become unpredictable in clash timing; three compound further). A
  learner must not progress if more than 3 new techniques are not yet at moderate conscious competence.
- **The cognitive load budget (L05).** A technique itself consumes working-memory budget until it is
  automatized; teaching a fully-loaded final technique before its prerequisites are automatic would push
  total load out of the productive band. Scaffolds are deliberately simpler, lower-load versions of the same
  underlying mechanism, introduced so early practice fits inside the load budget while the skill is still
  effortful, then swapped for the full version once the earlier skill frees load by becoming automatic
  (automatization frees load — L08/L05 interaction).

Practice must dominate theory: the recommended ratio is at least 5 hours of application per 1 hour of
instruction, up to 40:1 for complex skills — the commonly perceived 75%/25% theory/practice split is inverted
in reality (see learning-system-architecture for the exact breakdown: ~15% basic theory clarity + 20%
practice-to-find-errors + 10% deepening + 55% further practice, cycling between practice and understanding).

## Stage gates

Progression between stages and between technique sets is gated by explicit competence thresholds, not by
elapsed time or content coverage:

- **≥80% consistency/accuracy** on the current technique set (≈ "moderate conscious competence") before
  advancing to new techniques; the Fundamentals checkpoint that closes the High-Yield phase uses the same
  ~80% competence threshold across taught skills, explicitly warning that lower competence on even one
  checklist item can compound problems in later stages that build on that foundation.
- **Readiness signals**: comfortable with current techniques, and having practiced them under varying
  challenges/subjects, versus **must-not-progress signals**: techniques not yet practiced, or more than 3 new
  techniques not yet at moderate conscious competence.
- **Practice-time guidance** (once experimentation/reflection/frequency criteria are met): 5-10 hours per
  technique in the High-Yield phase; 20-30 hours per technique in the Growth phase. A typical weekly rhythm:
  2-3 hours of theory over the weekend covering 2-3 new techniques, 15-30 hours of practice during the
  following week, reflection daily where possible (minimum 3x/week, capped at 30-45 minutes/session), and
  feedback sought at least weekly. Under this schedule, moderate conscious competence for most techniques is
  expected within approximately 2 weeks.
- **Decline as a stop signal**: if technique level declines — especially as the system grows more advanced —
  the correct response is to pause 2-4 weeks to consolidate before adding new techniques, not to push forward.
- **Stage-end checklists** (competence-assessment tables and, at some stages, quizzes) gate advancement into
  the next stage throughout Camp I, Camp II, Ascent III, and Base Camp; several of these gates are explicitly
  "soft" (advance as soon as the learner feels ready) rather than hard-blocking.
- Progressing with an incomplete task list is also a stop condition: do not advance if more than 3
  actionable/practicable items remain outstanding.

The rate of technique-mastery progress is stated as a function of rate of experimentation × rate of
reflection; slow progress is characterised as rarely a technique-difficulty problem and almost always an
experimentation/reflection-frequency problem. More than 4-5 hours/week of theory has a *detrimental* effect on
completion time (overload), while variation beyond that range has little further effect — theory time has an
optimal band, not a monotonic "more is better" relationship (cognitive-load-budget, L05).

## The replacement/scaffold ladder

Each row names the earlier (scaffold) technique, the later (canonical/replacement) technique, and where the
canonical version is fully specified. "Replaces" here follows the corpus's own explicit replacement-table
language (see Base Camp table below); several rows are variants or supplements rather than full replacements,
noted individually.

| Scaffold / earlier form | Replaced by | Stage of replacement | Full spec |
|---|---|---|---|
| TLS red light (question generation) | Aim | Ascent I | inquiry-based-learning; ../03-details/techniques/tls-procedure.md, ../03-details/techniques/aim-procedure.md |
| TLS green light (curiosity satiation) | Shoot | Ascent I | bear-hunter-system |
| Prestudy basics (question step) | Aim | Ascent I | prestudy |
| Notes-and-processing / collecting-processing | Shoot / non-linear ("mindmap") note-taking | Ascent I | non-linear-note-taking |
| Aim + Shoot (BHS core) | Hipshot (speed variant, not a full replacement) | Base Camp | see below |
| Maintaining focus (basics) | Focus training + OFF-Rest timing | Base Camp | attention-focus |
| BEDS-M | Focus training + PEER-Peer system | Base Camp | habit-formation |
| Scheduling basics (prioritising guidelines) | Priority 0+1 system + advanced time management | Base Camp / Camp II | time-priority-management |
| Revision basics | Revision strategy + WPW | Camp II | revision-strategy; ../03-details/techniques/wpw.md |
| Test-Target-Teach (interim/shortcut) | Advanced group study method (interleaving table) | Fundamentals 2 → later stages | interleaving |
| Pomodoro | OFF-Rest timing | Base Camp | attention-focus |

The Base Camp welcome page states the replacement table explicitly and in this exact form: "Aim + Shoot →
Hipshot; Maintaining focus → Focus training + OFF-Rest timing; BEDS-M → Focus training + PEER-Peer system;
Scheduling basics (prioritising guidelines) → Priority 0+1 system," while listing Bear Hunter System,
Scheduling Basics, and Spaced Interleaved Retrieval as techniques that *continue* rather than being replaced
(their mastery is assumed to already be very high by this stage). Test-Target-Teach is introduced explicitly
as an interim/shortcut technique, used so that progress does not halt while the full interleaving
table is being learned, substitutable once the learner is confident with the fuller advanced group study
method — the two substantially overlap in function.

### Hipshot: variant, not a strict replacement

Hipshot is a faster mode of Aim-Shoot cycling (rapid alternation rather than separate passes) rather than a
different technique — it exploits the high cognitive load tolerance trained and the ability to form
relational networks rapidly. It is explicitly gated on **near-unconscious competence with traditional
Aim-Shoot cycling**: Hipshot is only possible with close to unconscious competence with the traditional
Aim-Shoot cycle; if Hipshot is attempted and struggles occur, reverting to the traditional BHS is the
recommended response. It commonly emerges as a natural byproduct of BHS mastery rather than being
deliberately learned. Three implementation
tiers trade time-efficiency against difficulty: Standard (efficiency 5/10, difficulty Low), Accelerated
(7/10, Moderate), and Ultra-accelerated (10/10, High, targeting ~90% coverage to ~90% retention rather than
100%). Full procedure: technique-progression's sibling concept bear-hunter-system.

## Prerequisite chains

Several later-stage techniques carry explicit prerequisite gates, stated directly rather than implied:

- **Multipass** requires the learner to "already have excellent order control, be an inquiry-based learner,
  and be able to write effective notes (using VPReFRE)" — three named prerequisites (order-control,
  inquiry-based-learning, non-linear-note-taking) before the system is introduced.
- **Hipshot** requires near-unconscious competence in traditional Aim-Shoot cycling (see above).
- **Breaching questions** is stated to be "an advanced method because it requires a high level of cognitive
  load tolerance and the ability to do a significant level of non-linear mapping mentally."
- **WPW** requires a minimum wait of **at least 3 days after finishing the GRINDE map** before attempting it
  (a wait of at least 3 days after finishing the GRINDE map is one of nine execution tips). WPW is also
  gated more generally on the rest of the learning system being optimised: it is described elsewhere as
  effective but time-consuming, advisable only once the rest of the learning system is optimised.

## "Intensive encoding segment ends at Ascent III" marker

The Ascent III final checkpoint states explicitly: **"Ascent 3 marks the end of the intensive encoding
strategies segment of the Growth phase."** From this point forward, later stages develop time-efficiency and
more complex combinations of the encoding skills already taught, rather than new encoding quality: issues
with encoding *quality*, *effectiveness*, or *consistency* are unlikely to be improved by the lessons in
future stages; if competence is doubtful at this point, the greatest improvement will come from
practice and feedback rather than later theory. This is a structural marker for the whole progression graph:
everything downstream of Ascent III (Camp I, Camp II, Base Camp, Summit) assumes encoding competence is
already in place and builds speed, self-management sophistication, and system integration on top of it.

The Ascent III checkpoint also specifies six expected findings that function as a rapid competence check
before advancing past this marker: significant retention improvement after the first encoding session;
significant retrieval improvement across diverse challenges/contexts; reduced total study time from less
relearning; significant confidence improvement; significant growth-mindset/error-learning improvement; and
clear appreciation of higher-order-thinking demands with high tolerance for cognitive load and discomfort. If
these are absent, feedback on practice is strongly recommended before proceeding.

## When-to-not-advance rules

**Exam-imminent: do not destabilise.** When an assessment is close, the rule inverts the normal
progression logic: gain mastery over *current* techniques and fill immediate gaps without destabilising the
existing system, rather than learning new ones. "The worst thing we can do is learn many new techniques that
take training to master, learn them incorrectly, and do even worse than if we had just slammed through a
million flashcards instead." Concretely:

- **New members near an exam** should focus on High-Yield techniques only (masterable in under two weeks,
  large immediate return) and skip the Growth phase; learning Growth-phase techniques before mastering
  High-Yield fundamentals typically produces *worse* performance than simply consolidating fundamentals,
  because almost all Growth-phase techniques require weeks to gain competence.
- **Existing members near an exam** should stop progressing through new content and consolidate: complete a
  skills audit against the milestone checklists at the end of High-Yield, Technique Training, and Ascent III,
  then use Kolb's cycle (kolbs-cycle) to target and optimise weaknesses in the current system.
- The only stated exception: if foundational techniques are entirely lacking, learning them still yields high
  benefit even short-term, since their mastery time is comparatively low.
- Stated key principle: the benefit of learning new techniques is usually not worth the instability it
  introduces to the learning system and the time it takes away from using effective foundational techniques,
  when an assessment is imminent.

This rule generalises the stage-gate logic above (do not add load faster than it can be automatized) to the
time-pressured case, where the cost of destabilisation is highest and the recovery window is shortest.

## Retrieval-before-encoding sequencing (pre-Fundamentals-2)

The program deliberately teaches interleaved retrieval before any encoding technique, and directs learners
to encode however they already know until Fundamentals 2. Stated rationale:

1. Correct encoding is harder to learn and to self-assess than interleaved retrieval — days of retrieval
   practice yield visible improvements in memory and depth, while days of encoding practice can leave a
   learner unable to judge whether they improved.
2. Retrieval-first creates a stronger base system with regular spacing already in place.
3. Many interleaving strategies force higher-order learning even without deliberate encoding technique,
   building familiarity that makes later encoding techniques easier to acquire.
4. Working on encoding and interleaved retrieval simultaneously risks technique overload — failing to gain
   competence in either (the parallel-acquisition cap applied to the program's own first stages).

Learners who still feel encoding-limited despite practising interleaved retrieval are directed to the 30-day
self-regulation or deep-processing challenges as a foundation-builder (content of those challenges: locked,
see 99-provenance/not-captured-register.md).

## Late-stage positioning (Summit)

Summit introduces no new encoding mechanisms — its techniques (cramming/multipass, exam execution) are
"advanced variations of the BHS" optimised for high-pace sprints and peak exam performance; all prior-stage
techniques continue unchanged. The stage carries an explicit warning that intensive encoding-skill focus
commonly causes fundamentals neglect (e.g. SIR quality) and recommends a rapid skill assessment or full
skills audit (skills-audit-leep) at this point — an instance of the technique-decline watch.
