## Statement

Durable behaviour change is built and dismantled at the cue and reward ends of a habit loop, not by willpower applied directly to the response. Systems that make the desired behaviour the path of least resistance (willpower-enhanced) persist; systems that depend on ongoing motivation to execute (willpower-dependent) fail under pressure. This holds for study behaviours, focus, and procrastination alike, and is the basis for the program's canonical habit system, PEER-Peer.

## Anatomy of a habit

A habit is a cue → response → reward loop. Behaviour change operates on the cue and reward ends, not the response directly:

1. **Identify the cues** that trigger the response (the habit, good or bad).
2. **Remove bad cues** that cause an undesired response — e.g., a social media icon on a home screen, or the visible presence of a phone. Removal (deleting the icon, browsing in incognito mode, physically relocating the phone) eliminates the need to resist the cue with willpower.
3. **Adopt and integrate good cues** that facilitate a desired response — e.g., books left open to the correct page the night before, running shoes hung on a doorknob.

**Conditioning**: an unrelated external stimulus can be paired with a behaviour to evoke it automatically (classical/Pavlovian conditioning applied to study behaviour). Pairing studying with an immediately-following enjoyable activity can eventually cause the brain to enjoy studying itself, even without the reward being present.

**Nudge theory**: environmental cues influence behaviour more than willpower does. Successful, consistently productive people rely on environment design (nudge theory); people who burn out or rely on motivation alone use willpower as their primary lever. If a first attempt at cue optimisation is unsuccessful, it is rare for repeated optimisation to remain ineffective.

## Willpower-enhanced vs. willpower-dependent

Motivation is not a single, controllable quantity — it fluctuates because of many variables (mood, sleep/energy, interest, sense of purpose, punishments/rewards, autonomy, identity/values alignment, competence, psychological reserve, physical wellbeing). A plan must succeed even on a low-motivation day, which is why habit/environment design is preferred over motivation-driven effort.

| | Willpower-dependent | Willpower-enhanced |
|---|---|---|
| Reliance mechanism | Relies on motivation and willpower to take action | Relies on habits and environment to make action frictionless |
| Productivity driver | Productivity depends on motivation | Productivity depends on habits |
| Consistency | Inconsistent | Highly consistent |
| Burnout outcome | Leads to burnout | Prevents burnout |
| Reserve for challenges | Low reserve when unexpected challenges arise | Able to draw on willpower reserve to push through increased challenge |

Naming procrastination generically as a single behaviour prevents effective troubleshooting: each trigger-response pair needs its own diagnosis and remedy (see `procrastination.md` for the six-mechanism taxonomy that follows from this).

## PEER-Peer system (canonical)

PEER-Peer is the core-pillar habit-creation system, built directly on the cue-response-reward anatomy above. It is a low-learning-curve, quick-to-set-up system with components named **P**rep, **E**asy, **E**xit, **R**eward, **Peer**:

- **Prep**: cue optimisation (remove bad cues, adopt good cues), iterated until effective.
- **Easy**: set a minimum viable goal (MVG) — the smallest possible action step, with motivation cost as close to zero as possible.
- **Exit**: explicit permission to quit at each MVG. Telling oneself that stopping is allowed at any point keeps the brain focused on the achievable MVG rather than being scared off by the size of the final goal.
- **Reward**: a reward tied to goal attainment, requiring minimal work to grant and delayed as long as possible — the closer to the final goal before rewarding, the better. Relying on willpower alone to avoid a premature reward is failure-prone; physically locking the reward away (with others' help if needed) is more reliable.
- **Peer**: external accountability (family, friends, community), the same mechanism as the burnt ships strategy (below). This step becomes unnecessary once the behaviour is fully automatic — PEER-Peer usage decreases as behaviours automatize, and the accountability step is the first to become redundant.

Full step-by-step specification and per-step criteria: `03-details/techniques/peer-peer.md`.

## Burnt ships strategy

Setting a high, genuinely feared consequence of failure to remove the option of retreat (named for generals who burned their own transport ships after landing). Applied to study/focus contexts: setting high stakes for failing to act (e.g., an accountability pair with a public, embarrassing consequence for non-completion).

- **Where it fits**: a useful adjunct when other methods alone are insufficient, or for behaviours that are a high, urgent priority to change. It is more suited to short-term change — it operates at the "consequence" level of behaviour change, which is generally either unsustainable or inadequate alone in the long term.
- **Requirements**: the consequence must be high enough to be genuinely feared, and it must actually be enforced (an accountability buddy is recommended).
- Full component detail, alongside the rest of BEDS-M: `03-details/techniques/beds-m.md`.

## V-ABC model (stub)

Vulnerability-Antecedent-Behaviour-Consequence: behaviours are triggered by antecedents and reinforced or punished by consequences; setting-up events and vulnerability factors change how a given antecedent affects behaviour. Changing antecedents and vulnerability factors is much more likely to produce behaviour change than willing the behaviour to change directly; consequences can facilitate habit change but are generally less powerful than pre-behaviour (antecedent-level) interventions. Effort and willpower should be directed at the V or A components, not the C.

Effectiveness of a given behaviour-change strategy should be treated as a spectrum rather than binary — changing a single antecedent may be insufficient; a specific behaviour may require changing several (the source material's example: five) antecedents/vulnerability factors together.

**Status note**: this model is captured in the source corpus only as a compressed principles list (key-message callout box, no worked procedure, no examples, no sub-page content despite being listed as a full lesson). No procedure for *identifying* which antecedents or vulnerability factors to change, or how, is present in the available material. It is retained here as a named framework whose principles are established but whose operational method is not captured (stub).

## Chain analysis

A 6-step clinical-psychology technique for diagnosing and fixing entrenched behaviour patterns (procrastination, over-eating, social-media over-use, binge-watching, emotional reactivity) by tracing the causal chain from prompting event to target behaviour and identifying solutions at each link. ## Limiting habits and scripted actions

- **Limiting habits**: automatic, usually-unconscious response patterns that sabotage progress, typically triggered by stress, tiredness, time pressure, bad mood, or discomfort. Because they are automatic and outside awareness, self-report is unreliable; identification can take days to weeks. When unsure what one's limiting habits are, the first step is simply to begin observing habitual response tendencies under those trigger conditions.
- **Scripted actions**: pre-planned responses designed to require as close to zero willpower as possible, executed automatically when a limiting-habit trigger occurs (the response is decided in advance, not improvised in the moment).

## Habit timeline expectations

Habit formation is not immediate. Forming a new habit takes time because: (1) the habits of practice and self-reflection that make later habit-formation faster and more intentional must themselves be built first; (2) old habits must be unlearned; (3) the barriers preventing (1) and (2) usually have to be removed first. Once a new habit is formed, it no longer requires daily motivation to sustain — but the upfront investment period (during which demotivation is a real risk if progress isn't visible) is measured in **weeks to months**, not days. Marginal-gains tracking is the mechanism recommended to sustain motivation through this formation window until the habit becomes self-sustaining.
