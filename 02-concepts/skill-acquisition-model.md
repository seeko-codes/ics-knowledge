## Learning techniques are skills

Techniques taught in the program are treated as skills to be acquired, not information to be
understood. A technique becomes a **learning asset** — something that continuously benefits
future learning by making it faster, easier, and more effective — only once four requirements are
met:
1. **Understand** how it works and why it is effective.
2. **Remember** it well enough to use when needed.
3. **Apply it correctly** in real situations.
4. **Perform it consistently and easily**, even under pressure.

## Two laws of growth

**First law — lock in learning assets.** Focus deeply on one or two techniques at a time; refine
and reinforce until habitual; only advance once a technique is locked in as reliable. Techniques
collected but never mastered leave the learning system scattered and unreliable.

**Second law — learn from the right teacher.** Mastery requires three kinds of knowledge:
declarative (facts/theory — the *what*), procedural (skill execution — the *how*), and
conditional (context-sensitivity — *when/why*). Guidelines: master theory as best possible
without trying to forecast every mistake in advance; move from theory to practice once a
*working* understanding exists (not full mastery); use mistakes as the diagnostic tool — identify
them, analyse them, refine the approach, iterate. "Perfection is when there are no more mistakes
to make" — mistakes are the terminal diagnostic signal, not something to be avoided pre-emptively.
Fail fast, fail safe: make small mistakes early to avoid larger ones later.

## Competence hierarchy

**4-stage form:** unconscious incompetence → conscious incompetence → conscious competence →
unconscious competence.

**7-stage elaborated ladder**, with diagnostic signs per stage:

| Stage | Signs |
|---|---|
| Unconscious incompetence | Not even aware of where the mistakes are; technique untried or unknown; mistakes not yet discovered |
| Low-to-moderate conscious incompetence | Some mistakes discovered while practising, not yet overcome |
| High conscious incompetence | Several mistakes discovered across multiple attempts, not yet overcome, but adjustments and experiments are much more targeted and marginal |
| Low conscious competence | Technique can be done correctly; consistency and accuracy poor; new mistakes still occasionally surface under different contexts |
| Moderate conscious competence | Technique done correctly with reasonable consistency and accuracy; starting to feel slightly more comfortable |
| High conscious competence | Technique done correctly with high consistency and accuracy, at reasonably low effort; very comfortable |
| Unconscious competence | Technique performed correctly as a new habit; effort remains only for the thinking/processing that is part of the technique itself, none extra to apply it correctly |

Consistency improves before time/effort reduction during conscious competence — this ordering of
sub-skill emergence is itself a diagnostic marker.

## Practice dominates theory

**1:5 baseline ratio:** for every hour spent learning a new technique, at least five hours should
be spent applying it. **Practice-to-theory ceiling, restated as a cap:** never exceed a 5:1 ratio
of practice to theory (e.g. 25 study hours/week → at most 5 theory hours/week; even this may be
excessive). Learners have achieved strong progress with ratios as high as **40:1** for complex
skills. More than 4–5 theory hours/week has a detrimental effect on completion time due to
overload; changes to theory time within the recommended range have minimal effect on completion
or mastery time.

Complexity of learning increases **exponentially**, not linearly, when multiple techniques are
introduced at once — each technique influences the others, making individual mistakes harder to
isolate and refine.

Practice time is not *additional* time: it replaces existing study methods. Treating new
technique practice as extra work (on top of old methods) rather than a replacement for them
causes technique abandonment, because trying to run multiple conflicting habit systems in
parallel fails.

**Real vs. perceived theory-practice split.** Perceived: 75% theory/understanding, 25% practice
(for refinement/fluency). Actual: ~15% basic theory clarity, ~20% practice/experience to surface
the biggest misconceptions and errors, ~10% deepening understanding and correcting
misconceptions, ~55% further practice and challenging experiences — with a **theory-practice
cycle** running between "more practice/challenge" and "deeper understanding," described as the
true engine of improvement. Most theory only becomes truly comprehensible once practice-
discovered errors give it relevance.

## Parallel acquisition cap: 2–3 at once

At most 2–3 techniques/processes should be learned simultaneously (do not advance if more than 3
new techniques remain below moderate conscious competence). Rationale: skill acquisition
resembles a computer solving a complex algorithm — each skill has variables that must be
deciphered before correct use, costing time, effort, and cognitive load. Simultaneous multi-skill
attempts increase confusion and frustration and reduce net acquisition speed via two mechanisms:
(1) limited time/effort/energy spread across too many experiences, and (2) brain overload from
tracking too many variables at once. Even when a learner genuinely needs many skills for a goal,
the fastest path is still to reduce simultaneous skill count and develop each one as quickly as
possible — counter-intuitive, but it improves concentration and depth.

**Spinning-top analogy:** a single spinning top is easy to predict (how it spins, slows, falls).
Adding a second top next to it makes clash timing and outcome nearly impossible to predict; a
third compounds this further. Each technique may be simple in isolation, but integrating several
into one system multiplies complexity because more variables interact. A system that becomes too
entangled makes root-cause identification for any single problem very difficult, sometimes
requiring extended specialist-coach attention.

Skill-acquisition cycle underlying this constraint (4 repeating steps): identify barriers,
analyse root causes creating those barriers, propose solutions, repeat until no barriers remain.

## Progression gates

Do not advance if more than 3 actionable/practicable items remain in the task list, or if the
current technique set is not executed to at least **80% consistency and accuracy** (roughly
equivalent to moderate conscious competence). Readiness to advance requires: comfort with current
techniques, and practice of them under varying challenges and subjects.

**3-tier skill-readiness rubric**, used to gate stage transitions:

| Level | Description | Level of practice | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Confidently unknown | Confidence may be high, but this confuses "understanding" the theory with actual competence | Little to no practice in different contexts | Practice needed for accurate data on real skill level; do not proceed until further practice |
| Developing skill | Low accuracy or consistency across situations | Some practice has revealed weaknesses/gaps | Additional practice recommended to consolidate; learning new skills now risks overload |
| Stabilising skill | Comfortable and consistent; relatively little effort required | Significant practice across different challenges and situations | Ready to learn new skills |

A capstone example applies this rubric to four gating skills before progressing past an early
stage: evaluating tasks by importance vs. urgency, scheduling important non-urgent tasks,
calendar-based manageable scheduling, and consistent focus/procrastination management.

**Practice-hour guidance** (when experimentation/reflection frequency criteria are met):
5–10 hours of practice per technique in the High-Yield phase; 20–30 hours per technique in the
Growth phase. Reflection: daily if possible, minimum 3x/week, capped at 30–45 minutes per
session (quality is bounded by the prior stage's quality — spending more than 45 minutes on a
weak reflection stage is unlikely to add benefit; with improved self-awareness this stage
compresses toward ~10 minutes). Feedback should be sought at least weekly.

| Weekly scenario (2–3 hrs theory/week) | High-Yield completion | Growth-phase completion |
|---|---|---|
| 30 hrs practice/week | 4–6 weeks | 4–7 months |
| 15 hrs practice/week | 6–12 weeks | 6–10 months |
| 5 hrs practice/week | 8 weeks – 4 months | 12–20 months |

If technique level is observed declining, especially as the system grows more advanced, 2–4 weeks
of consolidation is recommended before adding new techniques.

## Rate of progress

Rate of skill-development progress is a direct function of **rate of experimentation × rate of
reflection**. The first diagnostic question when progress feels slow is whether experimentation
and reflection frequency are adequate — it is rare to progress slowly with adequate frequency of
both.

## Technique decline

Skills decline without use ("technique decline"), including silently in skilled practitioners who
stop monitoring themselves (complacency erosion) — a skilled, high-self-regulation learner can
grow complacent, assume current effectiveness persists, and let technique quality erode silently
over months without consistent reflective practice. This is a primary trigger for a skills audit;
full audit procedure at ../03-details/techniques/skills-audit-leep.md.

## Unlearning

**Unlearning** is the ability to replace old habits, skills, ideas, and practices with more
effective alternatives; it requires detaching from ego and acknowledging that current practice
may be wrong. Retaining old bad habits alongside a new system jeopardises the new system's
effectiveness. Two named anti-patterns block proper habit retraining:
- **Tug-of-war practice** — reverting to old habits, or softening new methods into easier
  versions, when the new method becomes difficult or unfamiliar.
- **Batman-style practice** — practising the new method separately without replacing the old one
  ("old habits by day, new methods by night").

Four tips for effective unlearning: (1) observe whether the reaction to a new method contradicting
expectations is open or closed off; (2) spot hesitancies — reluctance toward a method because it
conflicts with an existing habit is a warning sign; (3) avoid tug-of-war practice; (4) avoid
Batman-style practice.

## Three traps

Traps are behaviours causing disproportionate harm — a single trap can nullify otherwise-correct
methods entirely.

- **Deathly spirals** — a technique that helps short-term but harms long-term. The deeper the
  fall, the less time remains to escape. Creates **learning debt**: the future self must do extra
  work to pay for present inefficiencies, and because the future self is usually even busier,
  overwhelm compounds over time. Canonical pattern: spending too much time on ineffective
  "content-covering" methods leaves too little time to build real skill, eventually forcing a
  fail-or-give-up choice.
- **Bandwagon fallacy** — using a technique because it is common; common does not mean good.
  Judging a method's effectiveness requires knowing both who succeeded *and* who failed with it —
  people who failed with a popular method are typically far less vocal than those who succeeded.
- **Misfitting techniques** — a technique used incorrectly or in the wrong context. The right
  technique makes short work of a problem; if a method requires substantial effort without
  results, it is a signal of misfit, not a signal to try harder. If method 1 needs less effort
  than method 2 for the same result, method 1 is both superior and scales better with added
  effort (method 2 plateaus).

**Flashcard-overreliance compound case** — a single named example combining all three traps at
once: (1) a system increases dependence on more flashcards; (2) this creates learning debt (future
repetition needed to cover material); (3) it struggles to build knowledge networks, producing poor
memory and superficial understanding; (4) this increases the need for still more repetition/gap-
filling; (5) less time remains for other methods (or even learning about them); (6) daily card
accumulation escalates the cost of escaping the spiral over time. Flashcards themselves are not
the problem (they are useful for spaced retrieval and micro-learning) — overreliance is.

## Dimensions vs. skills

**Skills** are the ability to execute techniques masterfully to achieve a desired effect.
**Dimensions** are attribute-like measures reflecting habits, processes, and tendencies (e.g.
deep processing, self-regulation, mindset, retrieval); dimensions are not fixed. Dimension level
bounds skill-execution quality — for example, non-linear note-taking as a skill requires
sufficient deep-processing and self-regulation dimensions, and mindset must be sufficiently high
just to be able to learn the skill in the first place. Dimensions improve indirectly, through
learning new skills/techniques and building new habits via lesson practice — not through direct,
dimension-targeted training. Dimensions improve at different rates: deep processing and mindset
require substantial cognitive retraining (slower); retrieval is more technique-focused (faster to
improve). Diagnostic reassessment of dimension scores is recommended every 2–3 months (earliest
allowed retake: 1 month).
