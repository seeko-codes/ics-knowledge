## Mechanism

Learning by generating problems and then answering them changes both what information is
consumed and how it is processed — this is inquiry-based learning (IBL). Self-created questions
are the steering interface of cognition: they direct attention toward what is relevant, prime
the brain to seek specific relationship types, and — most fundamentally — create the purpose
without which consumption does not encode ("studying only produces learning when the
information consumed has a purpose; problems create that purpose").

With a driving question in place, less cognitive effort is spent deciding what is relevant
(attention is pre-committed to the question), and the act of seeking an answer is itself
generative (see higher-order-learning). Conventional (non-inquiry) learning is unfocused: it
takes in too much information at once, is harder to process, is forgotten faster, and requires
costly relearning. Because the brain does not like the unstructured nature of conventional
consumption, it is also experienced as tedious and unenjoyable.

Failure to apply learned knowledge is attributed mainly to *not applying information immediately
after learning it*, rather than to a general deficit in the ability to apply knowledge.

### The curiosity cycle

Solving a problem generates more information, which in turn generates more questions. This is a
self-feeding loop: answering produces the material for further, deeper questions (curiosity
satiation followed by renewed curiosity), producing progressively deeper, more applicable
understanding and higher retention.

### Question form determines relationship type

The form of a question determines which relationship types get explored, and therefore what
schema gets built. Full ranking and rationale: ../03-details/techniques/question-quality-criteria.md.
In summary: "why is this important?" forces multi-concept evaluation against the big picture
(highest yield); "how does X relate to Y?" probes specific relations, typically 2-3 concepts
(medium yield); "what is X / how does X occur?" yields isolated process or fact learning, with
a risk of collapsing into pure memorisation (low yield).

## The retrospective-rationalisation validity failure

A question is not functioning as inquiry if the answer is already known before asking it.
Genuinely curious questions, asked from a real problem-solving mindset, generate more knowledge
than questions posed only to retroactively justify a conclusion already held. This is the
"already knew the answer" trap: it has the surface form of inquiry (a question is asked and
answered) but does none of inquiry's cognitive work, because no attention-direction or
relationship-search actually occurs — the answer is simply retrieved, not discovered.

Emotionally engaging, personally relevant, and genuinely curiosity-sparking questions bind more
strongly than neutral ones and are more likely to sustain a genuine problem-solving mindset
(source: ics-course/03-growth/03-ascent-i/03-aim-question-quality.md).

## Not every question needs a full answer

Complex or advanced questions do encoding work in the *attempt* even when unresolved (generation
effect); questions that cannot yet be answered can be parked and routed to a lower-order,
rote-memorisation method rather than blocking progress (see the TLS flashcard escape hatch,
../03-details/techniques/tls-procedure.md).

## TLS as the deliberate beginner scaffold

The Traffic Light System (TLS) is the introductory technique used to train inquiry-based
learning. It divides the process into a **red light** phase (generate keywords and questions,
directing focus) and a **green light** phase (satiate curiosity by answering those questions,
finding relationships, and writing them into notes). TLS is explicitly framed as the
"beginner's introduction to self-facilitated inquiry-based learning," extended later in the
Ascent stages. Full procedure, including the exact red-light question list and green-light
principles: ../03-details/techniques/tls-procedure.md.

TLS is later replaced in full: TLS red light is replaced by Aim, and TLS green light is replaced
by Shoot (together, Aim + Shoot form the Bear Hunter System). Aim is described as "a more
focused and strategic use of questions to create only those relationships that are likely
high-yield" relative to TLS red light. Aim's full specification, including its interaction with
prestudy and its objection-handling, is at ../03-details/techniques/aim-procedure.md.

## Question-quality standard

Correct inquiry must satisfy a fixed set of cognitive principles regardless of stage (TLS or
Aim): questions must help find relationships; answering must force evaluation of importance and
value judgments; the process must require mental effort; information must always be connected
to a big picture; information must form a network; and the network must be logical and
meaningful, not merely connected. Full criteria set, yield ranking, and named failure modes
(simple question and answer, lower-order inquiry, importance checklist, etc.):
../03-details/techniques/question-quality-criteria.md.

## Order independence

With correct inquiry, a learner can start from any keyword and still surface the truly most
important words and ideas — inquiry does not depend on where one begins. Groups must emerge
from critically examined inquiry, not from the original presentation order or from preconceived
(possibly suboptimal) organisational ideas already held about the information. This connects to
order-control (see ../02-concepts/order-control.md): resources are structured for exposition by
their expert authors, not for the learner's own knowledge network, and the learner must
reconstruct that network independently rather than memorising the taught order.
