## Reconstruction vs. recall

Reconstruction goes a step beyond recall. Recall retrieves information as previously stored;
reconstruction additionally requires applying that information to construct new, valid
relationships that were not simply retrieved verbatim. This forces critical evaluation of each
piece of information — checking whether the newly constructed relationships are accurate — while
comparing and examining multiple chunks against each other.

In Bloom's-taxonomy terms, reconstruction operates at the **Evaluate** and **Create** levels while
also, as a side effect, testing the lower orders that recall alone would test (compare
higher-order-learning: higher-order operations produce lower-order outcomes as a side effect, not
the reverse).

## Ratio guidance

Because reconstruction-based strategies take longer than pure recall, an effective revision
practice blends the two rather than using either exclusively. The correct ratio is individually
variable, not fixed: some students find roughly 10% recall / 90% reconstruction most efficient,
while others find a more even 50% recall / 50% reconstruction more manageable. There is no single
prescribed ratio — it is self-calibrated against what a given student can sustain.

Independent of that individual calibration, there is also a phase-based shift across a learning
arc: more reconstruction is efficient earlier in a learning phase, because it enables more rapid
and comprehensive gap-finding across a wide surface of the material. More recall becomes efficient
in later phases, where the priority shifts toward developing detailed, high-fidelity retrieval
performance and mastery rather than broad gap discovery.

## Reteaching mechanism

Whole-Part-Whole (WPW) is introduced explicitly as "a heavily reconstructive retrieval method" —
the canonical worked application of the reconstruction principle. Reteaching a topic from memory,
moving from a whole overview into its parts and back to the whole, forces the learner to
reconstruct the relationships between components rather than simply recite what was recorded, and
in doing so tests higher-order structure, inter-layer relationship strength, and detailed recall
accuracy simultaneously (see 03-details/techniques/wpw.md).

Reteaching more generally is one instance of high-volume retrieval practice (HVRP) — comprehensive
retrieval that covers a topic's relationships and its specific details together. Brain dumps are
the other named example of HVRP, but are noted to be less effective than reteaching at testing
relationships and conceptual understanding specifically (see revision-strategy).
