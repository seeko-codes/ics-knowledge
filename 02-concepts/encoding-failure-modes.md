## Statement

This entry collects every named chunk/mindmap fault in the corpus into one taxonomy. Each fault
is a way the schema-structure substrate (L02) fails to form correctly, either because importance
was not genuinely evaluated (L03) or because processing order/source order was not actively
controlled (L06). Faults are diagnosed from the map's own structure — branch counts, chain
shapes, and connection patterns carry information about retention risk independent of the
content on the map.

A meaningful share of this vocabulary is **not documented in the core course text at all**. A
student in Live Clinic 30 asks directly "why isn't the list of mistakes such as cracked glass
and waterfalling not made public?" — indicating this terminology and its diagnostic use is
understood, inside the program, to live primarily in Live Clinics rather than in the written
lessons. Each entry below states its source(s) and flags explicitly where a term is named only
as a quiz answer option with no accompanying explanation anywhere in the captured corpus.

## Spiderwebbing

**Mechanism (L02/L06 violation).** A mindmap symptom: excessive, chaotic, scattered arrows added
retroactively after chunks were already formed without order control or
importance/retrieval-matching. It is the visual result of trying to bolt lateral relationships
onto a structure whose chunks were never chosen to hold them.

**Diagnostic signal.** Many crossing/chaotic lines; relationships added after chunking rather
than before; a sudden jump in the number of relationships that feels impossible to prioritise.
Spiderwebbing **can be a normal, non-error intermediate stage** of mapping — it is not realistic
to anticipate every relationship while first chunking, and an early spiderwebbed draft is
expected to be refined in later iterations (the retinal-detachment worked example in
chunking-worked-examples.md is the corpus's canonical illustration of this progression).

**Remedy.** Convert a textbook hierarchy to a flat list first (removing hierarchy bias), then
identify relationships, then decide chunks from those relationships — reversing the faulty
order. Regularly check whether the chunk structure is the simplest/most intuitive available; if
arrows look chaotic, consider merging the connected items into the same chunk; avoid committing
to a fixed structure early; avoid chunking exactly like a textbook; evaluate whether each
arrow/line is actually necessary and what relationship it expresses. Re-chunking around a
high-connection node (see Encoding-failure interaction with the 2-4 Rule, below) is the specific
mechanical fix.

**Sources.** spiderwebbing.md (canonical); 86.md; 04-inquiry-clean-up.md (worked example);
09-ascent-i-check.md ("Out Comes The Rain" — confirms spiderwebbing can be a normal stage, and
that it is *not* caused by insufficient prestudy or missing visual anchors, contrary to
distractor claims in the same quiz); LC30 (student self-report: "Until this LC I did hierarchy
purposely to avoid spiderwebbing").

## Waterfalling

**Mechanism (L06 violation).** A "top-down" mapping pattern: everything funnels through one
central topic or chunk, with very little connectivity between ideas that sit on the same layer
of the hierarchy. It is what spiderwebbing gets bolted onto when lateral relationships are added
retroactively (spiderwebbing.md identifies waterfalling as "step 1," spiderwebbing as "step 2,"
of the same failure sequence).

**Diagnostic signal.** Connections spread only outward/downward from a hub; no lateral
relationships between same-level ideas; information becomes forgettable because the learner
must memorise every terminal point individually rather than deriving them from structure. Many
unrelated topics produce structurally near-identical waterfalled maps, since surface topic
structure (not importance) is driving the shape.

**Remedy.** Frequently reconsider how concepts — and how whole chunks — relate to each other,
even after they are already placed in chunks; do not stop at the first relationships found.

**Sources.** 09-ascent-i-check.md ("Be Like Water" — waterfalling occurs because inquiry was too
superficial to explore multiple relationships, producing weak order control and independent
chunking; explicitly *not* caused by inquiry being too detailed or covering too many concepts at
once); 06-aim-examples.md ("chunking for medicine," iteration 1: retinal detachment funnels
entirely through the topic name); spiderwebbing.md.

## Cracked glass (stick-figure appearance)

**Mechanism (L02 violation).** Chunks are connected to each other **through their sub-concepts**
rather than directly, chunk-to-chunk. It is not hierarchy per se, not the absence of a backbone,
and not a synonym for breaking the 2-4 Rule (all three are explicit wrong-answer distractors in
the corpus's own quiz) — it is specifically indirect, sub-concept-mediated linkage standing in
for a real backbone connection.

**Diagnostic signal.** Relationships between chunks are hard to remember because of their
complexity/indirection; the map may visually resemble a "stick figure" — a student in LC30 asks
directly whether "cracked glass" and "stick figurey" describe the same thing, self-diagnosing
their own statistics mindmap this way after two weeks of material ("I feel like my mindmap still
has a stick figurey vibe, is this what you call a cracked glass appearance?").

**Remedy.** Create clear backbones between major chunks *before* committing to detailed
information. Cracked glass can also be a symptom of segmental mapping (below); fixing the
segmental-mapping habit removes the cracked-glass symptom as a side effect.

**Sources.** 07-final-checkpoint-ascent-ii.md ("A Cosmic Trio" — full definition and cause);
06-final-checkpoint-ascent-iii.md ("Read Between The Chunks" — recaps the definition, links to
Islands); LC30 (self-diagnosis quote above).

## Single-node chains and terminal nodes

**Mechanism (L02 violation).** A node ("chunk" or concept) with exactly one inbound and one
outbound connection is a **single-node chain**. If the chain ends with no further outbound
connection at all, that endpoint is a **terminal node** (the leaf of the tree). Both indicate
low network connectivity at that point in the map.

**Diagnostic signal.** A concept that links from exactly one thing to exactly one other thing,
contributing no organisational value while still cluttering the map; retention risk concentrates
at these low-connectivity points.

**Remedy.** Concatenate (merge) the single-node chain into its neighbour, or remove/re-chunk it
entirely. Full mechanical procedure — including the memory-anchor visualisation technique used
at the point of concatenation — is in chunking-rules.md.

**Sources.** 05-ascent-iii/02-chunking.md (introduces both terms with the India/British
colonisation example); 06-final-checkpoint-ascent-iii.md ("Blooming Chunk Structures" — recaps
single-node chains as a distinct mistake alongside reverse chunking); LC30 (a student
self-diagnoses "unnatural chunk structures with single node chains" after repeatedly defaulting
to textbook-similar, comfortable chunk structures); LC29 (a student self-diagnoses "I've got
some single node chains" as the likely cause of poor sub-chunk retention).

## Lonely chunks

**Mechanism (L02/L06 violation).** A chunk that is created but contains no content — "a storage
box with nothing inside it" — or, relatedly, a map that is really several disconnected
mini-maps. Caused by prechunking based on framing bias that inquiry never corrects.

**Diagnostic signal.** An empty chunk sitting on the map, or visibly separate mini-map clusters.
Acceptable transiently as an early hypothesis, but detrimental if left uncorrected: it adds
elements and cognitive load with no retrieval benefit, since (by definition) a properly-formed
chunk is defined by its potential contents from the outset.

**Remedy.** Form chunks only from identified similarities, then group; if a chunk has nothing to
put in it, that is evidence the chunk itself should not exist.

**Sources.** 09-ascent-i-check.md ("Antisocial Chunks" — full definition and cause).

*Distinct from* Islands (below): lonely chunks are empty or disconnected-by-omission; Islands are
densely developed but only superficially stitched together. The corpus does not cross-reference
the two terms to each other despite the family resemblance.

## Islands

**Mechanism (L02/L06 violation).** A larger map assembled from what were effectively several
separate maps, superficially connected together. The map shows discrete, dense, heavily-connected
patches with just one or two long arrows bridging each patch to the rest — each "mini-map" was
evidently developed in isolation, without frequent consolidation back into the big picture.

**Diagnostic signal.** Visually dense sub-clusters joined by a handful of long-distance arrows;
each patch is internally rich but the connections *between* patches are thin. Islands (like
cracked glass) creates an *illusion* of big-picture connectivity, while the actual relationships
joining the islands are weak and easily forgotten.

**Remedy.** Caused by prestudy that was too narrow/detailed in scope, or by segmental mapping
(below); the fix is upstream — widen prestudy scope and consolidate to the big picture
throughout, rather than developing regions independently and stitching at the end.

**Sources.** 06-final-checkpoint-ascent-iii.md ("Read Between The Chunks" — co-occurring with
cracked glass); LC25 (a student asks what to do when two topics studied separately turn out to
belong together: "should I use my mindmaps as a reference or should I make the new mindmap from
scratch?" — tagged `#Islands`, unresolved in captured text).

## No backbone

**Mechanism (L02 violation).** The main chunks of a topic have not been related to each other,
so no clean top-level flow (backbone) exists — distinct from cracked glass (which has an
indirect backbone) and from wheel-and-spokes (which has a backbone of sorts, radiating from one
hub).

**Diagnostic signal.** Points on the map "look like they are all over the place or seem
chaotic"; per the Ascent I checkpoint's own quiz text, this is specifically "not having a clear
backbone of the main ideas because the main chunks have not been related with each other" — as
distinct from spiderwebbing's chaotic-line symptom or missing prestudy/visual-anchor causes
offered as wrong-answer distractors for the same quiz.

**Remedy.** Explicitly ask how the largest chunks relate to and affect each other before
proceeding to sub-chunks and detail.

**Sources.** 86.md; 09-ascent-i-check.md (quiz distractor text, no dedicated learning-note);
05-barriers-to-aiming.md (named in the bare mistake-list, undefined there).

## Segmental mapping / segmented mindmap

**Mechanism (L06 violation).** Fully developing one chunk or concept to complete depth before
moving to the next, without considering how it relates to the big picture. The clinic-level term
for the same failure is "segmented mindmap" or "segmented mindset."

**Diagnostic signal.** Three named consequences: it diminishes chunk-structure quality; it
creates potentially massive time inefficiency (because the brain has no big-picture relevance
frame to organise around while working a given chunk to full depth); and it increases the
likelihood of other problems, including spiderwebbing. A student in LC30 links segmented
mindmaps specifically to prestudy scope not matching final mindmap scope, and to "mixing
layers" (not finishing one layer before starting the next) — both offered as candidate causes,
neither confirmed in captured text. A student in LC29 self-reports roughly 60 keywords collected
in about an hour for one topic (endodontic treatment) and links this directly to suspected
segmented-mindmap risk, tentatively also to their own mental-health state at the time.

**Remedy.** Not captured beyond the general layers-of-learning discipline (finish global
structure before committing to full depth in any one region); avoiding segmental mapping is
named as a target in LC44 ("avoiding segmental mapping") with no method captured.

**Sources.** 07-final-checkpoint-ascent-ii.md ("A Cosmic Trio," names segmental mapping as a
cause of cracked glass); 06-final-checkpoint-ascent-iii.md (Islands' causes); LC30; LC29;
LC44 (named, mechanism uncaptured — video-gapped clinic outside this file's direct sourcing but
consistent with the pattern).

## Framing bias

**Mechanism (L06 violation).** The general cognitive bias that conclusions are shaped by how
options are presented, applied to chunking: chunks default to mirroring how content was
presented by a teacher, textbook, or lecture rather than being independently, cognitively
optimised. Framing bias commonly produces the false conclusion "there is no other way to chunk
this," when in fact multiple cognitively-advantageous structures usually exist and must be
actively sought.

**Diagnostic signal.** Only one chunk structure comes to mind, and it closely mirrors the
source's presentation order; difficulty even *imagining* an alternative framing. In anatomy
specifically, this is flagged as a red flag when a student "can only think of ONE way" to chunk
the region.

**Remedy.** Order control and independent chunking through genuine inquiry; actively challenge
"there's no other way" conclusions; if prechunking is being used and framing bias is strong, stop
prechunking altogether (see Prechunking risk below).

**Multi-session compounding (advanced class).** Live Clinic 28 documents framing bias as
*compounding across sessions*, not just a single-instance mistake: re-organising a mindmap
becomes progressively harder with every additional study session because each session leaves
behind a "pre-existing frame" that the next session's re-organisation attempt has to fight
against. The remedy for this compounding form is not captured (the student's own question is
answered only in uncaptured video).

**Rigid-domain resistance class.** A related, domain-specific variant surfaces repeatedly with
highly structured source material: medical treatment algorithms (LC28 — "what is the best method
to represent a treatment algorithm for a group of diseases? ... I always end up with a single
node chain pattern") and rigid diagnostic-criteria domains such as a fixed list of clinical
conditions (LC21 — a student reports being able to chunk "Fear-Related Disorders" well, but
"Psychotic Disorders" and "Cervical Ectropion & Cancer" resist chunking because of "the rigid
nature of these Core Conditions," producing forced, weak single-link connections). Both cases
describe the same underlying class: source material whose external structure is itself rigidly
authoritative (a protocol, a diagnostic checklist) resists independent re-chunking more than
looser subject matter does. No general remedy for this class is captured beyond the standard
order-control/framing-bias guidance above.

**Sources.** 06-aim-examples.md (canonical definition + "Deep dive: Anatomy" red-flag);
07-final-checkpoint-ascent-ii.md ("Baking Without Ingredients" — prechunking's interaction with
framing bias); LC28 (multi-session compounding; treatment-algorithm rigidity); LC21 (Core
Conditions rigidity); LC26 (a student reports "I don't have importance based questions I already
knew the answer to" after redrawing a map three times — a related retrospective-rationalization
risk, see Lower-order inquiry).

## Reverse causality

**Mechanism (L02/L03 violation).** Information is justified as important only by reference to
another piece of information that itself has to be memorised, creating a closed loop: "A is
important because it creates B" connects A to nothing except B, so the arrow from A to B must
itself be rote-memorised. This defeats the purpose of chunking — instead of reducing memory load
through logical structure, it adds a new item to memorise. The direction of arrows matters here
specifically: a student in LC29 asks whether the important node in a chunk should be the one
with many arrows going *into* it or many arrows going *out of* it — the underlying concern is
that circular or self-referential importance-justification (arrows that only point back into a
closed A–B loop) is the failure, regardless of which direction is drawn.

**Diagnostic signal.** Answering "why is X important" collapses into restating X's own
definition rather than giving a genuine downstream consequence. The clearest self-caught
instance in the corpus (LC24): a student notices their own answer — "Domain is important because
well, it provides the information needed to find the range" — is circular, since domain and
range are being used to justify each other with no connection to anything outside that pair. The
full diagnostic test for this pattern, with the closed-loop vs. network figures, is preserved
verbatim in reverse-causality-test.md.

**Stage-gating.** Live Clinic 47 explicitly flags reverse causality as "very advanced... only
applicable for students at Ascent 3 and above" when first introducing the term. In practice,
however, students well below Ascent 3 already run into and self-diagnose the same pattern without
the vocabulary for it: LC24's Domain/range example, LC29's arrows-in-vs-out question, and LC20's
more general request to "distinguish between relationships which are the most meaningful versus
relationships are superficial" (explicitly tagged `#Reverse causality` by the student) are not
attributable to any stated Ascent-3+ stage. The stage-gate therefore reads as a recommendation
about when the *named diagnostic concept* is formally taught, not a claim that the underlying
confusion is cognitively inaccessible earlier — students visibly encounter the failure mode
before they have a name for it.

**Prevention.** Intuitive chunking (above) is stated to prevent reverse causality; importance
checklisting and simple question-and-answer (below) are stated to increase its risk.

**Sources.** 87.md (canonical definition + diagnostic test); LC47 (stage-gating claim); LC24
(Domain/range worked instance); LC29 (arrows-in-vs-out question); LC20 (generalized
meaningful-vs-superficial framing); LC23 (a student's own attempted taxonomy: "cause effect
relationships? type of a larger category?" as an unresolved attempt to classify valid vs.
invalid "why important" answer types).

## Prechunking risk

**Mechanism (L06 violation, conditional).** Prechunking is forming chunks *before* inquiry
begins. It is not inherently a fault — it can save time as a hypothesis springboard — but becomes
one when the prechunked structure is treated as correct/final rather than actively challenged and
revised through inquiry.

**Diagnostic signal / conditions under which to avoid it entirely.** Three explicit rules:
(1) prechunking is acceptable only if the resulting chunks remain hypotheses and continue to be
modified; (2) avoid prechunking if there is any difficulty breaking from chunking the same way
the source presents information (i.e. weak order control); (3) avoid prechunking entirely if
spiderwebbing is occurring or the Aim step feels ineffective. A stronger, later restatement adds:
because framing bias can be powerful, learners who struggle to break free from prechunked
structures specifically are advised to avoid prechunking altogether and form chunks only after
deliberate inquiry.

**Remedy.** Treat any prechunk as a springboard, not a conclusion; if resistance to revising it
appears, stop prechunking for that topic.

**Sources.** 06-aim-examples.md ("Demonstration of core techniques 2" — the three rules; the
"chunking for medicine" retinal-detachment example is explicitly cross-referenced elsewhere as
the corpus's canonical illustration of prechunking risk); 07-final-checkpoint-ascent-ii.md
("Baking Without Ingredients" — deepens the caution, cross-references the retinal-detachment
example by name).

## Lower-order inquiry

**Mechanism (L01/L04 violation, expressed as a chunking fault).** Questions asked about isolated
facts, processes, or a single relationship between just two concepts — not "why is this
important," and not multi-concept comparisons.

**Diagnostic signal.** Questions promote only isolated thinking; caused by prior habits of
isolated thinking not yet replaced by relationship/grouping habits — explicitly *not* caused by
time pressure or by inherently difficult terminology (both offered and rejected as
distractor-causes in the corpus's own quiz).

**Remedy.** Follow the question-quality guidelines: keep questions broad, open, and
multi-relational (see inquiry-based-learning / question-quality content elsewhere in the corpus
for the full criteria; this entry preserves only the chunking-relevant framing).

**Sources.** 09-ascent-i-check.md ("Relationships Vs Chunking").

## Simple question and answer

**Mechanism (L01/L04 violation).** Writing literal, often brief, answers to inquiry questions,
which closes off the potential for multi-relational, higher-order evaluation — distinct from
lower-order inquiry (a fault in the *question*) in that this is a fault in how the *answer* is
handled even when the question itself might be adequate.

**Diagnostic signal.** Answers are short and final rather than explored for multiple possible
relationships. Occurs because learners have not understood that the value of asking questions is
the higher-order thinking it invokes — not because some questions are inherently simple, nor
because there are too many relationships to reasonably address (both rejected as
distractor-causes in the corpus's own quiz).

**Remedy.** Treat every answer as a candidate for further relational exploration before moving
on.

**Sources.** 03-aim-question-quality.md (first named/defined here as answering with "a single,
simple linear answer, avoiding deeper processing"); 09-ascent-i-check.md ("Finding Flags -
Ascent 1" — full cause statement).

## Importance checklist(ing)

**Mechanism (L03 violation).** Answering a "why is this important" question by finding only
**one** reason, when the question had potential for many — the question itself may be
high-quality, but its yield is not exploited. Distinct from asking questions in an unnecessarily
narrow context, and distinct from merely writing the reason directly under the question (both
rejected as distractor-definitions in the corpus's own quiz).

**Diagnostic signal.** Each importance question generates exactly one relationship; not enough
relationships are generated to compare and evaluate for chunk formation, despite the question's
potential for many. Similar failure family to simple question-and-answer — both blunt the
benefit of the inquiry process, just at different points (question-answering discipline here vs.
answer-handling there).

**Remedy.** Find multiple reasons for importance and evaluate how multiple concepts are similar
in different ways; rearranging just a few concepts into an alternative chunk can reveal an
entirely new, potentially very different, chunk structure.

**Sources.** 07-final-checkpoint-ascent-ii.md ("Interchunkecular forces," full definition and
cause; note the accompanying Learning-notes block for this item is itself headed "Loading..." in
the source capture — a rendering artifact, not a missing definition, since the definition text
that follows is intact); LC27 (a student tags their own submission `#Importance checklisting`
after collecting 100+ keywords and feeling repetitive); LC25 (`#Importance based chunking,
prevent importance checklist`, a student explicitly asking whether counting non-directional
detail lines toward the 2-4 Rule risks reintroducing this fault).

## Over-visualisation

**Mechanism (L02 violation, note-taking-specific).** Turning too many words into visuals/images.
Visuals work as memory "landmarks"; too many landmarks (a different one at the end of every
street) stop functioning as landmarks at all.

**Diagnostic signal.** Four named sub-causes: visuals placed on the most forgettable (rather
than most important/highest-forgetting-risk) concepts; fear of using words to represent
concepts; the belief that every keyword needs a visual to be memorable; and over-focus on the
act of making visuals over other processing methods such as grouping and comparing.

**Remedy.** Reserve images for content that is conceptually important or at elevated risk of
being forgotten, rather than applying them uniformly.

**Sources.** fundamentals-2/03-note-taking.md (introduces "avoid over-visualising"); 07-final-
checkpoint-ascent-ii.md ("Drawing Abstractly Vs Accurately" — precise definition: visuals for
too many ideas/concepts, making the map hard to follow); fundamentals-checkpoint.md ("A-maze-ing"
— the four sub-causes, most granular version of this definition in the corpus).

## Hieroglyphics

**Mechanism (L02 violation, note-taking-specific).** The same image is used repeatedly, directly
substituting for a specific word at a roughly 1:1 ratio, instead of images being used as
distinctive landmarks. When used this way, imagery loses its memory benefit through repetition
and commonness — landmarks become streetlamps (indistinguishable, forgettable individually).

**Diagnostic signal.** Words directly replaced with simple images at a 1:1 ratio, used
repeatedly and sometimes linearly; the underlying notes remain "still very linear" despite the
presence of images — the images do not rescue an otherwise-linear, unprocessed structure. Occurs
because learners have misunderstood *why* visualisation helps (it is meant to reduce word count,
force deeper processing, and create landmark cues — not to serve as a direct picture-dictionary),
compounded by high content volume and time pressure.

**Remedy.** Use grouping/relationships as the primary processing tool; add images only as
reinforcement once material is already processed, and vary/personalise the imagery rather than
reusing one image per recurring word.

**Sources.** fundamentals-2/03-note-taking.md (canonical definition, worked chemistry-equation
example: "metal + acid → salt + hydrogen," figure
`../research/_assets/inline-images/IMG_209CC244D6A7-1.jpeg` for the hieroglyphic version and
`../research/_assets/inline-images/IMG_6BF664C53B4C-1.jpeg` for the improved, integrated-image
version); fundamentals-checkpoint.md ("Why We Process Notes"); 07-final-checkpoint-ascent-ii.md
(named as a quiz option; the item's own "Learning notes" block is captured only as the literal
text "Loading..." in the source — a genuine capture failure, not an absence of definition, since
the term is fully defined via the two Fundamentals-2 sources above).

## Wheel and spokes

**Mechanism (L02 violation).** Good chunking may exist, but all relationships radiate outward
from a single central hub — usually the topic's own title — rather than showing lateral
connectivity between chunks and concepts. Indicates inadequate independent chunking: the map
communicates "these are the main concepts for this topic" without ever establishing how the main
concepts relate to each other, which is what a real backbone is supposed to express.

**Diagnostic signal.** All top-level chunks connect only to a central node, never to each other;
in advanced/repeated form, a learner may reuse a "wheel" or "cycle" chunk structure across
unrelated topics, producing similarly-shaped maps regardless of subject and correspondingly weak
retrieval in complex, nuanced contexts, because core ideas are never deeply interconnected.
Usually the topic's own name does not need to appear on the map at all. Can co-occur with
waterfalling.

**Remedy.** Deeply examine similarities between concepts to form accurate chunks at each layer,
and explicitly determine and express the relationship between the main ideas rather than
defaulting all of them to the topic title.

**Sources.** 05-ascent-iii/03-grinde-maps.md (first defined, under GRINDE's "Interconnected"
element); 06-final-checkpoint-ascent-iii.md ("Ascent 3 Stopper" — full mechanism, explicitly
flagged as a mistake that "should never occur at an Ascent 3 level," co-occurring here with
insufficient chunking and missing keyword anchors).

## Too wordy

**Named-but-undefined.** Appears only as a quiz answer option (Ascent III checkpoint, "70D6CEE2"
item) alongside cracked glass, wheel and spokes, waterfall, spiderweb, keyword-anchoring-needed,
and islands. No definition, mechanism, or remedy is given anywhere in the captured corpus. The
plain-language reading (notes/labels carrying more words than necessary, crowding out visual and
relational processing) is consistent with the broader note-taking guidance (VPReFRE's
word-count-limit instruction) but this specific named fault is never itself explained.

**Sources.** 06-final-checkpoint-ascent-iii.md (named only).

## Unshaped

**Mechanism (L02 violation).** The map lacks a clearly defined overall shape. Each topic's
relationships and chunk structure should produce a unique, distinctive visual "shape"; unshaped
occurs specifically when spatial arrangement is not emphasised, not because chunk structures
themselves are poor or because relationships are too superficial (both offered and rejected as
distractor-causes).

**Diagnostic signal.** A common, easily-fixed mistake at Ascent 2–3; the resulting map is a
normal iteration right after Shooting but should not persist into the Skin step.

**Remedy.** Be deliberate and intentional with spatial arrangement; doing so forces a higher
level of evaluation (it requires being explicit about how relationships and main flows are
expressed) and creates a visually distinctive map that serves as an additional memory cue.

**Sources.** 06-final-checkpoint-ascent-iii.md ("A Collision Of Theories" — full definition and
cause).

## Detail stripped

**Mechanism (L02 violation, opposite failure to over-detailing).** The map's more detailed
layers (3–4) are not expressed at all, producing a very simple, "barren" structure.

**Diagnostic signal.** Acceptable and even appropriate in the earliest, hypothesis-testing phase
of a topic; detrimental if it persists, because it avoids processing the detailed information
that will eventually need to be retrieved, and hides relationships between detailed concepts
that might otherwise refine the chunk structure further. Caused by avoidance behavior (fear of
being overwhelmed by volume), by lower-order habits that overcommit to whichever layer the
learner is *not* afraid to forget, or simply by learners not knowing that further detail should
be added or how to process it.

**Remedy.** Establish a clearer backbone/logic layer, then add detail step by step, one new
"layer" of keywords at a time, repeating the inquiry → similarity → propose-chunk →
evaluate-intuitiveness → hypothesise-structure cycle at each layer rather than stopping after the
backbone.

**Sources.** 06-final-checkpoint-ascent-iii.md ("Shape Of You" — co-occurs there with Unshaped).

## Lines over arrows

**Named-but-mostly-undefined.** No formal definition or mechanism is given anywhere in the
captured corpus; the term appears only as a quiz answer option (Ascent I checkpoint, "Be Like
Water" item; Ascent II checkpoint, "The Final Chunkdown" item). The only substantive context is a
self-diagnosis in LC30: a student, after reporting they have "some single node chains," adds
"And lines over arrows may be making it a bit harder to recall" — implying the fault is using

## Insufficient chunking

**Mechanism (L03 violation).** Not enough relationships come in and out of one chunk — after the
most obvious, first-pass groups are formed, no further exploration looks for sub-groupings within
them. One of the most common beginner mistakes.

**Diagnostic signal.** A chunk with very few connections branching off it, where further
sub-grouping was never attempted; caused by not exploring comparison/similarity/relationship-
finding past the first obvious groups — not by the information genuinely being incapable of
finer grouping (though the corpus does concede that *some* information cannot be grouped further
due to its own nature, so this is a real but bounded exception).

**Remedy.** Even within an existing group, look for further similarity and relatedness; multiple
groups can sometimes also be combined upward into a larger parent group. The simpler and more
discretely information can be grouped and connected, the better the resulting retention and
depth.

**Sources.** fundamentals-checkpoint.md ("Beginner Level Prestudy - Basic Organisation" — precise
corpus definition, resolving what earlier inventories flagged as an undefined term); 86.md
(precursor form: ">4 branches from a single point" as the lighter-weight fundamentals-level
signal, the structural inverse of insufficient chunking); 06-final-checkpoint-ascent-iii.md
(named as a quiz option, no separate learning note beyond the fundamentals-checkpoint
definition).

## Logic-backbone crowding

**Mechanism (L06 violation, clinic-only construct).** As concepts and sub-concepts are added
beneath a clean top-level logic relationship (e.g. A → B), the individual sub-concepts (e.g.
f, g, h, i, j feeding that relationship) visually crowd out the logic layer's shape. A student in
Live Clinic 27 gives the fullest captured description of this in the corpus: "The logic layer
completely disappears when I add the concepts that dictate the logic... when I add them, the
clear shape of the logic is gone... the more topics I add, the more the backbone disappears...
if I keep the thick arrow from A to B, it just crowds the mindmap further leading to
Spiderwebbing. Arrows cross over one another making it messy." The clean, crisp, memorable logic
layer and the crowded, illegible concept-layer version are described as two states of the same
map.

**Diagnostic signal.** A logic-only view of the map is clean and memorable; adding the concept
layer beneath it destroys that legibility rather than building on it; arrows begin crossing and
losing clear meaning.

**Remedy.** Not captured — the student's question is answered only in the clinic's uncaptured
video. This is a genuine, named-but-unresolved gap in the corpus's own layering guidance: the
general layers-of-learning instruction to keep logic, concepts, and details visually distinct
does not, on its own, specify how to add a concept layer beneath a backbone without the backbone
disappearing.

**Sources.** LC27 ("LOGIC BACKBONE DISAPPEARS").

## Mindmap-completion illusion

**Mechanism (L09-adjacent failure, expressed through chunking/mapping).** Finishing and
documenting a mindmap can create a false sense of "knowing" the material, because the effort of
transcription gets conflated with the effort of encoding. A student in Live Clinic 20 states this
directly: "whenever I am done with a mind map and document it, I feel like the knowledge somehow
shifts from my brain to the mind map, which makes this illusion that I 'know' the material
without actually retrieving it fully."

**Diagnostic signal.** Confidence in mastery rises simply because the map exists and looks
complete, independent of any retrieval performed against it.

**Remedy (student-proposed, not confirmed in captured text).** The same student proposes creating
curveball questions as the next interleaved-retrieval step specifically to counteract this
illusion; Justin's response is not captured.

**Sources.** LC20 (full quote and proposed remedy).

## Building-floors mental-model mismatch

**Mechanism (L02 violation, root-cause class).** A learner defaults to picturing a topic's
structure as a building with floors — a linear, foundation-up, sequential dependency model —
instead of as parallel, co-equal layers. Live Clinic 21 gives the fullest captured description:
"I think of things more of as a building with floors with the foundation being at the bottom than
a structure composing of layers with scaffolding then furniture and so on... how can the 4 main
ideas be in the same layer if one depends on the other?" This mismatch is offered by the student
as the self-diagnosed root cause of an inability to identify 3–4 co-equal main ideas in a chapter
during prestudy, and of a broader tendency toward linear, A-leads-to-B-leads-to-C mapping.

**Diagnostic signal.** Difficulty accepting that top-level ideas can be co-equal/parallel rather
than sequentially dependent; a persistent pull toward linear notes despite explicit non-linear
training.

**Remedy.** Not captured beyond the student's own diagnosis; Justin's response is in uncaptured
video. A related, domain-specific instance of the same underlying rigidity — external material
whose own structure is rigidly ordered — is documented under Framing bias's "rigid-domain
resistance class" above (the same clinic's Core Conditions example).

**Sources.** LC21 (full quote).
