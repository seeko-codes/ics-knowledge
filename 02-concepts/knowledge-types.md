## Taxonomy (kickstart Second Law)

The kickstart curriculum's "Second Law" ("learn from the right teacher") states that mastering a
skill requires three types of knowledge: **declarative**, **procedural**, and **conditional**.
Alongside the taxonomy, this lesson frames mistakes as diagnostic rather than as failures — they
reveal personal tendencies and barriers that must be identified and iterated on — under a
"fail fast, fail safe" principle: make small mistakes early to avoid larger ones later.
Over-reliance on questions and theory-gathering, without moving to practice once a working
understanding exists, is named as a delay mechanism to be avoided.

## Definitions

**Declarative knowledge** is information based on facts, concepts, and logic — the knowledge
involved in encoding content material. It is sometimes also called conceptual or descriptive
knowledge. Predominantly declarative topics include science subjects, history, politics, law,
economics theory, accounting theory, mathematics theory, business and finance theory, coding
logic, and language vocabulary/syntax/grammar/structure.

**Procedural knowledge** is stored in different parts of the brain than declarative knowledge and
involves different cognitive processes. It is based on actions, behaviours, and processes rather
than facts or concepts — the knowledge of *how* to perform a skill or procedure. It is dynamic,
action-oriented, skill-based, context-dependent, and often implicit, requiring practice and
hands-on experience to master; unlike declarative knowledge's static facts, it is tied to
performance and improves with experience. Examples of procedural-focused disciplines: conducting a
lab experiment with technical precision, driving or riding a bike, cooking or baking, executing
study skills in practice, solving mathematics or physics problems by calculation, speaking or
writing a language fluently, producing an accurate accounting balance sheet, and building/coding an
application. In most of these examples the theoretical knowledge of *what* to do is declarative
while the executed skill of *doing* it is procedural.

**Conditional knowledge** is the knowledge of *when* and *how* to deploy declarative and procedural
knowledge. It is developed specifically via integrative retrieved execution — practice that
combines multiple skills/processes to achieve a more complex function, rather than isolated
repetition of a single procedure (see interleaving table).

## The declarative/procedural relationship

In most real knowledge applications, a combination of strong declarative and procedural knowledge
is required. Someone with **strong procedural but weak declarative** knowledge may excel at
familiar problems but struggle with novel challenges: a coder with strong procedural skill may
script common functions effortlessly but struggle with coding challenges, technical interviews, and
projects outside their comfort zone, because the declarative gap creates a bottleneck in the
ability to design logical, efficient, strategically aligned solutions. Someone with **strong
declarative but weak procedural** knowledge understands problem-solving approaches but struggles
with accurate execution: they may conceptualise ambitious projects but produce coding errors, bugs,
and inefficient output, because the procedural gap creates a bottleneck in execution regardless of
design quality. Truly excellent learners are strong in both domains.

## Sequencing (LC39)

Live Clinic 39 ("Learning procedural skills with interleaving") states an explicit sequencing
claim: **declarative knowledge must be acquired before procedural knowledge**. It further claims
that increasing the declarative component of a subject increases the higher-order thinking applied
within it, and that teaching plus the use of analogies help deepen declarative knowledge. This
session frames its content around the same three-way declarative/procedural/conditional split
described above, applied specifically to choosing an interleaving strategy that matches how the
knowledge will actually be used (see interleaving).

## The professional 2×2 (LC59)

Live Clinic 59 ("Learning for work and professional settings") combines the declarative/procedural
axis with the higher-order/lower-order axis (L01) into a single four-quadrant framework for
professional and work-based learning contexts: declarative × procedural crossed with
higher-order × lower-order. Documented claims from the session: in professional settings, it is
not necessary to maximise all four quadrants simultaneously — the framework is used to identify
which quadrant(s) a given task or context actually calls for; coming prepared to engage in
higher-order learning matters specifically in situations like seminars and workshops; a person does
not need a high knowledge baseline to begin higher-order thinking; non-linear notes help manage
cognitive load and facilitate higher-order thinking in professional contexts such as meetings,
where the volume of information can vary unpredictably. A SaaS sales representative is used as a
worked example of professional-context learning, and developers who lack higher-order thinking are
cited (from the presenter's coaching experience) as a group who struggle as a result. The layout
and full worked content of the four-quadrant grid itself is
*[video-only: not captured]* (ics-course/04-supplementary/01-live-clinics/17-lc59.md); only the
session's framing and topic sequence were captured in text.

## How type selects technique

Knowledge type is the primary sorting key for choosing an interleaving technique: the interleaving
table is organised as separate declarative-focused and procedural-focused technique sets, each
further crossed by order of learning (lower/mid/higher-order for declarative; lower/mid/higher-order
for procedural). See 03-details/tables/interleaving-table.md for the full technique-to-type-to-order
mapping, including guidance for mixed-knowledge-type subjects.
