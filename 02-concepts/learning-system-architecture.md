## Mechanism

The program models a learner as a system to be engineered ("learning system"), built by understanding the
mechanics of how learning happens in the brain rather than by blindly applying techniques — a stance the
corpus calls opening the "black box" of learning. The model is organised around three spheres of skills, a
two-process account of what learning actually is, a dimensions/skills distinction that explains why the
same technique performs differently across learners, and a chain-of-performance model that traces outcomes
back through skills to attributes to identity.

## Encoding and retrieval: the two key processes (simplified)

Learning is stated to divide, in simplified form, into two key processes:

- **Encoding** — produces the biggest long-term change but is slow to train.
- **Retrieval** — faster to train, and prioritised first because it exposes higher-order thinking patterns
  that make later encoding work easier.

The source explicitly flags this split as a simplification ("Learning can be divided into two key processes
(simplified): encoding and retrieval"), not a complete account — the fuller mechanism of each process (deep
processing, schema, memory dynamics) is carried by the basis vectors (L01, L02, L07) and by the concepts that
combine them (higher-order-learning, knowledge-as-schema, retrieval-practice), not by this architecture-level
statement.

## Three core spheres of skills

An ultra-learner is modelled as having three spheres of skills and techniques:

1. **Learning** — the cognitive skills, habits, and techniques required to process and retain information.
2. **Enablers** — time management, attention control, procrastination regulation, strategic goal setting,
   and task prioritisation; these ensure effective learning practices are applied consistently to goals that
   matter.
3. **Growth** — how quickly and effectively mental and behavioural habits can be changed and learning
   processes improved over time: reflective practice, learning from mistakes, structured improvement.

**Growth is the rate-limiter.** Growth skills are stated to be a rate-limiter to improvement in all other
spheres, and therefore make the biggest difference to overall improvement — a claim that recurs as the
justification for the program's sequencing (see two-phase rationale, below): enabler skills are strengthened
first to remove consistency rate-limiters, then growth mindset and reflective skills are developed so that
later technique-learning is not wasted, and only then is technique training (retrieval, then encoding)
undertaken.

At its core, learning is framed as an information-processing system, measurable via retention and quality
(mastery). Higher-order learning (forming schemas of integrated knowledge) is more efficient and improves
both; lower-order (isolated) learning does not build schemas, decays quickly, and requires time-consuming
repetition to retain — see higher-order-learning for the full mechanism.

## Dimensions vs skills

**Skills** are the ability to use and execute techniques masterfully to achieve a desired effect.
**Dimensions** are attribute-like measures reflecting habits, processes, and tendencies (e.g. deep
processing, self-regulation, mindset, retrieval); they are not fixed and change over time through training.

Dimensions gate skill execution: a learner's ability to execute a given skill depends on their dimension
levels. For example, non-linear note-taking (a skill) requires sufficient deep processing and
self-regulation (dimensions) to perform accurately, and mindset must be sufficiently high just to be able to
learn the skill in the first place.

Dimensions are improved indirectly, not by direct dimension-targeted training: learning new skills and
techniques and building new habits through lesson practice directly enhances the underlying dimensions.
Dimensions improve at different rates — deep processing and mindset require substantial cognitive retraining
and are correspondingly slower to shift, while retrieval is more technique-focused and improves faster.
Diagnostic reassessment is recommended every two to three months (earliest permitted retake: one month after
the prior assessment).

## The five dimensions

The diagnostic assessment evaluates five key dimensions, each scored on a four-level scale (Level 1-4) and
each corresponding to one lesson in the program's initial diagnostic sequence:

1. **Deep processing** — the brain's ability to extract meaningful learning from information (see
   deep-processing-development).
2. **Mindset** — fixed vs growth orientation toward mistakes, uncertainty, and difficulty (see
   mindset-emotion).
3. **Self-regulation** — the ability to monitor, guide, and refine one's own learning processes (detailed
   below).
4. **Retrieval** — the ability to recall and use knowledge from memory (see retrieval-practice).
5. **Self-management** — the habits, routines, and environments that make consistent action possible (see
   time-priority-management, habit-formation).

## Self-regulation: the enabler of all other skills

Self-regulation is singled out architecturally: if deep processing is the power of a car's engine,
self-regulation is the driving skill. Both must be well developed for consistent performance across varying
conditions. With good self-regulation, learners make strategic adjustments even in unfavourable or unguided
conditions; with poor self-regulation, performance depends on raw deep-processing ability alone and degrades
under less guidance or harder conditions. Self-regulation is explicitly named "the enabler of all other
skills" in the learning system, and rests on **metacognition** — thinking about one's own thinking, i.e.
monitoring cognitive processes and intentionally changing them to stay aligned with learning objectives.

**Technique decline** is named as a standing risk even for skilled, high-self-regulation learners: complacency
after early success can cause a gradual, often months-long, undetected decline in processing technique,
sometimes resulting in reverted skills and performance. The stated remedy is to maintain consistent
reflective practice and calibrate regularly — "learning is a lifelong endeavour."

### Three requirements for developing self-regulation

1. **Fostering metacognition** — learning to reflect on, monitor, and correctly judge how techniques
   influence cognitive processes (and therefore outcomes); begins within the first few program stages, with
   more nuanced reflection methods introduced once the High-Yield-phase foundation is established.
2. **Embracing desirable difficulty** — recognising that something beneficial can feel difficult, and
   resisting the common failure of misreading difficulty as ineffectiveness (and therefore wrongly
   simplifying the approach); available from the moment any new technique is learned, but can take weeks or
   months to fully embrace.
3. **Getting book smart** — building theoretical knowledge of learning and self-management so that
   adjustments to one's system are targeted rather than trial-and-error; an ongoing process across the
   entire program.

## Chain of performance

A four-tier causal stack is used to diagnose which layer of the system is actually the constraint, since
simply wanting an outcome does not produce it:

1. **Outcomes and performance** ← come from...
2. **Skills and processes** (e.g. encoding and retrieval skills, time management and prioritisation skills,
   self-reflection and problem-solving skills, focus and attention management skills, emotional
   self-regulation skills) ← come from...
3. **Attributes and behaviours** (e.g. open-mindedness, willingness to experiment, willingness to face
   discomfort, diligence and commitment, patience, humility, self-compassion) ← come from...
4. **Mindsets, beliefs, and identities** (e.g. fear of failure, beliefs about intelligence and abilities,
   beliefs about talent and capabilities, identity as a learner, identity as someone who tries their best,
   identity as someone who always gets back up).

Deficiencies at level 2 (skills/processes) cause outcome problems directly; missing attributes at level 3
block skill development even when the relevant technique is known (the source gives the example of trying to
develop program skills without patience or open-mindedness); mindsets, beliefs, and identities at level 4
drive the attributes and behaviours above them. Overcoming a fixed mindset is framed as requiring three
things: taking action without waiting to feel motivated, reflecting effectively (Kolb's cycle — see
kolbs-cycle), and identifying which chain-of-performance level is actually the constraint before intervening.

## Two-phase program rationale

The program is structured into two phases whose relative speed and impact differ by design:

| | Phase 1: High-Yield | Phase 2: Growth |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Build a comprehensive, holistic learning system as fast as possible | Replace existing cognitive habits with more efficient ones; improve natural processing ability for life |
| Relevance to current situation | High — solves most common learning and self-management barriers | Moderate — the current biggest constraint is unlikely to be natural processing ability |
| Time to value | Low — techniques give immediate value, starting short-term and continuing for life | High — techniques take weeks to months to master |
| Overall impact | Moderate — highly beneficial, lifelong foundations, but some challenges remain hard to overcome without retraining cognitive habits | High — can be life-changing by making all learning more effective and efficient |

Rushing or underestimating the High-Yield phase is flagged as producing a poor foundation that forces
learners to return and re-learn later, wasting time and opportunity; for most learners, the majority of
learning and self-management barriers are resolved within the High-Yield phase alone. The Growth phase
requires the High-Yield foundation first specifically because its techniques take weeks to months and cannot
be layered onto unresolved urgent issues (see technique-progression for the exam-imminent corollary: do not
start Growth-phase techniques close to an assessment).

The commonly assumed skill-acquisition ratio (roughly 75% theory/understanding to 25% practice) is stated to
be inverted from reality: approximately 15% basic theory clarity, 20% practice/experience to surface the
biggest misconceptions and errors, 10% deepening understanding and correcting misconceptions, and 55% further
practice and challenging experience — with a feedback cycle running between practice/challenge and deeper
understanding ("the Theory-Practice cycle is the true engine of improvement"). This ratio recurs at the
technique-acquisition level in technique-progression.

## Learning triad (pointer)

Self-diagnosis in the corpus treats cognition, notes, and revision as a triad that must logically align — a
misalignment (e.g. relationally-processed cognition undermined by linear notes) is itself a diagnosable
fault. Full treatment: self-diagnosis (improvement-loop cluster, concept 26).
