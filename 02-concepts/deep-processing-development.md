## Definition

Deep processing is the brain's ability to extract meaningful learning from information. The
determining factor in meaningful learning is not the stimuli encountered but the cognitive
processes those stimuli trigger. Processing exists on a spectrum:

- **Higher levels** of deep processing result in stronger memory, deeper understanding, and the
  ability to use information in more complex ways. This involves critically analysing,
  connecting, and critiquing what is learned; linking ideas to prior knowledge; reflecting on
  broader implications.
- **Lower levels** (shallow processing) result in weaker memory and more superficial
  understanding: passive, "easy" learning such as rote-learning, repeating, and memorising or
  understanding information without wider context — grasping individual concepts while failing
  to understand the bigger picture.

Deep processing is significantly influenced, for most people, by genetics and early-childhood
experience. It is nonetheless trainable through consistent, deliberate practice.

## Deep-processing tanking

A classic "genius" is someone with strong deep-processing ability who can perform well using
mediocre learning methods, because exceptional deep processing compensates for weak technique.
This is termed **deep-processing tanking**. It is a source of confusion for learners without the
same baseline processing ability: they do not obtain the same results as "top learners" even when
using identical strategies, because the strategies were never the limiting factor for the top
learner. Deep-processing tanking is not sufficient on its own for sufficiently challenging
learning goals — even learners with strong native deep processing must train their other
dimensions to reach full potential.

## 4-level diagnostic

Each level carries a distinct habit profile and a distinct subjective-feeling profile.

**Level 1**
- Habits: rereading material repeatedly (reinforces recognition, not understanding); copying
  notes verbatim or highlighting large sections without analysing what matters most; memorising
  facts in isolation, disconnected from broader ideas; attempting to connect ideas but struggling
  to identify meaningful relationships.
- Feelings: frustration that effort isn't paying off; overwhelm at not seeing how new material
  connects to existing knowledge; disengagement because tasks feel dull or lack depth; a sense
  that no technique can move memory and performance past a ceiling; some problems appear
  impossible to solve, with no visibility into how others solve them.

**Level 2**
- Habits: understanding topics individually rather than noticing interconnections; thinking about
  connecting new ideas to existing knowledge, but not systematically or thoroughly; occasionally
  analysing for patterns but rarely beyond surface-level relationships; prioritising task
  completion over critically evaluating understanding; reviewing through repetition without
  restructuring notes or reflecting deeply.
- Feelings: confidence on simpler topics but uncertainty or frustration on complex material; a
  sense of grasping individual ideas but struggling to fit them into a cohesive whole; occasional
  progress that feels rewarding but is inconsistent; alternating engagement and disengagement
  depending on material difficulty; performance in a subject tracks closely with how much the
  subject is inherently enjoyed or how easily it happens to be presented.
- Diagnostic note: difficulties with retention or understanding can be masked when content
  difficulty is low; when challenge increases, compensating through time and effort stops
  working.

**Level 3**
- Habits: making clear connections between new information and existing knowledge; organising
  knowledge into meaningful structures; actively evaluating and refining understanding for
  accuracy and relevance; engaging in inquiry and critical thinking to explore relationships and
  patterns across topics; thinking about the big picture and trying to simplify information.
- Feelings: confidence in understanding and retaining most encountered information; capability
  with challenging material, with occasional uncertainty about the best approach; clear,
  organised thinking with a sense of control over the learning process for most topics; new
  information feels easier to handle than it does for many peers, even without heavier study.

**Level 4**
- Habits: naturally organising new ideas into existing knowledge frameworks; approaching learning
  strategically, focused on meaningful engagement and long-term retention; identifying and
  prioritising key connections, integrating ideas across domains and contexts; constantly
  relating information to the big picture; seeking the simplest way to understand something via
  analogies and categorisation.
- Feelings: confident and in control with complex or advanced material; clear and organised
  thinking with a natural ability to make meaningful connections; efficient and effective
  learning, with a sense of ease even in challenging situations.
- Failure mode even at Level 4: redundant or unnecessary processes not yet pruned from the
  learning system limit further efficiency gains.

## 4 training phases

Four phases of processing ability, developed in a structured sequence:

1. **Understanding and awareness.** Gain awareness of one's position on the processing spectrum
   and which techniques/thinking patterns produce deeper vs. shallower processing; begin making
   initial adjustments toward deeper processing. Sub-skills: recognise shallow vs. deep learning
   strategies; understand how the brain organises knowledge into schemas; build awareness of how
   effective habits enhance engagement and retention. Timing: begins within the first few stages
   of the program.
2. **Aligning technique.** Learn practical techniques that force deeper processing and enhance
   performance; these can be challenging and take time to replace existing habits. Sub-skills:
   identify similarities and differences between concepts; group and chunk related information;
   practise evaluating relationships and questioning meaning; layer learning, building
   foundational ideas before details. Timing: begins within the first few stages, continuing over
   several weeks.
3. **Refining and deepening.** Modify techniques at will to engage in deeper processing; deeper
   and more effective processing patterns become familiar, extending technique toward even
   deeper, more complex processing. Sub-skills: evaluate the importance of connections and
   relationships; refine and rework understanding for better organisation and cohesion;
   prioritise key ideas for clearer, more meaningful knowledge structures. Timing: begins in the
   Growth phase, from approximately stage 6–7 of the program onward; continues over weeks to
   months.
4. **Integration and mastery.** Bring multiple processing habits together into one fluid learning
   system, requiring high competence and fluency with every prior technique; produces a high
   baseline processing level that supports memory and understanding across every facet of
   learning. Sub-skills: use multiple cognitive processes together (questioning, analysing,
   grouping) in one sequence; apply knowledge flexibly across varied, complex contexts; simplify
   and adapt learned material for different purposes. Timing: focused development begins after
   approximately 4–9 months; mastery can take months to years depending on aptitude and
   diligence. It is technically possible to begin from the middle of the program onward, but the
   actual start point depends on prior practice level, baseline, consistency, and accuracy in the
   earlier phases.

## Tennis / scaffolding acquisition model

The brain relies on cognitive habits — patterns of thought developed over time — to process
information, because not every aspect of processing can be consciously controlled. Changing these
habits requires more than good intentions or instruction; it requires targeted experience. The
corpus illustrates this with a tennis analogy:

- Tennis requires mastering many technical skills (footwork, grip, swing mechanics, strategy)
  working together seamlessly. Stepping onto the court and playing full matches repeatedly, with
  no isolation of sub-skills, produces frustration, inconsistency, and stalled improvement.
- The effective approach is to **scaffold**: practise grip and stance, then isolate specific
  skills (a consistent forehand, controlled backhand, precise serve), drilling each until it
  becomes second nature before integrating them into structured rallies and game scenarios.
- As skills become fluid and automatic, attention frees up for higher-level strategy —
  anticipating an opponent's moves, adapting playstyle, maintaining mental endurance under
  pressure. High performance becomes habitual.

This mirrors deep-processing skill development: a learner cannot consciously micromanage every
cognitive process while learning, any more than a tennis player can consciously think through
every mechanical detail mid-match. The key is automating essential habits through practice and
refinement until they become second nature, after which complex material can be handled more
efficiently.

## 7-level processing ladder

Deep processing unlocks higher-order learning, but a central difficulty in training it is knowing
whether it is being performed "correctly." A 7-level ladder is used as a calibration reference:

1. Memorise
2. Try to understand
3. Rate the importance of each keyword
4. Group keywords together based on similarities
5. Find different kinds of groups, based on different similarities
6. Rate the importance and relevance of the different kinds of groups, deciding on the group
   judged best
7. Connect groups together to show how they influence each other

Most techniques taught in the program can be performed at different levels of this ladder; some
techniques make deeper processing easier, or force it. Once comfortable with a level, the
prescribed response is to recalibrate and move to the next, deeper level. The full calibration
procedure (task instructions, keyword-set sizing, self-monitoring protocol) is recorded in
`03-details/tables/processing-levels-ladder.md`.
