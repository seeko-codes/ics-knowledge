## ics-learning-support/skill-development/101.md
- **stage**: Learning Support / Skill development
- **role**: Q&A article — defines knowledge-mastery measurement framework
- **terms**:
  - Mastery levels 1-5: composite of Bloom's revised taxonomy + SOLO taxonomy; ordinal depth-of-learning scale
  - L1: recall facts/isolated details; repetition/memorisation-based
  - L2: explain isolated concepts; simple problems; simple discussion
  - L3: relationships between concepts; compare/contrast; not yet connected to big picture
  - L4 ("significant learning benefits"): heavily connected appreciation across concepts; value judgments on relative importance; big jump from L3
  - L5: creates new/novel info; hypotheses/conjectures; rare pre-PhD in formal education
  - Whole-Part-Whole reteaching technique (referenced as L4 method, defined elsewhere)
  - Chunkmaps (referenced as L4 method)
- **claims**:
  - Key principle: higher-order = integrated; lower-order = isolated
  - Better big-picture understanding → more meaningful info → greater retention/encoding → less repetition needed
  - Both lower- and higher-order methods needed in a comprehensive system
  - Lower-order approaches only produce lower-order outcomes; higher-order approaches produce both
  - Better to start learning with higher-order approaches; use lower-order as supplement / closer to assessment
  - Higher-order methods ease learning new content over time (builds expertise); lower-order methods increase overwhelm (info stays isolated)
- **criteria**: 5-level ordinal scale (no numeric %); L3 impact = "moderately helpful"; L4 impact = "extremely helpful, forces substantially deeper encoding"
- **tables**: Level(3,4) × Characteristic × Impact-on-learning
- **relations**: feeds directly into learning-efficiency.md (partial-mastery calc uses these levels)
- **sophistication**: canonical — definitional source for the mastery-level taxonomy used elsewhere in corpus

## ics-learning-support/skill-development/feedback-guide.md
- **stage**: Skill development
- **role**: guide — comprehensive feedback-seeking/using framework ("Mastering Feedback")
- **terms**:
  - Process-oriented goals: goals that improve HOW we learn, not WHAT — flagged as most valuable for long-term growth (vs unnamed contrast: outcome-oriented goals)
  - Feedback (def.): info about performance/understanding that guides improvement; 3 functions — (1) make sense of progress, (2) identify improvement areas, (3) guide future learning-behaviour decisions
  - Feedback as GPS (metaphor)
  - Feedback sources: Finder AI search (ICS proprietary AI search tool), Coach feedback, Self-Feedback
  - CLEAR Framework: Context, Learning Goal, Evaluate Your Work, Ask targeted questions, Review & follow-up ("Evaluate Your Work" = longest/most detailed step)
  - Sustainable assessment (research term) = self-evaluation as a durable, independent skill (not dependent on external judge)
  - Closing the Feedback Loop: receive → analyze → implement → produce new work → receive more feedback (cyclical)
- **claims** (research-cited):
  - Feedback reduces performance gap (Hattie & Timperley, 2007)
  - Feedback develops self-regulation (Butler & Winne, 1995)
  - Effective feedback reshapes knowledge/skills, enhancing outcomes (Wisniewski et al., 2020)
  - Constructive feedback builds confidence, accountability, engagement (Guo & Zhou, 2021)
  - Seeking/receiving/acting-on feedback is itself a transferable real-world skill (Richardson et al., 2012)
  - Quality of feedback received strongly depends on how it is asked
  - How a learner self-evaluates signals insight/metacognition to coaches — "often more valuable than the work itself"
  - Best learning outcomes occur when learners actively engage with feedback AND use it to produce new work
- **procedures**:
  - CLEAR (5 steps): Context (background, stage, special circumstances) → Learning Goal (specific skill/process, stage-relevant) → Evaluate Your Work (sub-foci: what you tried, what went well/poorly, vs expectations, biggest improvement area) → Ask Targeted Questions (limit 1-2 areas) → Review & Follow-up
  - Closing the loop (3 steps): 1) Read objectively (stay emotionally detached, clarify confusions), 2) Make an action plan (identify/prioritise/plan specific steps), 3) Test and review (experiment; multiple experiment-ideas per feedback round before needing more feedback)
- **criteria**: Finder AI ≈90% accuracy on common questions; ask targeted questions limited to 1-2 areas ("impossible to improve on too many things at once"); complex situations may need 3-4 more experiments before next coach round; simple situations can go weeks without more feedback
- **failure-modes**: "I don't know" as self-eval answer flagged as insufficient — must attempt detailed reflection regardless; vague/broad feedback requests → unhelpful/overwhelming response (quadrant model: narrow+low info=precise-but-vague; narrow+high=targeted skill improvement [ideal]; broad+low=general overload; broad+high=detailed-but-unfocused)
- **examples**: Detailed example — 2nd-year undergrad history exam (3 months out), interleaved retrieval prep, Fundamentals 2 stage. Full CLEAR walkthrough (context/goal/self-eval noting 70% retention, weak on details/dates, suspects re-reading is inefficient/questions Method allocation/targeted questions). Closing-the-loop actions: dropped re-reading, added 30 min/day high-yield flashcards, replaced summary pages with mindmaps; iterated after 1 week — 30 min insufficient (→45 min), mindmaps too hard (→hybrid linear notes + mini-mindmaps).

## ics-learning-support/skill-development/learning-efficiency.md
- **stage**: Skill development
- **role**: Q&A / procedural article — defines + calculates the learning-efficiency metric
- **terms**:
  - Learning efficiency = Total mastery and retention ÷ Total time spent learning
  - Total mastery = average of all partial masteries
  - Partial mastery = (retention at mastery level) ÷ (required retention at that level)
  - Depends on mastery-level framework from 101.md
- **claims**:
  - "Amount covered per session" is a useless efficiency metric (ignores retention, relearning time, transfer quality)
  - Efficient learners focus on quality, not "doing more"
  - More study hours don't proportionally raise mastery % if methods are limited (diminishing returns); "hammer" analogy — some mastery levels are categorically unreachable with the wrong method regardless of time/effort
  - Rate of forgetting rises with learning volume → learners rarely reach 100% retention, especially at higher mastery levels
  - Method strongly dictates ease of reaching higher mastery; some methods incompatible with high mastery absent very high deep processing
  - Learning efficiency depends on required mastery/retention level — lower requirements = faster to meet
- **procedures**: Calculating learning efficiency (6 steps):
  1. Identify highest & lowest required knowledge-mastery levels (ref. 101.md)
  2. Assess retention after ONE WEEK (%) per level (rationale: spacing intervals should allow a 1-week retrieval session; extrapolates to assessment retention)
  3. Identify required retention per level for the assessment
  4. Partial mastery = real 1-week retention ÷ required retention
  5. Total mastery = average of partial masteries
  6. Total time = sum of all learning + retrieval session time across all weeks for the subject
- **criteria**: retention measured specifically at the 1-week mark (not immediate); no other numeric threshold given (comparative only)
- **examples**: Jeremy — 2nd-year university economics exam.
  - Mastery range: L1 (min, fact recall) to L4 (max)
  - Raw data table: L1 60%/80% required; L2 60%/90%; L3 50%/70%; L4 30%/60%
  - Partial masteries: L1=75%, L2=67%, L3=71%, L4=50%
  - Total mastery = avg(75,67,71,50) = 66%; time spent = 30 hrs → "learning efficiency = 66% over 30 hours"
  - Inefficient-learner variant: same Jeremy, if inefficient — 30→60 hrs only raises mastery 66%→76% (10-point gain for double time) — diminishing-returns illustration
- **tables**:
  1. Level × Retention × Required-for-assessment (Jeremy raw)
  2. Level × Retention × Required × Partial-masteries (Jeremy calc)
  3. Learner-type comparison: Learner type × Mastery profile × Time investment × Example values — 4 rows: inefficient+low-deep-processing (50%/100hrs, flat with more hours); inefficient+high-deep-processing (70%/50hrs, slow increase); efficient+low-deep-processing (70%/50hrs, proportional increase); efficient+high-deep-processing (90%/30hrs, proportional increase)

## ics-learning-support/skill-development/metacognition-learn-better.md
- **stage**: Skill development
- **role**: Q&A / conceptual+practical guide connecting metacognition to Kolb's and Marginal Gains
- **terms**:
  - Cognition: mental processes of acquiring knowledge/understanding (thinking, understanding, learning, remembering)
  - Metacognition: thinking about how you think; reflecting on/controlling cognitive processes (monitor, evaluate, recognize, adjust)
  - Self-regulation (aka regulation / self-regulated learning in research lit): using metacognitive insight to change habits/processes; components — Planning (set goals/strategies before), Monitoring (track progress/correct application during), Evaluating (reflect/decide improvements after)
  - Metacognitive-knowledge-types taxonomy (deep-dive, flagged as "limited practical benefit" by ICS):
    - Learning Context: Person Knowledge (own strengths/weaknesses), Task Knowledge (task requirements), Strategy Knowledge (which strategy + when)
    - Nature of Knowledge: Declarative (facts/concepts), Procedural (how to perform), Conditional (when/why to use a strategy)
  - ICS's preferred simplified framing: (1) know what you currently do, (2) know what you should do, (3) adjust current→should, one step at a time
- **claims**:
  - Metacognition = "engine" powering ability to upgrade learning; drives the practice→mistakes→reflection→learning cycle
  - Metacognitive skills are transferable across domains (problem-solving, time management, etc.)
  - Benefits: better self-regulation, adaptability, better results, confidence/motivation
  - Metacognition is continuous — before study (strategy selection), during study (monitoring effectiveness), after study (reflect for future change); KEY MESSAGE "metacognition never ends"
  - Marginal Gains Tracking & Stacking + Kolb's Experiential Cycle both closely align with / operationalise metacognitive thinking
  - ICS explicitly states the academic metacognitive-knowledge-types taxonomy has "limited practical benefit" vs their simplified 3-step framing — an explicit distinction between textbook taxonomy and applied program framing
- **procedures**:
  - Enhancing metacognition generally (4 steps): (1) develop awareness of current doing/thinking, (2) learn correct ways to think, (3) practise regulating/adjusting, (4) reflect+experiment to improve the metacognitive skill itself
  - Enhancing Kolb's with metacognition (4 tips): 1) focus reflection on WHY/what-triggered/how-reacted, not just WHAT happened; 2) in abstraction, identify habits/tendencies/beliefs (possibly unconscious) producing the events, not just surface patterns; 3) avoid circular feelings — explore triggers/responses, don't just restate feelings; 4) take small steps — apply marginal gains to track/stack; don't reflect on experiences too large to identify a 1% improvement in
- **examples**: student reading history textbook — cognition (focus on content, remember dates, summarise) vs metacognition (self-questioning "Do I understand? Should I re-read? How will I remember this?" → switches to mind map/self-testing); flashcards for vocab = task+strategy knowledge; mind map for historical causes = conditional knowledge

## ics-learning-support/skill-development/skills-audit.md
- **stage**: Skill development
- **role**: guide — structured procedural audit method
- **terms**:
  - Skills audit: structured review/assessment of learning system to pinpoint issues + drive targeted improvements; reviews techniques against guidelines
  - Technique decline: skill loss/forgetting from disuse after a long break
  - LEEP: acronym for the 4-step audit process — Learning flow map, Evaluate, Error correction, Perfect practice
  - Skill screen: fast/simple audit approach (lesson-by-lesson rating using checkpoint videos + major lessons)
  - Comprehensive audit: slow/detailed approach (rates EVERY lesson, checkpoint, end-of-stage quiz from course start)
  - Gear Checklist (Briefing-stage tool, referenced not defined here)
- **claims**: growth mindset crucial for accurate self-assessment; a "bad" rating signals commitment to improvement, not a negative reflection on past effort; regular audits keep the learning system flexible/effective
- **procedures**: LEEP (4 steps):
  1. Learning flow map — build a flow map reviewing each process step (use Gear Checklist from Briefing); confirm flow includes techniques from all completed stages
  2. Evaluate — check techniques against guidelines (checkpoint videos, Milestone Checklists, Meta-Checklists, stage objectives, End-of-Stage Quizzes); look for rushing/gap issues; rate 1-5 (1=poor, 5=excellent); be objective
  3. Error correction — fix gaps against target metrics/checkpoints; apply with focus on consistency+practice; limit to 1-2 aspects at a time (overload prevention); seek feedback via self-evaluation, Discord community, Office Hours
  4. Perfect practice — use Marginal Gains Tracking + Kolb's Cycle to develop skills; reflect via systematic experimentation with both frameworks; reflection is cyclical (may need multiple experiments, especially after rushing/technique decline); hold growth mindset, gradual approach
- **criteria** (exact):
  - Rating scale: 1 (poor) – 5 (excellent)
  - Recommended audit frequency: every 2-3 months (more often if learning new techniques quickly)
  - Error-correction focus limit: 1-2 aspects at a time
  - Skill-screen threshold: focus on areas scoring <4/5
  - Comprehensive-audit threshold: focus on areas <4/5 OR <5/5 for foundational techniques (stricter bar for foundations)
- **failure-modes / triggers** (4):
  1. After rushing or selective learning (skipping techniques) → introduces mistakes/deficient systems, hard for even experienced coaches to root-cause → audit isolates errors
  2. Resuming after long break → "technique decline" → audit finds weak areas + improvement plan (cross-ref: Live Clinic 48, "Pace yourself")
  3. Progressing to new stages requiring mastery of prior stage (e.g. Fundamentals 2→Briefing, Technique Training→Ascent 1)
  4. Feeling stuck/plateau → audit surfaces subtle technique mistakes
- **tables**: 1 — Skill screen vs Comprehensive audit, 3 rows: review scope, focus, score threshold (as above)
- **relations**: ties Marginal Gains + Kolb's together as the improvement mechanism used AFTER audit identifies gaps (step 4); references Briefing/Ascent 2/Base Camp Final Checkpoints as canonical core-program audit touchpoints; cross-refs Gear Checklist, Live Clinic 48
- **sophistication**: canonical — THE skills-audit procedure (LEEP)

## ics-learning-support/skill-development/stacking-marginal-gains.md
- **stage**: Skill development
- **role**: guide — mechanism explainer with worked good/bad examples + procedure
- **terms**:
  - Stacking: making improvements that ADD VALUE and BUILD ON existing progress; a gain "stacks" if it contributes productively to skill development
  - REDO guidelines: Reflect, Evaluate, Define, Optimise — 4-step process for stacking productively
  - 3 requirements of efficient skill development (Venn diagram): (1) focus on important skills that matter, (2) persist long enough to find/overcome mistakes → habitual competence, (3) think in systems (multiple processes together), not isolated techniques — best marginal gain evolves over time
  - Exponential growth (via stacking, contrasted vs. "additive" gains — cross-ref Fundamentals 1 lesson)
  - New habit formation (stacking gradually opens doors to better techniques/habits without excess effort/concentration)
- **claims**:
  - Stacking fulfills all 3 requirements simultaneously = optimal skill development
  - When all 3 requirements met → exponential growth + new habit formation unlocked
  - Stacking compounds the 1% improvement across attempts, making future improvement progressively easier
  - Without new habits, change requires too much effort/concentration
- **procedures**: REDO (4 steps):
  1. Reflect — what was learned from previous attempts; look for trends across multiple attempts; ask "what might the next 1% look like?"
  2. Evaluate — prioritise direction: unblock (switch skill) vs unlock (more experimentation/theory/reflection on current skill)
  3. Define — identify what a 1% improvement in the chosen direction looks like; note early improvements often = just finding mistakes, before performance gains appear; estimate time-to-results
  4. Optimise — create a specific plan, try it, reflect again (loop to step 1)
- **criteria**: no strict numeric threshold beyond the "1%" framing; implicit failure signal = switching skills too often (see bad example)
- **failure-modes**:
  - "Bad example" — switching between multiple skills too frequently → self-sabotage; insufficient time/reps per skill to make mistakes/reflect/build a 1% gain → weeks/months pass with little progress; explicitly called "a very common behaviour in learners that can self-sabotage progress"
  - Contrast: choosing a marginal gain that does NOT build on existing progress (e.g. BEDSM/procrastination doesn't build note-taking's deep-processing progress) vs one that DOES (Prestudy builds on deep processing)
- **examples**: Rayla (recurring persona), 3 sub-examples on note-taking:

  2. Dissect the goal — what knowledge/attributes/skills/processes/resources are needed (consider: mental health, focus/concentration, time management/prioritisation, studying skills); ask "how sure am I?" to test assumption reliability

  5. Identify the 1% — define the next 1% step toward the target

- **terms**:
  - Rate limiter: the part of a process that caps overall improvement regardless of effort elsewhere (bucket-with-a-hole-in-the-wall analogy) — improving non-limiting parts doesn't raise the ceiling
  - Rate limiters drift over time as skills evolve (this month's limiter may not be next month's)
- **claims**:
  - Even misidentifying the rate limiter, attempting to address ONE is still more effective than not trying
  - Progress without addressing rate limiters wastes significant time/effort (bucket-water-waste analogy)
  - Continued rate-limiter targeting → gradual continued improvement; plateaus resolved by finding + addressing the current limiter
- **procedures**:
  - Identifying rate limiters: reflect every 1-2 weeks using marginal gains + Kolb's, specifically probing "is something holding me back?"; if already addressing it, continue; if a new limiter surfaces, redirect effort to it instead of continuing prior technique
  - Overall progression (6 steps): 1) learn new methods/principles in lessons, 2) practise/apply in real life, 3) reflect on effectiveness via marginal gains + Kolb's, 4) identify mistakes/barriers, 5) prioritise barriers by how rate-limiting they are, 6) work on higher-priority items, repeating iteratively
