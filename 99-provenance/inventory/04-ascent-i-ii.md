## ics-course/03-growth/03-ascent-i/02-how-to-aim.md
- **stage**: Growth phase, Ascent I (Aim step), file 2/9 — first substantive Aim lesson.
- **role**: lesson — defines Aim technique + FAQ (Common questions) section.
- **terms**:
  - Aiming — "pointing your brain in the right direction" via the two core questions to force specific relational thinking patterns.
  - "Why is this important?" — core Aim question; drives Evaluate-level thinking, prioritization, memorability.
  - "How is this related to ___?" — core Aim question; surfaces non-obvious relationships, counters bias/preconceptions, can force re-evaluation of whole chunk structure.
- **claims**:
  - Asking the right questions makes the brain form specific relational patterns/networks.
  - "Why is it important" pushes to Evaluate-level thinking (Bloom's-like ordering implied) → improves memorability/intuitiveness of chunks.
  - "How is it related" escapes existing bias/preconceptions that "why" alone can't reach; can trigger re-evaluation of the whole chunk structure.
  - Two questions are conceptually distinct despite surface similarity — arrived at "after years of trial and observation" (no citation).
- **procedures**: Aim's purpose (3 items): (1) make new info relevant, form networks immediately; (2) activate higher-order learning as early as possible; (3) create chunks/relationships that are robust, versatile, suited to retrieval needs.
- **criteria**: no fixed time given for full Aim; brief prestudy (5–10 min) → only superficial Aim (largest chunks/major relationships only); comprehensive prestudy → full Aim + more detail possible.
- **relations**: Aim completely replaces TLS red light (explicit, in FAQ). Aim vs prestudy: prestudy = *when* you learn (timing), BHS = *how* you learn (method); they often overlap but aren't the same. Aim should be the first step of any learning journey; if no prestudy, Aim still goes first (ideally), though intensive lectures may force it to self-study time instead ("not optimal"). Shoot step (later) can also be folded into a more comprehensive prestudy.

## ics-course/03-growth/03-ascent-i/03-aim-question-quality.md
- **stage**: Growth phase, Ascent I (Aim step), file 3/9 — deepens question-quality standard.
- **role**: lesson (short) + checkpoint quiz (rendered-state capture only).
- **terms**:
  - Simple question and answer — failure mode: answering with a single, simple linear answer, avoiding deeper processing.
- **claims**:
  - "What" questions → too simple/unconnected → low retention. "Why"/"How" questions connect info → more meaningful/retained.
  - Emotional/personal/curiosity-sparking questions connect more strongly than neutral ones.
  - Genuinely curious questions (not ones you already know the answer to) generate more knowledge — problem-solving mindset requirement.
  - "Why is X important?" ranked as one of the highest-yield questions: forces both examining a concept in relation to others AND judging relationship importance (higher-order learning).
  - Answering "why important" with only ONE relationship diminishes the question's value — the point is multiple relationships + judgment across them.
  - "Examples of X" questions are usually NOT a sufficient substitute for "why is it important" — examples tend to be lower-order and don't build cross-concept similarity/grouping.
  - Relationship-probing questions ("how does X affect Y," "how are X/Y different") build relationships too, but weaker than "why important" — limited to 2–3 concepts vs. "why important" which invokes multi-concept big-picture thinking.
  - "What is the purpose of X" ≈ reworded "why is X important."
  - "How does X occur?" ≈ "what is the process?" — does NOT contextualize against other concepts; risks becoming pure process memorisation.
- **criteria**: quiz-implied ranking of question yield: why-important (highest) > relationship/dependency questions (medium) > "how does X occur"/process questions (low, ≈"what").
- **failure-modes**: simple question and answer (single literal answer, closes off multi-relational evaluation) — named/defined here for the first time in the corpus.
- **examples**: quiz scenario — friend's Aim questions on "metacognition" using only "what is X / what are examples of X" pattern; critique: examples ≠ sufficient importance-reasoning.
- **sophistication**: canonical treatment of question-quality standard (definitional, referenced later without redefinition).
- **scaffold**: interactive QUIZ page captured only as rendered state (score snapshot 83%, 5/6) — quiz mechanics themselves are scaffolding/assessment, not new instructional content beyond the "Learning notes" blocks under each question.

## ics-course/03-growth/03-ascent-i/04-inquiry-clean-up.md
- **stage**: Growth phase, Ascent I (Aim step), file 4/9 — core deep-dive lesson (largest/most substantive Ascent I lesson).
- **role**: lesson — introduces and fully walks through importance-based chunking with a worked medical example (retinal-detachment-adjacent, generic keyword list) plus per-subject chunking-pattern reference.
- **terms**:
  - Importance-based chunking — chunking method centred on judging *importance* of each keyword/concept; explicitly named "core pillar of our program."
  - Order control — (referenced, not redefined here) exercising independence from presented/textbook order when forming groups.
  - Spiderwebbing — chaotic/messy web of relationships across a map; occurs when many lateral relationships are added without re-chunking; "if left at this stage, we will feel overwhelmed."
  - Inter-chunk relationships — relationships between (sub-)chunks themselves, distinct from within-chunk relationships; clean inter-chunk relationships → a clean "backbone."
  - Backbone — the simple, clean structure formed by clean inter-chunk relationships; ability to do this = key beginner/intermediate learner differentiator.
  - Active management of cognitive load — deliberately raising cognitive load via higher-order thinking, then offloading via chunking/non-linear notes.
- **claims**:
  - Higher-order ("why"/"how related") questions automatically produce lower-order learning as a side-effect — so "what" questions become unnecessary once higher-order questions are used.
  - Not knowing "how to think about" a subject forces the brain to solve 7 listed sub-problems simultaneously (what does this mean / why know it / how to use it / is it relevant / must I memorise details / how to solve problems with it / how does it fit with everything else) → reduced focus, more time/energy for less learning.
  - Poorly organised information → low retention (causal claim, unqualified).
  - Logical frameworks/patterns (e.g., "cause and effect") repeat across domains and are learned early in life (child-throwing-object analogy) — this is why "why important" reliably surfaces reusable structures.
- **procedures**: Importance-based chunking instructions (6 steps): (1) ask "why is this keyword/concept important?"; (2) find possible answers (usually multiple reasons); (3) move to next keyword in any order, repeat; (4) notice similarities across "why important" answers → potential chunks; (5) judge which relationships/similarities are worth keeping/using to form chunks; (6) repeat for all concepts, continually consolidating/mapping as you go (process-and-consolidate-as-you-go, don't defer).
  - Visualising-the-process worked walkthrough (matches keyword-list example): keyword list (unheaded, to avoid bias) → first relationships/chunks (a few keywords, general sense of grouping) → non-linear flow draft (hypothesis only, don't commit) → evaluate further relationships (may need outside research) → spiderwebbing appears (chaotic yellow+pink new relationships) → revise chunk structure around identified high-connection node (e.g., "Types") → cleaner final map.
- **criteria**: none quantitative beyond "usually multiple reasons" for importance; no explicit chunk-count rule here (see mind-map file for 3–7 rule).
- **failure-modes**: spiderwebbing (defined above) — remedy: identify high-connection nodes and re-chunk around them.
- **examples**: generic "keyword list" worked example (biology/medical-flavoured, retinal-detachment-adjacent) — 4 stages: keyword list → relationships drawn → non-linear flow map → spiderwebbed map → revised/clean map. Per-subject "common patterns of importance" reference table (not a worked example per se): Biology (function, structure-vs-function, scale, cause-group→effect-group; e.g. chunk organelles by function not location), Chemistry (mechanisms, real-world application, electronegativity, common reagents/products; e.g. chunk reactions by mechanism), Physics (real-world application, conceptual precursors; e.g. chunk by application not textbook order), Maths (real-world application, conceptual precursors; e.g. chunk equations by graph-effect), English (author/director background+values, story significance; e.g. chunk themes/techniques by author values), History (movements, cause-and-effect, motivations of key players; noted as *most diverse* range of chunking structures; e.g. chunk events by motivating force not geography), Law (social-justice principles, pragmatic/compensatory mechanisms, landmark cases, protected principles; e.g. chunk cases/bills by underlying legal principle), Languages (common usage, situational usage, effect on communication; noted as *hardest* to chunk since language isn't inherently logical; e.g. chunk words/phrases by emotion/use-case). ## ics-course/03-growth/03-ascent-i/09-ascent-i-check.md
- **stage**: Growth phase, Ascent I (Aim step), file 9/9 — stage checkpoint / capstone.
- **role**: checkpoint quiz + competency table (progression gate for Ascent I → Ascent II).
- **terms** (failure-mode/pattern names, DEFINED here via quiz learning-notes):
  - Waterfalling — "top-down" mapping pattern, very little connectivity between ideas on the same hierarchy layer. Caused by inquiry that is too superficial, not exploring multiple relationships → weak order control/independent chunking. Remedy: frequently reconsider inter-concept AND inter-chunk relationships, even after chunks are formed.
  - Spiderwebbing — messy map from many chaotic/chunky-crossing lines. Can be a NORMAL, acceptable intermediate stage of mapping (not itself an error) — refined in later iterations. Caused by items placed in suboptimal chunks without considering relation to other map concepts (not caused by lack of prestudy or lack of anchoring visuals — those are quiz distractors, explicitly not the cause).
  - Lonely chunks — chunk(s) created with no/sparse content ("storage box with nothing inside"), OR multiple disconnected mini-maps. Caused by prechunking based on framing bias, uncorrected by inquiry. Acceptable transiently as hypothesis but must be corrected as study continues; if kept, adds cognitive load without benefit.
  - Lower-order inquiry — questions about isolated facts/processes/single two-concept relationships (not "why important"). Caused by prior habits of isolated thinking, not yet replaced by relationship/grouping habits (explicitly NOT caused by time pressure or terminology difficulty — quiz distractors).
  - Simple question and answer — writing literal/brief answers that close off multi-relational, higher-order evaluation. Caused by learners not understanding that the VALUE of questioning is the higher-order thinking it invokes (not caused by inherent question simplicity — distractor).

- **terms**:
  - VPReFRE — 6-component note-taking quality checklist: Visual, Processed, Relational, Freehand, Reflective, Efficient. "If your mind map is done correctly, it will satisfy this checklist."
  - Visual (V) — reduce words, increase pictures/symbols/doodles/shorthand/diagrams; aids retention via multimodal encoding + acts as memory landmarks.

- **claims**:
  - Note-taking METHOD matters less than the THINKING PROCESS used while taking notes (stated as key principle, repeated across sublessons).

## ics-course/03-growth/04-ascent-ii/05-layers-of-learning.md
- **stage**: Growth phase, Ascent II (Shoot step), file 5/7 — core structural-framework lesson.
- **role**: lesson (core pillar concept) + checkpoint quiz.
- **terms**:
  - Layers of learning — structural framework for sequencing what to learn; explicitly named "one of our program's core pillar concepts."
  - Logic layer (layer 1, innermost) — the "backbone": biggest concepts/chunks/most important relationships, built via "why important"/"how does it relate" questions.

- **claims**:
  - People often skip the logic layer entirely — likened to building a house while neglecting the foundation.
  - Students who skip layer 1 struggle with layer 2 (concepts) — causal/sequential dependency claim.
  - Layers are NOT static — how "relevant" a detail seems depends on whether layers 1–2 give it structure; poor logic/concepts layers → MORE details appear "arbitrary" than actually are. Fixing layers 1–2 is the lever for reducing perceived layer-4 volume (explicit remedy, not just observation).
  - Starting with deep foundational (logic/concept) info dramatically increases overall study speed by converting would-be-arbitrary details into relevant/connected details.
  - Order control (from earlier stages) tells you WHICH order to consume info in; layers of learning tells you HOW to structure/organise it once reordered — explicit differentiation between the two related-but-distinct concepts.
  - "Overinvest" guidance: best to overinvest time in layers 1–2 since this dramatically reduces total time needed for layers 3–4, where MOST time is otherwise wasted.
- **criteria**: quantitative content-volume breakdown — of total information volume: ~10% conceptual (layer 2), ~20% important details (layer 3), remaining ~70% arbitrary details (layer 4). Explicit consequence: studying start-to-finish without layering → most study time spent on the 70% arbitrary/unconnected portion → poor retention.
