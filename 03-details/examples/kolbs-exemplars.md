## Dissection quiz: Jeremiah (outcome-as-experience)

Jeremiah's Kolb's "experience" section: *"I received 75% on my last maths test which is much less
than I had expected."*

This is the textbook case of using an outcome as an experience. Because outcomes are the result of
multiple processes, Kolb's reflection should be focused on the *processes* that led to the
outcome, not the outcome itself — otherwise it becomes difficult to identify what could be done
differently. Outcome-as-experience often leads to frustration: substantial time can be spent
reflecting without any meaningful insight emerging. The experience section of Kolb's should always
be brief and concise; not every experience needs to be the experiment carried over from a previous
cycle — new, unconnected experiences can also be reflected on, and if an experiment is designed
for a new experience, it feeds into the next cycle until sufficiently resolved.

## Dissection quiz: Zhao (brief/superficial reflection → theoretical abstraction)

Zhao's full cycle, submitted and later dissected in a checkpoint quiz:

> **Experience**: I tried non-linear note-taking during lectures and found it overwhelming and
> challenging.
>
> **Reflection**: I started by opening my note-taking app on my tablet and creating a new page.
> Then I jotted down keywords the lecturer said or that I saw on the slides. As they spoke I tried
> to think of relationships between the keywords. During this experience, I felt very overwhelmed
> and stressed. It was difficult for me to keep up with the content and I struggled to get
> everything down. I felt this throughout the whole process of trying to take notes. I responded
> by just typing it out instead when I felt like I was falling behind too much. Triggers were when
> I felt overwhelmed.
>
> **Abstraction**: I struggle to write notes when the lecturer is talking because the content is
> too fast and it is too overwhelming to think of all the relationships at the same time. This is
> mainly because there is too much text on the slides and the lecturer speaks too quickly so it's
> impossible for me to keep up.
>
> **Experiment**: Write notes normally by typing during lectures and process it non-linearly after
> class.

Zhao spent almost two hours on this cycle. Diagnosis: the reflection is far too superficial and
brief — there is almost nothing beyond "it felt overwhelming," with no detail on *when* it felt
overwhelming or what metacognitive processes were used to try to regulate it. This makes it very
difficult to find trends or habits behind the problem; the only conclusion supportable from the
reflection is that some part of the process feels overwhelming. Instead of noticing that the
reflection itself was too thin and going back to it, Zhao reasoned from his own ideas directly to
an abstraction — making the abstraction theoretical (unsupported by the reflection) and the root
cause misdiagnosed (changes to cognitive process or prestudy routine, which would have addressed
the real bottleneck, are never considered). The resulting experiment is specific and actionable
given how Zhao perceived the problem, but will not fundamentally help, because the abstraction it
is based on misdiagnosed the root cause. As long as this outcome is fed into the next cycle, the
limitation should surface and self-correct over further iterations.

This example is also the source of the 30-minute session cap: if a full cycle is taking close to
two hours and the learner is struggling specifically at the reflection stage, this indicates
limited self-awareness or reflective technique — normal in the early stages of learning the
skill. The recommendation is to cap sessions at around 30 minutes, even if this means not reaching
the abstraction or experiment stage on a given day; self-awareness and reflection speed improve
naturally with repeated sessions, after which the remaining stages become reachable within the
same time cap.

## Dissection quiz: Lilly (experiment overload)

Lilly, after completing the High-Yield phase, identifies 5 new techniques she wants to integrate
and sets herself the task of using all of them, as accurately as possible, every day. After a
month of diligent effort her consistency remains poor and each experiment simply reveals more
mistakes; she understands each technique with enough clarity to execute it, but nuances that
appear only in practice make application harder than expected.

Diagnosis: her theoretical understanding is good enough that she is right to be practising and
evaluating with Kolb's, and finding mistakes at this stage is itself a sign of marginal gains at
the conscious-incompetence level — but she is developing very slowly because she is running 5 new
techniques simultaneously, which is experiment overload (see the 2–3-technique parallel cap,
../../02-concepts/skill-acquisition-model.md). Even though a month can be enough time to gain
conscious competence with High-Yield-phase techniques individually, experiment overload
significantly slows the pace regardless of technique difficulty. There is insufficient
information in this case to also conclude she is limited by reflection or abstraction quality,
though that remains possible.

## LC23: law-student 3-part cycle (problem → cause → experiment)

A law student, just past Briefing → Common Traps, reports a Kolb's cycle done on their own
prestudy mindmap and visual-representation practice, structured as three separate reflected
problems each carried through to its own cause and experiment:

1. **Unclear relationships.** Reflection identified unclear relationships in the mindmap.
   Reported as substantially improved in the current attempt (though partly attributed to the
   current topic simply having easier-to-represent relationships than before).

2. **Absence of visual representation for abstract concepts.** Abstraction: a tendency to fixate
   on finding highly directly-relevant drawings, which is nearly impossible for abstract ideas,
   at which point the learner defaults back to writing words instead. Experiment: allow the use of
   *less* directly-relevant images, and use image search to generate ideas when stuck. Reported
   outcome: partially successful — worked for some concepts, not for others (image search tended
   to surface pictures of words rather than usable visual metaphors for abstract ideas at the
   bottom of the mindmap).

3. **Too much time spent drawing.** Abstraction: a desire for the work to look neat and
   aesthetically pleasing, which becomes hard to stop once started (example given: drawing one
   perfectly straight arrow with a tool creates a felt obligation for the rest of the mindmap to
   match that standard, which is time-consuming). Experiment: deliberately make the work "uglier
   right off the bat," so the path toward ultra-neatness is never started. Reported outcome:
   "definitely worked."

This is a fully self-contained worked instance of the cycle's actual internal reasoning — problem
identified, root cause abstracted, and a specific experiment run per problem — captured without
needing the instructor's response to be legible as a complete example.

**Corroboration.** An unrelated learner (Live Clinic 28) reports the identical fix — "make it
uglier right off the bat" — for the same ultra-neatness/perfectionism time-sink, suggesting this
is a validated, reusable remedy rather than a one-off tip specific to the law student's case.

## Checkpoint worked example: studying-over-learning + brief reflection

The Technique Training checkpoint quiz includes a real student Kolb's submission
(`Kolbs-red-flags.jpg`) used to test recognition of two co-occurring failure patterns:
**studying over learning** (measuring success by volume — hours studied, pages covered — instead
of meaningful metrics like retention or depth/mastery per unit time; caused by time pressure
making it easier to revert to familiar habits, and by not being aware of the multiple factors that
influence learning efficiency, rather than by laziness or peer pressure) and **brief reflection**
(minimal-content Kolb's reflection, diagnostically signalled by single-sentence or few-bullet
answers to each prompting question).
