example states specifically what it demonstrates.

## Example 1 — Ascent I: keyword list → relationships → flow → spiderweb → clean map

**Demonstrates:** the basic six-step importance-based chunking procedure end to end, and the
transition from an acceptable spiderwebbed intermediate state to a re-chunked clean map (see
Spiderwebbing in encoding-failure-modes.md). Source topic is medical/biology-flavoured
(retinal-detachment-adjacent), presented as a generic keyword list.

**Stage 1 — Keyword list.** The sequence begins with a plain, unheaded, ungrouped list of
keywords, deliberately given no headings or groupings, so that groups are formed independently
(exercising order control) rather than inherited from a source's presentation.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/Keyword list.jpeg`

**Stage 2 — Finding first relationships and possible chunks.** Starting from a keyword that
intuitively feels relevant, "why is this important" and "how might it relate to the other
keywords" are asked repeatedly across a few keywords to get a general sense of grouping. Each
yellow link in the figure marks a potential relationship; the rationale for each relationship is
written explicitly rather than just drawing a line, to keep the nature of each relationship
clear. At this point the drawn relationships closely mirror how the same content would be
presented in a textbook — not yet shown to be a problem, but also not yet shown to be optimal.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/Relationships of keywords.jpeg`

**Stage 3 — Representing the flow non-linearly (hypothesis, not commitment).** The keywords are
rearranged into a first non-linear flow hypothesis, purely to visualise the pattern and offload
cognitive load — not to commit to a final structure. Whether to draft this hypothetical flow
early or late depends on how comfortable the learner is managing cognitive load purely in list
form; in doubt, draft earlier rather than later.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/Mindmap flow 1.jpeg`

**Stage 4 — Evaluating further relationships (spiderwebbing appears).** Continuing the inquiry
process — asking how concepts relate, why they're important, and researching where needed —
surfaces new relationships (marked in yellow) and even new keywords not on the original list
(marked in pink, judged important/relevant enough at the big-picture level to include). The
result is **spiderwebbing**: many additional relationships that are difficult to arrange, which
would leave the learner feeling overwhelmed if left at this stage. This is presented explicitly
as an acceptable, expected intermediate state, not a mistake to have avoided.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/Mindmap flow 2.jpeg`

Cognitive-load mechanism accompanying this stage: mental effort (cognitive load) is deliberately
raised through higher-order engagement, then offloaded through chunking and non-linear notes —
the corpus's general cognitive-load-management cycle, illustrated alongside this example.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/Active management of cognitive load.jpeg`

**Stage 5 — Revising the chunk structure (re-chunking around a high-connection node).** Reviewing
the spiderwebbed map shows many connections clustering around the "Types" chunk (bottom left),
alongside many chunks still branching from the central "retinal detachment" node. Hypothesising a
new structure that uses "Types" as a more central part of the flow — rather than the original
topic name — resolves the tangle.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/Mindmap flow 3.jpeg`

The resulting map is simpler and more intuitive while showing the *same* important relationships
as the spiderwebbed version — the improvement comes from better chunking, not from adding or
removing lines. As deeper detail is added in further passes, the chunk structure may need to be
adjusted again to accommodate it.

## Example 2 — Ascent III: India / British colonisation (2-4 Rule sequence)

**Demonstrates:** the full 2-4 Rule (chunking-rules.md) across four iterations — sub-chunking at
≥4 branches, concatenating a single-node chain, dynamically reintroducing a previously-merged
chunk, and concatenating a terminal node with an added memory-anchor visualisation.

**Iteration 1 — more than four branches.** The "Consequences in India" chunk has six points
branching directly off it, violating the 2-4 Rule's upper bound.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/India chunking example 1.jpeg`

**Iteration 1 fix — sub-chunking.** The six points are re-chunked into two sub-chunks of three
points each. Re-chunking this way also surfaces a new lateral relationship between the two new
sub-chunks that would otherwise have contributed to spiderwebbing if left as six loose branches.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/India chunking example 2.jpeg`

**Iteration 2 — fewer than two concepts (single-node chain).** "British colonisation" sits
between "Drivers" and "Consequences" with only one inbound and one outbound connection — a
single-node chain that clutters the map without adding organisational value.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/India chunking example 3.jpeg`

**Iteration 2 fix — concatenation.** "British colonisation" is removed from the flow entirely,
connecting "Drivers" directly to "Consequences." This is judged safe because the whole topic is
already about British colonisation — nothing is lost by not stating it explicitly, since
"drivers" and "consequences" are unambiguously drivers/consequences *of* British colonisation.
"Economic gain" is simultaneously re-chunked to sit under "Drivers" directly, making the
Drivers → Consequences relationship more straightforward.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/India chunking example 4.jpeg`

**Iteration 3 — dynamic reintroduction.** "Economic gain" as a standalone sub-chunk is
reintroduced once political and religious drivers are also identified — without reintroduction,
"Drivers" would now have three branches feeding it directly and risk exceeding the rule again as
more drivers are found. This demonstrates the rule is applied dynamically in both directions
(merge and re-split), not as a one-way simplification pass.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/India chunking example 5.jpeg`

**Iteration 4 — single-node chain ending in a terminal node.** "Quality of life" leads to
"Famine," which leads to "Malnourishment." "Famine" has exactly one inbound and one outbound
connection (a single-node chain); "Malnourishment" has no further outbound connections at all,
making it a terminal node.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/India chunking example 6.jpeg`

**Iteration 4 fix — concatenation with memory anchor.** "Malnourishment" is added directly under
"Famine," removing the single-node chain and its terminal node. "Poverty" and "working
conditions" are moved to give the merged node more space, and a visualisation (rotting meat) is
added at the concatenation point as a memory anchor for "malnourishment," compensating for the
detail no longer having its own dedicated map position.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/India chunking example 7.jpeg`

Overall claim carried by this sequence: continual chunking, sub-chunking, and re-chunking to find
the simplest available structure frees up cognitive and physical map space to explore additional
relationships, producing a map that looks simpler while representing more nuanced, deeply
organised understanding underneath.

## Example 3 — Ascent II: epithelial cell types (importance vs. textbook-order chunking)

**Demonstrates:** the difference between chunking directly off a source's own hierarchy
(producing mild spiderwebbing) and chunking by the root/immediate-reason standard (see Root
reason vs immediate reason in importance-based-chunking.md), applied to a cell-biology source
excerpt.

**Source material.** A sample textbook excerpt presents epithelial cell types (squamous,
cuboidal, columnar) and cell arrangements (stratified, simple, pseudostratified) as two separate
lists, then presents four "important epithelial cell linings" (e.g. stratified squamous
epithelium, simple cuboidal epithelium) as named combinations of a type and an arrangement, each
with its own function.

**Initial attempt — chunked by the source's own categories.** Chunking directly into "types" vs.
"arrangements" (mirroring how the excerpt itself is organised) produces a map with slight
spiderwebbing — multiple relationships between multiple concepts that look messy and hard to
follow, because the four "important linings" each have to be cross-referenced back against both
parent lists separately.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/image-1.png` ("red mind map")

**Re-chunked attempt — chunked by importance.** Asking why cell types and arrangements are
important reveals that the four "important linings" are simply the meaningful combinations of
type × arrangement — so the map is rebuilt chunked by **function** instead (why each lining
matters), rather than by the source's type/arrangement split.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/image_0.png` ("blue mind map with pictures")

The re-chunked version is described as clearer, easier to follow, and more intuitive. This
example is used to state the general principle that the first attempt at a chunk structure is
almost never the simplest available one, and that in this particular case function happened to be
the better organising principle — but the corpus is explicit that function is not universally the
right basis (e.g. chemistry's importance is usually reaction-mechanism/reactivity rather than
function; English's importance ties to author perspective) — see
subject-importance-patterns.md.

## Example 4 — Ascent I: chunking for medicine (retinal detachment — framing bias, waterfalling, spiderwebbing)

**Demonstrates:** framing bias as the root cause, with waterfalling and prechunking risk in
iteration 1, spiderwebbing in iteration 2, and full resolution via re-chunking around a
high-connection node in iteration 3. This is the corpus's single most cross-referenced worked
example: it is explicitly named as the canonical illustration for framing bias in its own lesson,
and is cross-referenced by name from two later checkpoint quizzes — the Ascent I checkpoint's
"Out Comes The Rain" spiderwebbing item ("an example of this is given in the *chunking for
medicine* sublesson") and the Ascent II checkpoint's "Baking Without Ingredients" prechunking item
("this problem was alluded to in the *chunking for medicine* sublesson in Ascent 1's *Aim
examples* lesson").

**Framing bias framing.** Framing bias is the general cognitive bias that conclusions are shaped
by how options are presented; in learning, it commonly makes chunks mirror how teachers,
resources, or lectures already structure the material, rather than being independently,
cognitively optimal. Many learners conclude "there is no other way to chunk this" simply because
the given presentation seems logical — the corpus states this conclusion is usually false, and
that finding a better structure is the learner's job, not a matter of accepting the given one.

**Iteration 1 — the common, obvious chunking.** At first glance this structure looks organised,
but everything funnels through the single topic node "Retinal detachment," with no lateral
relationships between ideas at the same level — waterfalling. Many other diseases produce an
almost identical structure by default, which is exactly the framing-bias signature: the resulting
information is forgettable, and the learner ends up having to memorise every terminal point
individually rather than deriving them from structure.

Source-referenced figure filename: `Mindmap flow 1_0.jpeg`. *(Not present in the 102-file
`research/_assets/inline-images/` asset set at capture time — see note below.)*

**Iteration 2 — lateral relationships added, spiderwebbing appears.** Adding relationships
*between* ideas (rather than only down from the topic node) surfaces a whole new chunk that had
been missed on the first pass (marked in pink). The result is disorganised: left here, this is
spiderwebbing. Evaluating the new connections shows the "Types" chunk has an unusually high
density of connections — a candidate node to re-chunk around.

Source-referenced figure filename: `Mindmap flow 2_0.jpeg`. *(Not present in the asset set; see
note below.)*

**Iteration 3 — re-chunked, resolved.** The entire topic is re-chunked around "Types" instead of
around the original topic name. Both waterfalling and spiderwebbing are resolved simultaneously,
and the map represents the topic in a more unique, more memorable way. Critically, the same
relationships identified in iteration 2 are still present in iteration 3 — nothing found during
the spiderwebbed stage is lost — but they are now expressed through the organisational structure
itself (chunk placement) rather than through extra explicit lines, which is what makes the final
version both cleaner and more memorable at once.

Source-referenced figure filename: `Mindmap flow 3_0.jpeg`. *(Not present in the asset set; see
note below.)*
