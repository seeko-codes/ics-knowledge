## Statement

Live Clinics are where students submit real, in-progress mindmaps and Aim/chunking work for
critique. Across this file range, every clinic follows the same capture pattern: the student
submissions themselves are captured in full as a timestamped log, but Justin's spoken responses
exist only in uncaptured, DRM-protected video. Every item below is therefore either (a) a
reconstruction from a clinic's chapter-timestamp titles, honestly marked as unexplained, or (b) a
verbatim or near-verbatim student submission, which is genuine, complete corpus content in its
own right even without the accompanying answer. Nothing below should be read as reporting what
Justin said in response unless explicitly stated as captured.

## LC49 — generation-effect critique walkthrough (video-gapped)

Live Clinic 49 features a coach and medical student (Derrick) reviewing his own mindmap live with
Justin, in his clinical years. The clinic's chapter-timestamp index describes a structured
critique sequence, used here as the corpus's clearest *named* instance of the generation
effect and hypercorrection effect being used deliberately as an in-session teaching device:

- Derrick presents a self-assessment of his own mindmap.
- The audience/Derrick is prompted to guess what the issue is with the chunk structure before
  the answer is revealed — the chapter title itself notes: "engage in that generation effect!
  Don't worry if you get it wrong, you'll benefit from the hypercorrection effect too!"
- The answer is then revealed, followed by an explanation of **framing bias** in this specific
  case.
- The problem of **repetitive chunks**, and how repetitive chunks violate the **2-4 Rule**, is
  discussed, along with how to start moving away from repetitive chunking.
- The clinic explicitly notes that, despite the critique, the mindmap under discussion was still
  considered "intermediate" level going in.
- Later in the same clinic: how **pattern banks** help experts learn faster and how to develop
  relevant chunk structure; managing overwhelm from a large volume of content by working ahead
  rather than only focusing on the next immediate day.

**What is and is not captured.** The chapter titles above are the full extent of what is captured
for this walkthrough — the actual flawed mindmap, the specific framing-bias explanation given for
it, and the content of the "answer reveal" are not present in text anywhere in the corpus. This
entry preserves the *shape* of the critique (self-assessment → guess → reveal → framing-bias
explanation → repetitive-chunks/2-4-Rule explanation) as a documented pedagogical pattern, without
being able to reproduce its content.

## LC30 — student submissions, AIM-SHOOT pipeline, and the 3-color revision scheme

Live Clinic 30 is the single richest submission-log file found for chunking/mapping critique: 42
distinct, often lengthy student submissions, all self-contained text (mindmap descriptions,
self-evaluations, and direct questions), none with a captured answer.

### Representative submissions with named failure modes

- A student mid-Ascent-1 shares a full-book maths mindmap, self-diagnosing: "I like the
  backbone... There is a good amount of relationship between chunks and concepts, not a lot of
  waterfalling. I tried to maintain the main structure as much as possible to avoid a cracked
  glass pattern, something that went quite well, although there are still some parts with that
  pattern." They separately note that most of their inter-relationships were "found by sheer luck
  while searching for information" rather than through deliberate inquiry, and that layers 4–5
  of the map "all look the same" and are individually forgettable.
- A student explicitly asks, self-referentially, whether discomfort during Inquiry-Based
  Learning is itself proof of correct processing: "if I'm not feeling uncomfortable, cognitive
  load is not high enough, and I won't be learning properly" — offered as their own inference
  from course material, for confirmation (unresolved in captured text).
- A calculus student reports inconsistency in which details they chose to strip from a mind map
  after feedback (some parts keep formulas/processes/expressions, others don't), and difficulty
  converting mathematical expressions into visuals — both unresolved.
- A student states directly: "Until this LC I did hierarchy purposely to avoid spiderwebbing" —
  tagged `#antispiderwebbing #chunking`.
- A cluster of questions asks about **segmented mind maps**: whether the issue is worse for
  larger topics; whether it is the same thing as "mixing layers" (not finishing one layer before
  starting the next); whether prestudy scope should match final-mindmap scope to prevent it; and
  whether a segmented map should be restarted from scratch rather than extended.
- A student reports on **transitional Kolb's** — running a full week's Kolb's cycles anchored to
  one chosen marginal gain, then reassessing at week's end — and separately tags a related,
  distinct construct, **margin stacking** (undefined in text), while worrying the approach may
  cause them to neglect unrelated issues that surface mid-week.
- A student asks directly: "Why isn't the list of mistakes such as cracked glass and waterfalling
  not made public?" — the clearest evidence in the corpus that this vocabulary is understood,
  inside the program, to live in Live Clinics rather than in the public/written curriculum (see
  encoding-failure-modes.md, Statement).
- A statistics student (about two weeks of material mapped) self-diagnoses their own mindmap as
  having "a stick figurey vibe" and asks directly whether that is the same thing as a "cracked
  glass appearance" — see encoding-failure-modes.md, Cracked glass.
- A student reports approximately 90% one-week retention on an infertility-causes mindmap, judges
  their own logic backbone solid, but flags a lack of lateral connectivity between chunks as the
  likely ceiling on further improvement.

### The AIM-SHOOT six-step pipeline (student-proposed, verbatim)

A student mid-Ascent-2 proposes a full pipeline and asks Justin to validate it (answer not
captured). It is the most complete explicit statement of the Aim→Shoot→backbone→structure→final-
mindmap sequence found anywhere in this file range:

1. Organize keywords into topics of the textbook.
2. AIM questions.
3. SHOOT — answer questions and importance-base chunk.
4. AIM (how chunks are related) → SHOOT relationship between chunks = Mindmap Backbone.
5. AIM-SHOOT between sub-chunks and other keywords = Mindmap structure.
6. AIM-SHOOT all important details or the rest of the keywords = Final Mindmap.

Appended notes from the same submission: "Check Mindmap with VPReFRE"; "AIM = why is it important
or how relation questions"; constant swapping between AIM and SHOOT; the pipeline is explicitly
tied to Layers of Learning and Order Control throughout. The student also states their own
subject-specific application: for maths, importance-based chunks follow applications, and
important details are the equations that support understanding those concepts; for English,
importance-based chunks follow the themes the author intends, and important details are the
techniques used to develop those themes.

### The three-colour revision self-check (student-proposed)

A separate submission in the same clinic proposes a colour-coded self-check procedure for
revision, used to visually separate what is known from what is not:

1. Draw everything remembered, in **black**.
2. Draw everything forgotten (compared against the original mindmap), in **purple**.
3. Draw the rest — material not understood even when looking directly at the original — in
   **red**.

This scheme recurs, near-identically, in Live Clinic 26: a different student independently
reports "I first draw in black everything I can remember. Then in purple everything I couldn't
remember based on the original mind map I did while reading and, finally, in red I draw the rest
that represents the part of the original mind map that I couldn't understand (not even looking)."
The close match across two independent submissions is treated here as corroboration that this is
a stable, community/coach-shared practice rather than a one-off individual invention — though
neither submission's own question ("is this heading in the right direction, or a waste of time?")
has a captured answer.

## LC28 / LC23 — the ultra-neatness trap and its fix

Both clinics independently document the same failure pattern and the same self-applied fix,
providing rare cross-clinic corroboration of a fully-captured (non-video-gapped) remedy.

**LC23 (law student, prestudy mindmap).** A law student runs a three-part Kolb's self-critique on
their own mindmap: (1) unclear relationships, (2) absence of visual representation for
particularly abstract concepts, (3) too much time spent drawing. For (3), their own abstraction
is: "I tend to want my work to look neat and aesthetically pleasing and that once I start on this
path, it becomes very difficult to stop... if I am drawing a mindmap and I use the arrow tool to
draw a perfectly straight arrow, I will feel like it's a waste if the rest of my mindmap is less
neat." Their self-applied experiment: "just make it uglier right off the bat (so don't even begin
down that path of ultra-neatness)" — reported as having "definitely worked."

**LC28 (self-diagnosed independently).** A different student, in a different clinic, names the
identical pattern as "ultra-neatness" and reports the identical remedy already applied and
successful: "make it uglier right off the bat" is captured as a rare, fully-resolved micro-fix in
a file range where almost no remedies survive capture.

This file range treats the recurrence across two independent students and two separate clinics as
strong corroboration that "make it uglier first" is a validated, reusable fix for
perfectionism-driven mapping slowdown, not an idiosyncratic one-off tip.

## AST/ALT overlap edge case (LC29)

A student in Live Clinic 29 documents hitting a genuine limit of chunking-by-clinical-similarity
in medicine: viruses that elevate AST/ALT (liver enzymes) are numerous enough, and similar enough
in the non-specific findings they produce, that ordinary differentiation-based chunking
strategies fail outright: "I hit a brick wall when I was trying to study viruses that cause
elevated AST/ALT. Because it turns out SO many viruses CAN do this. They can seemingly all
generate the same set of non-specific findings. The only difference is how 'often' this happens,
which is very hard for me to express on a map." The student reports that both geographic chunking
and rash-presence chunking failed to help, since "they are all capable, and absence means nothing
most of the time." No resolution is captured. This is documented as a distinct, harder edge case
than the general medicine algorithm-rigidity bind in subject-importance-patterns.md: there, the
source material's *external structure* resists re-chunking; here, the *underlying content itself*
lacks sufficient differentiating structure for any chunking scheme (geographic, symptomatic, or
otherwise) to organise around.
