All examples in this file are student clinic submissions; in every case, the ICS response exists
only in DRM-locked video and is not captured in text. These are documented as student attempts
(successful or unresolved) rather than validated worked examples.

## LC22 — Homology-theory proof-math layering (3-rectangle + first-loop structure)

A student at Briefing/Technique-Training level submitted two math mind maps for feedback. The
first (Galois theory) was an earlier trial at incorporating proof detail, with no images because
the topic is "very abstract," using formulas and symbols themselves as condensed relational
representations rather than words. The second (Homology theory) is described as newer and more
general, with an explicit layering experiment:

> "The three biggest rectangles are about main concepts that I recognized in a prestudy session
> and I'm like halfway through the material with the first loop (definitions and relations in the
> guise of theorems but proofs only touched, without details yet)."

Read against the four-layer framework: the three largest rectangles function as the **logic
layer** (main concepts identified during prestudy); the "first loop" functions as a **concepts-
layer pass** — definitions and relations stated as theorems — deliberately without yet drilling
into proof details, which are treated as a later, not-yet-reached layer. The student reports
success with relations between concepts, but describes the grouping/chunking step itself as "a
bit elusive... especially when it comes to math where I need to learn a whole theory that new
facts are derived from many earlier facts and definitions." This is the only captured instance in
the corpus of layering applied to proof-heavy abstract mathematics, and it is a self-reported,
unconfirmed attempt rather than a validated technique demonstration.

## LC27 — Backbone-crowding case

An Ascent 2 student submitted a mind map explicitly titled "LOGIC BACKBONE DISAPPEARS":

> "I have a recurring problem adding the concepts and important details to the logic backbone.
> The problem being that the logic layer completely disappears when I add the concepts that
> dictate the logic. I have created this simple mindmap to show you what happens. I am able to
> find the logic throughout the topic. I try to cover as many topics at once as I can, the more
> topics I add, the more the backbone disappears. The thing is, a logic is made by multiple
> concepts, when I add the concepts e.g in relation A -> B, the subconcepts are f,g,h,i,j. When I
> add them, the clear shape of the logic is gone. This is repeating. When I keep the thick arrow
> from A to B, it just crowds the mindmap further leading to Spiderwebbing. Arrows cross over one
> another making it messy. And then I think what does this arrow even mean? if I have f,g,h,i and
> j to show the relation. Here is a real sample of a mindmap that I am working on where this
> isssue is repeating. The logic to the left is clear, crisp and memorable. When adding the
> concepts, the backbone is disappearing."

This is the fullest captured description of the concepts-layer-crowds-out-logic-layer failure
mode anywhere in the corpus (see 02-concepts/layers-of-learning.md for the concept-level
treatment). No resolution is captured in text.

## Other captured layering applications (LC27, briefer submissions)

- **Cell-transport-mechanisms mindmap** (timestamp 1:35:31, Ascent 2 student) — an explicit,
  self-reported *successful* layers-of-learning application, contrasting with the backbone-
  crowding failure above: the student kept layer 1 (logic) and layer 2 (concepts) visually
  distinct by emphasising layer 1 with bold arrows and using different colours per layer,
  continually rearranged the map when unsatisfied with the structure, and reports "great
  retention from the material as a result." No ICS confirmation is captured either way.
- **Condensed 40–45-lecture-slide mindmap** (timestamp 1:15:48, same clinic) — a student reports
  explicitly attempting to incorporate "the layers of learning concept" at scale, condensing
  40–45 lecture slides into one map, and requests feedback. No answer is captured.
