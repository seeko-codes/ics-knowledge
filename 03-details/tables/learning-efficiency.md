## Why "content covered" fails as a metric

Measuring efficiency as "amount of content covered per session" (e.g. 5 lectures in 2 hours) is
rejected as a useless metric for three reasons: it does not account for retention of the
knowledge; it ignores the extra time later spent relearning what was forgotten; and it does not
account for the quality of learning achieved or the ability to use the knowledge in different
ways. Efficient learners focus on high-quality learning; inefficient learners focus on "doing
more."

## The equation

**Learning efficiency = Total mastery and retention ÷ Total time spent learning.**

- **Total mastery** = the average of all partial masteries.
- **Partial mastery** = retention at a given mastery level ÷ required retention at that level.

Example framing: an inefficient learner might achieve 50% over 100 hours, while an efficient
learner achieves 90% over 50 hours.

## Calculation procedure

1. Identify the highest and lowest required knowledge-mastery levels (see
   ../../02-concepts/measurement.md and mastery-levels.md).
2. Assess retention after **one week** (as a percentage) for each level. One week is used because
   spacing intervals should allow for a one-week retrieval session, and this extrapolates
   reasonably to assessment-time retention.
3. Identify the retention required for each level for the actual assessment.
4. Calculate partial mastery: real one-week retention ÷ required retention, per level.
5. Calculate total mastery: the average of the partial masteries.
6. Calculate total time: sum of all learning and retrieval session time across however many
   weeks, for the subject.

## Worked example: Jeremy (second-year university economics)

Jeremy is assessed at a maximum of Level 4 and a minimum of Level 1 (occasional fact-recall
questions).

| Level | Retention | Required for assessment |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 60% | 80% |
| 2 | 60% | 90% |
| 3 | 50% | 70% |
| 4 | 30% | 60% |

**Partial masteries** (retention ÷ required):

| Level | Retention | Required | Partial mastery |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 60% | 80% | 60/80 = 75% |
| 2 | 60% | 90% | 60/90 = 67% |
| 3 | 50% | 70% | 50/70 = 71% |
| 4 | 30% | 60% | 30/60 = 50% |

In words: Jeremy has reached only 50% of the mastery and retention needed at Level 4, but 75% of
what is needed at Level 1 (fact recall).

**Total mastery** = average(75%, 67%, 71%, 50%) = **66%**. Time spent so far: **30 hours**.
**Learning efficiency = 66% over 30 hours.**

## Inefficient-learner variant

For an inefficient learner with the same profile, doubling study time from 30 to 60 hours raises
total mastery only from 66% to 76% — a 10-point gain for double the time. This illustrates that
when methods are limited, more hours of study produce smaller and smaller benefit ("hammer"
analogy: at some point, certain mastery levels cannot be reached with a given method regardless of
how much time or effort is applied).

## Key considerations

- Efficient learners reach a higher mastery percentage in less time.
- More study hours do not necessarily raise mastery proportionally: the rate of forgetting rises
  with learning volume, so learners rarely reach 100% retention, especially at higher mastery
  levels.
- The learning method strongly dictates how easily higher mastery levels can be reached; some
  methods are effectively incompatible with high mastery unless paired with very high deep
  processing.
- Learning efficiency depends on the required mastery/retention level for the task — lower
  requirements are easier to meet in less time.

## Learner-type comparison

| Learner type | Mastery profile | Time investment | Example values |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inefficient methods, low deep processing | Achieves lower levels; struggles with higher levels; retention generally low | High, due to excessive relearning | 50% over 100 hours; total mastery does not improve much as hours increase |
| Inefficient methods, high deep processing | Achieves most levels of mastery; retention low-to-moderate depending on deep processing | Moderate | 70% over 50 hours; total mastery may increase slowly with more hours |
| Efficient methods, low deep processing | Achieves most levels, struggles with the highest; retention generally moderate | Moderate to high | 70% over 50 hours; total mastery increases proportionally with hours |
| Efficient methods, high deep processing | High achievement across all levels; retention high | Low | 90% over 30 hours; total mastery increases proportionally with hours |
