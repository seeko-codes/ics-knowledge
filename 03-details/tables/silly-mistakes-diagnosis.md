## Mechanism

Most "silly mistakes" in tests and exams are not primarily exam-technique errors — they usually reflect a
deficiency of understanding or application, compounded by technique. Even mistakes that look incredibly
obvious most often represent that deficiency, not a lapse of care. Example: unit-conversion errors in maths
or physics almost invariably disappear once the student develops a deeper understanding of what the units
represent — the erroneous conversion then goes against what was memorised *and* fails to make sense, raising
an internal "alarm bell" that shallow understanding cannot generate. The recommended sequence is therefore to
fix preparation/understanding gaps **first**, then optimise exam technique **second**. Trying to overcome
silly mistakes through sheer intention or effort ("be more careful") does not work.

## Diagnostic procedure

1. Identify the biggest root cause.
2. Experiment with a targeted solution.
3. Reflect, and identify other potential causes.
4. Repeat the cycle of experimentation and reflection.

If it is unclear where to start identifying root causes, the double-generation practice-test strategy
(../techniques/double-generation.md) is named as a useful first step.

## Root-cause table

| Cause | Symptoms | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Test anxiety / nervousness | Mistakes follow a random pattern with no clear association to question difficulty, type, subject, or error type; mistakes increase with higher-stakes exams. | Graduated exposure during self-study (timed practice under test conditions, reduced time allowed, done in a group for added pressure); deep breathing techniques before tests (e.g. the Wim Hof breathing cycle); pre-test rituals that invoke calmness and confidence; avoid last-minute studying immediately before entering the test room (limited benefit, likely increases anxiety). |
| Insufficient lower-order knowledge | Mistakes cluster around discrete fact recall of specific details. | Incorporate a lower-order retrieval method into the learning system, such as flashcards. |
| Insufficient higher-order knowledge | Mistakes on conceptual questions asking about two or more concepts, on big-picture ideas, or on approaches/strategies for solving problems. | Increase higher-order learning during encoding and retrieval sessions (see interleaving for more higher-order retrieval ideas); most of the program's encoding techniques are suitable for this. |
| Insufficient procedural practice | Mistakes in applying knowledge to highly procedural tasks — common in software development, engineering, and maths. | Incorporate more generative procedural/execution-type interleaving into retrieval sessions (see interleaving). |
| Unidentified general knowledge gaps | A mixed combination of mistakes across multiple orders of learning; similar mistakes recur across practice tests. | Double-generation practice test strategy (../techniques/double-generation.md) to more clearly identify knowledge gaps. |
