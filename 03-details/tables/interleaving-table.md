This is the corpus's central technique catalog for interleaved retrieval, comprehensive by design.
It crosses technique against knowledge type (declarative vs. procedural) and order of learning
(lower/mid/higher-order), and is meant to be consulted for a few relevant methods at a time, not
read end-to-end.

## Useless techniques

These techniques offer so little benefit that they should not be used under any circumstances —
there are always better alternatives for the same amount of time.

| Declarative-focused | Procedural-focused |
|---|---|
| Passive re-reading and re-writing | Re-reading and re-writing notes |
| Passive re-listening | REBIM |
| Practice questions (passive method) | |
| Peer/group discussion (undirected) | |

**Passive re-reading, re-writing, and re-listening**: listening, reading, and re-writing notes
without particular cognitive focus. Usually boring, monotonous, disengaged, and repetitive; the
focus may be on trying to memorise what is being written, repeatedly saying sentences mentally, or
just "trying to understand." Does not activate the most effective learning pathways and is highly
time-consuming and demotivating.

**REBIM (Repetitive Execution Beyond Initial Mastery)**: a phenomenon coined by Dr Justin Sung —
the repetitive completion of tasks or activities for skills/processes that can already be done to a
high level of proficiency (e.g. coding the same simple scripts repeatedly, solving the same
difficulty of maths questions despite already answering them easily, or repeating the same kind of
dialogue in a language already fluent). The benefit is negligible given the alternative techniques
available.

**Practice questions (passive method)**: solving problems or answering questions and immediately
checking the answer, often paired with answering only mentally or concluding "I could solve that"
without going through all the steps and checking properly. Because the question feels familiar and
the answer makes sense, the brain overestimates ability and underestimates challenge — this method
consistently fails to identify knowledge gaps while wasting valuable practice questions.

**Peer/group discussion (undirected)**: group discussion can be useful, but most undirected group
study wastes time compared to what could be achieved in the same time through alternative
techniques.

## Enhancing interleaving using AI

**Principles for using AI**: AI chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek, etc.) can be powerful
learning tools when used correctly, detrimental when over-relied on for certain processes.

Use AI for:
- Saving time on tedious tasks that would not normally require much cognitive effort (e.g.
  collecting keywords for a new topic, getting a big-picture summary).
- Consolidating multiple sources of information into one, to avoid the split attention created by
  switching between sources frequently.
- Creating test questions (self-generated questions have a learning benefit, but AI-generated
  questions are judged overall more beneficial due to substantial time savings and the ability to
  use AI for external calibration of knowledge).
- Asking questions to explore a topic, similarly to asking a tutor.

Avoid using AI for:
- Checking the "truth" of a nuanced and complex concept — LLMs do not have an inherent
  understanding of truth or reality even when they appear to, so answers on nuanced/complex topics
  are much less reliably correct.
- Bypassing meaningful cognitive effort — building mental models, evaluating relationships, and
  understanding why something is important are crucial processes for forming knowledge networks.
  AI can state why something is important directly, but this can push the brain into a more
  passive "understanding" mode that is far less effective — a mode that is very difficult for
  beginner/intermediate learners to detect. Beginners are advised to avoid using AI for chunking
  and organising information until they have a clear internal sense of active vs. passive learning
  and cognitive load. If AI is used to chunk, evaluate, or group information, its output should
  always be challenged and checked against alternatives.

**Examples of adapting methods with AI**

| Initial method | Adapted with AI |
|---|---|
| Making flashcards | Use AI to generate 3 alternative flashcards on the same concept, to create more interleaving. |
| Answering questions and self-testing | Use AI to generate questions at a variety of levels and complexities; use AI to check answers and interact with AI to explore gaps and questions. |
| Brain dump | Use AI to check for gaps in knowledge after doing a brain dump. |

## Declarative-focused techniques

Some techniques suit multiple orders of learning; these are marked with an asterisk (*).

**Recap on declarative knowledge**: information based on facts, concepts, and logic — the
knowledge that encodes content material (also called conceptual or descriptive knowledge). Examples
of predominantly declarative topics: science subjects, history, politics, law, economics theory,
accounting theory, mathematics theory, business and finance theory, coding logic, language
vocabulary/syntax/grammar/structure.

| Order of learning | Techniques |
|---|---|
| **Lower-order** (best for direct fact recall and detail memorisation) | 3Cs; Flashcards (simple); Brain dump (linear); Generated questions (isolated); Teaching (isolated); Feynman method*; Method of loci or Modified Method of Loci; Story/link method; Ben system |
| **Mid-order** (best for knowledge application, basic problem solving, obvious relationships) | Flashcards (simple relational); Brain dump (mindmap)*; Generated questions (simple relational or multi-relational); Teaching (simple relational); Practice questions (direct method and advanced group method*); Feynman method* |
| **Higher-order** (best for discussions and complex problem-solving where multiple concepts affect each other) | Chunkmaps; Flashcards (evaluative); Brain dump (mindmap)*; Generated questions (evaluative); Teaching (Modified WPW); Peer/Group discussion (evaluative); Practice questions (extended method and advanced group method*); Feynman method* |

### Explanations

**3Cs (Cover, copy, check)**: a low-level "fill-in-the-blanks" retrieval technique — cover part of
the notes, attempt to rewrite/redraw the covered part from memory, then uncover and check. Shown to
improve short-term retention of basic information, especially among younger children. Flashcards
using image-occlusion are a more practical variant. Most effective for labelling diagrams and
processes.

**Flashcards (all variations)**

- *Simple*: physical or digital (Anki preferred). Each card tests isolated facts, concepts, or
  processes that do not relate to each other. Useful for specific definitions, constants, or
  other highly specific information that does not fit into relational notes. When higher-order
  encoding is inadequate, simple flashcards are often overloaded, causing wasted time through
  repetition and steep forgetting curves.
- *Simple relational*: each card tests the relationships and influences that two or three concepts
  have on each other. Useful for key relationships that are highly specific and hard to remember —
  often processes that are conceptually separate from the rest of the topic and therefore hard to
  represent through relational note-taking, so they may not easily fit into chunks.
- *Evaluative*: each card holds a generated question (evaluative) — see Generated questions below.

**Generated questions (all variations)**

- *Isolated*: create practice questions that focus on retrieving single facts, concepts, and
  explanations of isolated processes. Many "what"-based questions are isolated; this level is
  ideally served by simple flashcards instead.
- *Simple relational and multi-relational*: simple relational questions challenge explaining or
  discussing the relationship between two related ideas; multi-relational questions challenge
  retrieving the relationships between more than two related ideas. Can be combined as part of
  Practice questions — advanced group method.
- *Evaluative*: challenges discussing relationships between multiple related ideas and concluding
  on their relative strength and importance. A crucial part is that the learner must make a value
  judgment on the importance of concepts compared to others — this level has significantly
  beneficial learning effects for depth of knowledge and retention. Because evaluative questions
  are time-consuming to create, it is recommended to create them in one session and answer them in
  another: creating produces retrieval benefit, as does answering, so splitting the two yields two
  rounds of positive learning effect, and one session prepares for the next. Can be combined as
  part of Practice questions — advanced group method.

**Brain dumps (all variations)**

- *Linear*: an easy technique for testing mass retrieval at low-to-mid mastery. Type out everything
  known about a topic, following learning objectives as a guide if available. Due to the
  limitations of linear notes on representing relationships, it is inefficient for higher-order
  testing and inefficient at finding gaps (it relies on "dumping" everything until retrieval
  difficulty is stumbled upon). Helpful as an end-stage retrieval technique just before assessments.
- *Mindmap*: the same technique in mind map form. Superior for higher-order knowledge testing,
  inferior for lower-order detail testing. As assessment/retrieval standards force more relational
  thinking, this form becomes strategically higher-yield for most learners. Recommended in the
  early stages of retrieval practice to find big-picture gaps quickly, before transitioning to
  lower-order methods.

**Teaching (all variations)**

- *Isolated*: teach a topic to a friend or an imaginary student. An imaginary student is preferred,
  because a real friend may fill gaps in with their own knowledge instead of forcing genuine
  confrontation of gaps or inadequate explanations. Limited to directly explaining facts, concepts,
  and processes. Relatively time-consuming and lower-order-centred, so alternatives such as
  flashcards tend to be preferred.
- *Simple relational*: explaining and discussing how different concepts and processes relate to
  each other. A relatively effective form of retrieval that can be incorporated in the early-to-mid
  stages of revision scheduling, since it finds gaps quickly. **Pro tip**: isolated and simple
  relational teaching are most useful applied *during* learning sessions — teaching something
  immediately after processing it, sometimes called "micro-retrieval." Forcing immediate teaching
  activates generative effects and can help improve deep processing.
- *Modified Whole-Part-Whole (WPW)*: taught in the late stages of the program, potentially the most
  difficult learning technique it contains. It requires an expert level of knowledge mastery to
  execute correctly and challenges all orders of knowledge mastery with rapid knowledge-gap
  identification. Its benefit is directly tied to the learner's skill level with the technique and
  their encoding proficiency — it can be considered impossible unless the fundamental encoding
  techniques have reached total mastery, with at least near-mastery of the Ascent-stage encoding
  methods.

**Practice questions (all variations excluding passive)**

- *Direct*: the conventional way of using practice questions — solve problems and answer questions
  from memory, check answers immediately, review learning material for deeper understanding if
  incorrect. Activates generative learning effects and helps focus on knowledge gaps. However, it
  increases the risk of missing gaps or classifying incorrect answers as "silly mistakes," because
  the root cause of a mistake can remain hidden. Not recommended for most situations compared to
  alternatives, since practice questions are a limited, valuable resource.
- *Extended*: after answering practice questions to best attempt, mark the questions felt unsure
  about, then review the learning material and create a personal "perfect" answer sheet; if an
  official answer sheet is available, check the personal sheet against it. Any question that felt
  unsure about indicates a gap regardless of whether it was "officially" correct, because getting
  one variation right does not guarantee getting a variation of the same knowledge right elsewhere
  — a lack of confidence is a sufficient indication of a gap. Steps: (1) answer the questions;
  (2) evaluate confidence; (3) create perfect answers; (4) check answers against official answers
  to find even more gaps. Because this method is time-consuming, dedicate a large block of time or
  split it across multiple retrieval sessions.

**Peer/group discussion (evaluative)**: unlike undirected group discussion, this sets an explicit
goal to critique, judge, and argue the importance of different relationships and concepts,
especially in view of the bigger picture.

**Chunkmaps (or GRINDEmaps)**: mind map variations taught as a core part of encoding in the Bear
Hunter System, taught in the Growth phase. Although the foundational skills are trained from
Fundamentals 2, this method is not relevant until Ascent 3. Listed here as a retrieval method
because previously studied content (predating chunkmap skill) is worth re-encoding into chunkmaps
to stimulate higher levels of mastery and improve retention. In this situation, it should be the
first step of the revision process, performed as early as possible, making room for other forms of
interleaved retrieval. Most people struggle to create a chunkmap from previously poorly encoded
information, largely because it was never initially processed at higher levels.

**Method of loci, link/story method, and Ben system**: different memorisation techniques. Some are
taught in the course (in late stages), some are not. Although powerful for rote learning of isolated
information, their use cases are narrow. Learning them early tends to create over-reliance on them
instead of developing proper encoding techniques, which are several-fold more efficient for most
real-world learning needs — they are exclusively lower-order methods and are typically over-sold by
"memory champions." Recommended in specific use cases with large volumes of specific (sometimes
ordered) information to learn in a single discrete list; even then, there are usually more
effective ways to encode this.

**Feynman method/technique**: not a single technique but a guided structure. Classical instructions:
(1) choose a topic; (2) teach it to a child — explain using simple language, no jargon; (3) identify
gaps and simplify — note where the explanation is unclear or hard to simplify; (4) review and refine
— return to primary sources to fill gaps and simplify further. A popular early learning method that
forces multi-order knowledge mastery, and still valuable as a "micro-retrieval" technique (try to
teach everything learned "to a child" immediately after learning it, to stay anchored to the big
picture) — even though the program's own encoding techniques are held to directly supersede it for
comprehensive use.

## The advanced group method for practice questions

A composite of generated questions, peer/group discussion, and practice questions. An involved,
extensive retrieval technique, but one of the most consistently effective and useful across all
declarative-focused subjects.

### Instructions (practice instructions)

1. Choose a topic.
2. Create a study group with at least 1 other person of a similar knowledge level (or higher).
3. Each person creates a practice exam. Ensure some mid-order questions as well as more challenging
   and nuanced higher-order questions; strictly lower-order questions can be addressed through
   other retrieval methods.
4. Create a perfect answer sheet for the exam — brain dump to the best of ability first, then review
   learning materials to refine and perfect the answers.
5. Swap practice exams. Hold onto the answer sheet.
6. Answer the swapped exam from memory to the best of ability.
7. Mark answers where uncertain, for further review.
8. Create a perfect answer sheet for this exam by reviewing learning materials.
9. Swap answer sheets.
10. Compare answers against the personal answer sheet and against the other person's, looking for
    differences.
11. Discuss and resolve any disagreements together.

This method tests multiple orders from multiple perspectives and forces positive generative effects
during steps 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, and 10. For each hour spent, the amount of learning consolidation and
gap-testing is among the highest of all retrieval methods. As it is time-consuming, split it across
multiple days.

> Due to its high effectiveness **and** efficiency, the advanced group method for practice
> questions is generally recommended for all learners to try and use where possible.

## Procedural-focused techniques

Some techniques suit multiple orders of learning; these are marked with an asterisk (*).

**About procedural knowledge types**: procedural knowledge is stored in different parts of the
brain than declarative knowledge and involves different cognitive processes; it is based on
actions, behaviours, and processes rather than facts or concepts — the knowledge of how to perform
skills or procedures. Most settings require both declarative and procedural knowledge, but
different subjects/settings need different balances.

**Characteristics of procedural knowledge**: dynamic and action-oriented, involving the ability to
perform tasks and actions effectively; skill-based, context-dependent, often implicit, requiring
practice and hands-on experience for mastery. Unlike declarative knowledge's static facts,
procedural knowledge is closely tied to performance and practical application, and can be refined
over time with experience.

**Examples of procedural-focused disciplines**: conducting a lab experiment with technical
precision; driving or riding a bike; cooking or baking; study skills (theory of technique
performance is declarative; performing it is procedural); solving mathematics or physics problems
(the approach is declarative, the calculation is procedural); speaking or writing a language
fluently; creating an accounting balance sheet accurately and efficiently; building an app or
coding (language logic/syntax is declarative, executing it accurately and efficiently is
procedural).

**The relationship between declarative and procedural knowledge**: a combination of strong
declarative and procedural knowledge is essential in most applications. Strong procedural but weak
declarative math skills can excel at familiar problems but struggle with new challenges and the
utilisation of different concepts — e.g. a coder with procedural expertise may script common
functions effortlessly but struggle with coding challenges, technical interviews, and new projects.
Strong declarative but limited procedural skill can understand problem-solving approaches but
struggle with accurate execution — e.g. conceptualising innovative projects while producing coding
errors, bugs, inefficient practices, and reduced productivity. Truly excellent learners typically
excel in both domains.

| Order of learning | Techniques |
|---|---|
| **Lower-order** — best for following the rules of a procedure (e.g. basic vocabulary, applying a rule on a formula, correct syntax while coding, a basic function in a coding language, following data transformation processes) | Retrieved execution (simple); Simple flashcards; Ben system; Challenges (simple) |
| **Mid-order** — best for basic knowledge application (e.g. basic phrases and predictable conversation in a foreign language, simple problem-solving, creating simple applications/functions, straightforward analyses) | Retrieved execution (integrative); Challenges (integrative); Variable modification |
| **Higher-order** — best for challenging applications (e.g. unpredictable conversations, complex problem-solving in unfamiliar contexts, advanced functionality/applications, a series of related analyses/transformations) | Retrieved execution (applied); Challenges (edge-case); Variable addition |

### Explanations

**Ben system and simple flashcards**: as described above; in most scenarios less effective than
more procedurally heavy alternatives. An exception is language learning, where they remain a
valuable adjunct for vocabulary acquisition.

**Retrieved execution (all variations)**

- *Simple*: practising a skill or process from memory without any cue or guidance. The simple
  version is limited to isolated applications without variation, and is often the first way to
  practise a new skill. Many continue with the simple version even after mastery, at which point it
  becomes REBIM and ineffective.
- *Integrative*: taking multiple skills/processes and combining them to achieve a greater or more
  complex function (e.g. coding more complicated functions from a series of basic functions; for
  languages, forming common phrases and dialogues by chaining together simpler phrases already
  learned). The objective is a more diverse application of the same knowledge, improving
  **conditional knowledge** — the knowledge of when and how to use declarative and procedural
  knowledge. Learners should aim to move to integrative retrieved execution as soon as possible
  after gaining basic competency with simple execution, since it provides stronger generative
  effects, greater cognitive load, and more interleaving.
- *Applied*: while integrative practice combines individual components into a more complex product,
  applied practice starts with a broader project that requires multiple integrated components to
  function. Integrative starts from understanding basic procedures and thinking how they can be
  combined into meaningful chains; applied works backwards — starting from a target output and
  thinking how it can be achieved. In coding: developing a web application to fulfil a real need,
  requiring strategy and execution of design, implementation, and QA. In languages: thinking of a
  common scenario (e.g. conversing with friends at a mall in German), practising multiple
  variations of conversations that could arise, and ideally testing what was practised by actually
  immersing in that scenario. Applied retrieved execution is the most challenging but most
  effective form of practice; learners should move towards it once confident with integrative
  execution.

**Challenges (all variations)**

- *Simple*: similar to retrieved execution, but involving a prompt or problem stem that must be
  solved using the learner's skills. Can be self-created or externally provided. Helps build basic
  procedural competency with simple applications, requiring simple retrieved execution.
- *Integrative*: like integrative retrieved execution, combines multiple component pieces to solve
  a more complex problem, sometimes using two or more component processes/skills in ways not
  directly taught. Common example: solving physics or maths problems where different formulas and
  concepts combine in a multi-step calculation.
- *Edge-case*: problems centred on atypical examples of how skills/processes can be used, almost
  always different from how the skills were originally taught, requiring the learner to think
  about how processes apply in an unfamiliar context. Strong declarative knowledge of the
  principles is needed, since declarative knowledge is a significant component of identifying valid
  approaches in the first place. Edge-case challenges may appear in hackathons or competitions —
  so unfamiliar that most people struggle to know how to start. Creating an edge-case challenge is
  much easier than solving one, since solving requires in-depth knowledge of the correct approach
  plus procedural skill to execute, while creating merely requires knowing that concepts are
  related and may hypothetically influence each other (whether it really works is the solver's
  job). **This means edge cases can be used instead of evaluative generated questions in the
  advanced group method of practice tests.** Protip: AI can assist in creating more complex
  variations of existing problems.

**Variable modification or addition**

- *Modification*: changing the variables in a given problem/challenge/question to solve the same
  problem again with different figures. The nature of the problem and the approach stay the same.
  Good for practising low-level procedural skills before moving to more integrative retrieval.
- *Addition*: adding variables to an existing problem/challenge/question to increase its
  difficulty. The added variable must make logical sense (requiring good declarative knowledge)
  while remaining solvable. Beneficial learning effects come from thinking about: which variable to
  add to increase complexity; whether the variable makes sense; what value to give it for it to be
  reasonable; whether a solution actually exists once it is added; how it interacts with existing
  variables. More than one variable can be added if viable. For maths/physics: adding air
  resistance to a mechanics question and changing the trajectory due to collision; adding a
  second-order differential equation with added initial conditions for calculus; adding limits of
  integration when evaluating an integral; calculating the maximum volume of a cylinder while
  adding a surface-area constraint and a fixed 1:2 radius:height ratio. For coding: additional
  conditions such as time-out periods, additional user inputs, further data transformations. For
  languages: the number of speakers in a conversation, the number of viewpoints of an opinion, or
  the level of detail/conditions within statements or questions — for example escalating "Where
  shall we eat" into a conditional clause, then into a multi-clause statement with history and
  preference. Protip: AI can assist in creating more complex variations of existing problems.

## Example combinations

**Combination for mostly procedural knowledge** — appropriate for people with mostly procedural
requirements and minimal declarative knowledge needs. Predominantly procedural fields have
extremely simple but effective retrieval schedules, because: (1) skills continually build on
themselves, causing natural consolidation and repetition; (2) retrieval competence is strongly
related to the level of retrieval practice, error feedback, and difficulty — the path is
straightforward, even if it takes time; (3) lower-order competence is directly tested by
higher-order methods, unlike declarative knowledge, where lower-order knowledge may not necessarily
be tested to the same level of detail. The basic structure for procedural-focused retrieval systems
is therefore to start with lower-order methods and progressively aim towards higher-order methods.

**Combination for mostly declarative knowledge** — appropriate for people with primarily
declarative knowledge requirements and minimal procedural needs. Given as a guide for a
comprehensive system; parts that don't work should be adapted based on observation and need.

| When | What method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Before first encounter with new topic | Prestudy as per program guidelines | Creates big-picture framing. |
| Day 1 — major learning event | Encoding as per program guidelines | The major learning event (lecture, class, workshop, or dedicated first study session); proper encoding is critical for establishing well-organised knowledge. |
| Day 1 — while studying or during same-day review | Simple relational teaching while studying via micro-retrieval | Tests during the earliest encoding sessions; helps prevent dropping into lower-order learning patterns. |
| Day 2 to 5 — first interleaved retrieval session | Brain dump (mindmap) + create simple and simple relational flashcards | Mindmap brain dumps are a useful first-line method, testing mid-to-higher-order knowledge; quickly identify big gaps and establish a foundation. Flashcards created as lower-order gaps are discovered. |
| Ongoing | Review simple and simple relational flashcards | Completed opportunistically in small pockets of time; covers lower-order needs without dedicated sessions. |
| Day 7 to 14 — second interleaved retrieval session | Create relational and evaluative generated questions + perfect answer sheets, to set up the advanced group method | Builds on foundations, fills gaps, consolidates via mid-to-higher-order questions; also creates a resource for future retrieval sessions. |
| One month — third interleaved retrieval session | Collate all generated questions into an exam; swap with a friend; answer each other's; create a perfect answer sheet after best attempt; swap again per the advanced group method | An ideal time for a large, multi-order challenge; few gaps should remain afterward. |
| Two months — fourth interleaved retrieval session | Evaluative peer/group discussion, WPW teaching (if able), or continuously repeat the advanced group method | Lower-order requirements continue to be covered by flashcards; finish with repeated multi-order gap testing. |

**Combination for mixed knowledge types** — when requirements are mixed, use a basis of the
declarative combination (declarative knowledge is often the most time-consuming and challenging to
master). For subjects/topics with a higher procedural component, swap declarative techniques for
procedural techniques; topics with a high procedural component tend to have a more minor
declarative component. Extra interleaved retrieval sessions become unnecessary in these cases — for
those subjects the techniques may start heavily declarative, but after gaining a high level of
declarative knowledge from initial encoding (and maybe one interleaving session), the techniques
move to exclusively procedural.

Summary: use declarative methods until good declarative competence is reached (faster for
procedurally heavy topics — in some cases initial encoding alone suffices for the declarative
component); then transition to procedural methods, progressing from lower to higher order.

## FAQs

**Should I type or write out answers to questions?** Always generate answers fully — written or
typed, never answered mentally only. Whether typing or freehand writing does not matter for the
cognitive benefit; use whichever is faster. If the method requires drawing or mapping (e.g.
chunkmaps), freehand is superior, as most apps do not allow the same freedom as freehand writing
and drawing.

**Do I need to write out answers in full for practice questions?** Yes, always — written or typed,
never just answered mentally. This prevents gaps caused by the illusion of explanatory depth.

**Do I need to teach out loud if using teaching methods?** Preferred, yes. Speaking out loud forces
slowing down and being explicit about retrieving knowledge, preventing gaps from the illusion of
explanatory depth. Quiet speech is fine to preserve the voice.

**What if I go out of scope of my curriculum?** Use learning objectives from the curriculum (if
any) to gauge how far "out of scope" to go. Aim to go slightly out of scope for optimal results, as
staying too rigidly in scope can make it harder to re-encode complex concepts.

**How accurately do I need to space out sessions?** See spacing (02-concepts/spacing.md and
03-details/tables/spacing-schedule.md). Generally, good-quality interleaving matters more than
accurate spacing — the schedule can be off by days or even weeks as long as each session's quality
is good.

**What if I don't have enough time for all these sessions?** Do the best possible. As encoding
skill develops, retention rises (making retrieval sessions faster and easier) and learning outpaces
the cohort, allowing a learner to get ahead. An efficient system that gets ahead of the cohort
schedule (if one exists) allows "cramming" encoding early, so the rest of a semester/term/course can
focus mostly on interleaved retrieval. Until skill develops to this point, continue doing the best
possible and fit in as much interleaved retrieval as can be managed.

**For foreign language learning, I heard that I shouldn't try to speak or write anything until I
feel like I have mastered it already.** There are popular theories in the language-learning space
around immersion learning and comprehensible input, but the popular versions of these theories seem
far removed from the actual research — likely due to unqualified "language learning gurus"
misinterpreting it. There is no evidence supporting withholding retrieved execution practice for
language learning; doing so would go against most research on effective learning, including
research on immersion learning and the comprehensible-input hypothesis itself.
