# Mastery levels 1-5
---
type: table
serves: measurement
sources: [ics-learning-support/skill-development/101.md]
---

Composite of Bloom's revised taxonomy and SOLO taxonomy (structure of observed learning outcomes).
Higher levels of mastery correlate with the ability to solve more complex problems and with
higher knowledge retention.

## Level 1 (lowest)

Recall of facts and isolated details only; learning is heavily repetition- and memorisation-
based; terminology is recognised and its meaning understood, but not connected further.

- **Common methods:** flashcards on facts/definitions; cover-copy-check; questions on isolated
  details.
- **Commonly tested by:** fact-recall questions.

## Level 2

Explaining isolated concepts and discussing them individually; solving simple problems using a
single concept; simple discussion of the concept, but struggling with extended ideas or atypical
applications.

- **Common methods:** flashcards explaining concepts/processes; questions explaining how a
  process or concept works; teaching someone about a single concept; process maps and flow
  diagrams.
- **Commonly tested by:** questions asking for an explanation/description of a process or
  concept.

## Level 3

Seeing how concepts relate to each other; analysing and comparing concepts; relationships exist
between individual concepts but are not yet strongly connected to the big picture of the whole
topic. In-depth (though not expert-level) discussion is possible, including knowing what
questions to ask an expert.

- **Common methods:** teaching how two or more concepts relate; comparing/contrasting multiple
  concepts; questions testing the relationship between two or more concepts; mindmaps showing how
  concepts relate.
- **Commonly tested by:** questions asking for comparison/contrast across multiple concepts;
  problems requiring knowledge from multiple concepts.

## Level 4 — significant learning benefits

A heavily connected appreciation of the relationships between multiple concepts; individual facts
and details are seen in terms of how they fit the big picture; understanding often feels
"simpler" and more "intuitive," with little purely repetition-memorised information.
Contextually nuanced discussion with an expert becomes possible, navigating fluidly between
concepts and quickly appreciating how a change to one concept affects both other concepts and the
overall topic/process/function.

- **Common methods:** chunkmaps showing concepts categorised into groups by similarity, plus the
  relationships between chunks, weighted by relative importance; questions requiring
  justification/discussion of the relative importance of concepts and relationships; Whole-Part-
  Whole reteaching technique; discussion of how context changes the importance of concepts and
  relationships.
- **Commonly tested by:** questions requiring nuanced discussion of how concepts influence each
  other; complex problems drawing on multiple concepts and their mutual influence; contextually
  abstract problems requiring application across diverse, multi-factorial scenarios.

**The impact difference between Level 3 and Level 4 is significant:**

| Level | Characteristic | Impact on learning |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | Identifying relationships, similarities, and differences | Moderately helpful — starts developing connections and networks |
| 4 | Making value judgments and evaluating the overall importance/relevance of relationships, similarities, and differences | Extremely helpful — forces substantially deeper encoding and processing |

## Level 5

Creating new and novel information from existing knowledge — generating hypotheses and
conjectures.

- **Common methods:** novel research and exploration of a thesis; discussion of ideas that
  extend known principles and prior knowledge; hypothesising structures and connections from
  prior knowledge; exploring new information as an experiment to confirm or reject hypotheses.
- **Commonly tested by:** high-level narrative, commentary, or research; typically no formal-
  education assessments exist at this level prior to a PhD.

## Key principle

Higher-order is integrated; lower-order is isolated. Better big-picture understanding leads to
more meaningful information, which leads to greater retention and encoding, reducing the need for
frequent repetition — because strong, simple, highly refined organisational schemas let the brain
navigate a topic quickly and fluidly.

## Important considerations

- Both lower-order and higher-order methods are needed in a comprehensive learning system.
- Lower-order approaches only produce lower-order outcomes.
- Higher-order approaches produce both lower-order and higher-order outcomes.
- It is generally better to start learning with higher-order approaches, using lower-order methods
  as a supplement or closer to assessment.
- Higher-order methods make learning new content progressively easier as expertise builds;
  lower-order methods tend to increase overwhelm, since information stays isolated rather than
  consolidating into a network.
