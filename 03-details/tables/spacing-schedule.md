## Encoding and retrieval

Encoding and retrieval are two sides of the learning coin. Efficiency and accuracy require both.
Outside of inadequate encoding, "retrieval failure" is itself a common cause of decay of previously
encoded information. When information is encoded to a higher quality, less retrieval is needed.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/Encoding vs retrieval.jpeg` — graph showing that as
encoding quality rises, the retrieval requirement falls, but never reaches zero.

Key principles:
- As encoding gets better, less is forgotten and less retrieval is needed.
- Encoding skill takes time to develop; during that time, retrieval is crucial to address knowledge
  decay.
- Some level of retrieval is always needed, because the brain is never leak-proof — there is always
  some knowledge decay.

Technically, encoding and retrieval are not completely separate processes. The majority opinion in
learning science treats them as fundamentally intertwined rather than as two distinct processes.
They are discussed separately in this program because the physical actions and techniques used to
optimise retrieval tend to be distinct from the methods used for first-pass encoding — from a
practical perspective, keeping them separate is more helpful.

## Spacing schedules

It is not necessary to follow an ultra-strict spacing schedule. Some form of semi-regular spacing
that keeps a learner on top of their material is recommended.

> **General rule**: a spacing schedule of same-day, 1 day, 1 week, and 1 month — followed very
> loosely.

### Worked weekly example

| Day | Session type | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Monday and Tuesday | Same-day retrieval | New material is learned and revised on the same day. |
| Wednesday | One-day retrieval | Interleaved retrieval session for Monday and Tuesday content (and Wednesday if new material was learned too). |
| Thursday | Same-day retrieval for new content | New material is learned and revised on the same day. |
| Friday or Saturday | One-week retrieval | Interleaved retrieval session for all content over the last 2 weeks. |
| End of the month | One-month retrieval | Interleaved retrieval session for the last 4 weeks. |
| 3 to 4 weeks prior to assessment | One-month retrieval+ | Retrieve everything relevant for the assessment. |

With this schedule: a same-day revision, a mid-week revision, an end-of-week revision, and an
end-of-month revision. The end-of-week revision encompasses the last 2 weeks; the end-of-month
revision encompasses the last 4 weeks.

All material is revised within 1 to 3 days of first learning it, then 1 to 2 weeks later, then 2 to
4 weeks later. Combined with prestudy and on-the-day studying, this means any given material is
encoded and re-encoded (via interleaved retrieval practice) around **6 times** in a given month.

> This exact schedule is reported to be used by thousands of students to great success.

## Adjustment rules / FAQ

**Isn't this excessive and time-consuming?**

A spacing schedule like the above can seem excessive to those with lower levels of encoding, since
revising an entire month's content might appear to be a titanic task. In reality, effective encoding
techniques mean that approximately **80 to 90%** of the material is retained by the end-of-week
revision, so one week's material can be revised in just a few hours. Because each subsequent
interleaved retrieval session reduces knowledge decay and re-encodes the information, the
end-of-month revision for 4 weeks' worth of material can become a **half-day** activity. Combined
with prestudying and learning material in batches ahead of formal class/lecture/course schedules
(concentrated study bursts that put a learner a month or more ahead of "class," with spaced
retrieval of just a few hours every week), the total time spent and ease of maintaining such a
schedule is described as extremely easy.

**What if my encoding skills are not good enough yet?**

This kind of spacing schedule can be difficult for those with low encoding ability. Because
encoding and retrieval practice are both necessary for performance, deficiencies in encoding must
be addressed through *more* retrieval, not less. Over time, as encoding improves, the time taken
for retrieval naturally reduces. In the short term, a strict spaced interleaved retrieval schedule
is recommended as a core foundation of the learning system.
