## Statement

A reference of what "importance" commonly means per subject, offered to guide the "why is this
important?" question with domain-appropriate framing. The corpus is explicit that this list is
**not comprehensive**: relying on it alone as a substitute for actually asking "why is it
important?" will cause problems later. It is a starting hint, not a rule.

## Common patterns of importance by subject

| Subject | Importance commonly means chunking based on… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Biology | Function; structure vs. function; scale (microscopic to macroscopic); a group of causes leading to a group of effects | Instead of chunking organelles by location, chunk them by function |
| Chemistry | Mechanisms of reaction; real-world applications; electronegativity; common reagents or products | Instead of an unordered list, chunk reactions by the mechanisms that cause them |
| Physics | Real-world applications; conceptually important precursors to other concepts | Instead of following the textbook, chunk concepts and equations by common application |
| Maths | Real-world applications; conceptually important precursors to other concepts | Instead of following the textbook, chunk groups of equations by the effect they produce on a graph |
| English | The author/director/artist's background, motivations, or values; the story's significance | Instead of an unordered list, chunk themes and techniques by which of the author's values they derive from |
| History | Major cultural, political, religious, philosophical, or social movements; cause and effect; predominant emotions or motivations of key players | Instead of chunking events by geographical location, chunk them by motivating force |
| Law / Legal Studies | Underlying social principles of justice; pragmatic considerations and compensatory mechanisms; landmark cases that exposed gaps in the system; social, political, or economic principles that must be protected | Instead of listing cases and bills in isolation, chunk them by the underlying legal principle they relate to |
| Languages | Common usage; situational usage; effect on messaging or communication | Instead of an unordered list, chunk words and phrases by emotion or use case |

Two subjects are flagged with distinct qualitative notes rather than a simple pattern:

- **History** is described as likely having the *most diverse* range of possible
  importance-based chunking structures of any subject, since it tracks a massive range of human
  events and activities, each with its own reasons for mattering.
- **Languages** are described as the *hardest* to chunk by importance, because language is not
  inherently logical the way most other subjects are; importance there relates specifically to
  how the learner intends to use the language, not to an objective logical structure.

**Non-comprehensiveness caveat (verbatim intent):** this list is offered only as a reference to
give a general idea of common structures a learner will encounter — it is explicitly not a
substitute for asking "why is it important?" on the actual material.

## Domain-specific chunking binds

Two recurring domain-specific complications are documented in supplementary material, both
extending the general importance-based-chunking procedure into cases where it meets resistance
from the external structure of the source material itself.

### Maths: the hierarchical-dependency bind

A student in Live Clinic 25 (linear-algebra self-study) states the clearest captured version of
this problem: maths textbooks are often organised in a hierarchical knowledge structure with a
high degree of topic-to-topic dependency. Attempting to chunk by importance in this context
creates a genuine structural tension — following the material's logical dependency order risks
simply reproducing the textbook's own structure (the framing-bias failure mode), but deviating
from that order risks not understanding a later topic that depends on an earlier one not yet
covered. No resolution to this tension is captured in the corpus; it is documented as an open,
named problem specific to mathematics.

A related complaint (same clinic, a different student) is that maths requires attending closely
to details (formulas, rules) in order to see the broader relationships between concepts at all —
making early study sessions "not all that different from 'typical' sessions" despite genuine
effort to apply the technique, since the detail cannot be deferred the way it often can in other
subjects.

### Medicine: algorithm-rigidity

Live Clinic 28 documents a matching domain-specific bind for medicine: a student reports that
representing a treatment algorithm for a group of diseases "always ends up with a single node
chain pattern" (see encoding-failure-modes.md) no matter how the algorithm is approached, because
the source material (a clinical treatment protocol) is itself linear and sequential by design.
The same clinic documents a second instance: mapping a set of drugs by their effects, their
contraindications, and their special administration concerns separately raises the question of
whether the same keyword should appear multiple times on one mindmap (each appearance chunked
under a different importance-basis) — left unresolved in captured text.

Live Clinic 21 documents a closely related instance in a different medical sub-domain: a fixed
set of "Core Conditions" (170 conditions across reproductive health, children's health, and
mental health, each requiring symptoms, examinations, investigations, and management to be
learned per a rigid categorical template) chunks well when natural cross-condition relationships
exist (the student's own successful "Fear-Related Disorders" example), but resists chunking, and
collapses back toward linear notes, for conditions whose only connections to each other are
formal/categorical rather than causal (e.g. shared risk factors alone, producing what the student
calls "one weak link" per connection). This is treated in encoding-failure-modes.md as one
instance of a general "rigid-domain resistance" class shared with framing bias's multi-session
compounding problem — externally rigid, protocol- or checklist-like source material resists
independent re-chunking more than looser subject matter does, and no general-purpose fix beyond
standard order-control guidance is captured for this class.

## Chemistry equations: chunking by commonality (video-carried)

A dedicated Learning Support answer on making processed notes for chemistry equations exists but
is video-carried: the written record states only that the method simplifies chemistry note-writing
using a form of chunking by commonality, with spatial arrangement used to remember common features
of the reaction. The demonstration itself is [video-only: not captured]
(ics-learning-support/formal-education/92.md).
