## Teaching to learn

Dependency on a good teacher is not a reliable strategy — developing the skill to be one's own best
teacher means always being able to teach oneself. The Whole-Part-Whole (WPW) technique is highly
multi-order, covering all levels of retrieval in Bloom's revised taxonomy.

Figure: `../research/_assets/inline-images/Bloom's simplified version_1.png` — Bloom's revised
taxonomy (2001), cited to support WPW's claim of multi-level coverage.

This multi-level coverage is what makes the technique powerful, but it requires significant
practice. Even learners with a perfect level of competence in the program's other methods can take
**months** to master WPW.

Background research cited: the "good teacher reliance" phenomenon is partially responsible for the
fade-out effect (external summary article, Dr Justin Sung, LinkedIn); the WPW model itself draws on
Malcolm Knowles' *The Adult Learner: The Definitive Classic in Adult Education and Human Resource
Development*; also cited: Barradell & Kennedy-Jones (2015), "Threshold Concepts, Student Learning
and Curriculum," *Innovations in Education & Teaching International* 52(5):536–545; Mowling &
Heidorn (2013), "Every Shot Counts: Rallying Around Traditional Practices Strategies," *Strategies*
26(2):30–36.

## High-volume retrieval practice (HVRP)

WPW reteaching is an example of high-volume retrieval practice (HVRP) — comprehensive retrieval
covering both relationships and specific details together. It is a strong approach during the early
to mid stages of revision, catching mistakes and targeting weaknesses quickly. Another named
example of HVRP is the brain dump, though brain dumps are noted as less effective than WPW at
testing relationships and conceptual understanding specifically. HVRP is recommended as part of a
spaced retrieval schedule, to prepare knowledge for use in exams, essays, and problem-solving.

### Implementing HVRP

Schedule HVRP for the end-of-week or 2-week revision session, so subsequent revision sessions are
more targeted.

| Timing | Activity |
|---|---|
| Before class/lecture (e.g. the weekend before, or even weeks in advance) | Prestudy with inquiry-based learning via the Bear Hunter System, creating at least a basic mind map up to layers 1 or 2 (if not layer 3). |
| During class/lecture | Consolidate layers 1, 2, and 3. For subjects like Maths or Physics, use this time to practise applying techniques and solving problems. |
| Same day after class | Consolidate and add layer 4. Skin the mind maps and create flashcards (and potentially test questions). |
| Mid-week (only if studying a high volume of material every week) | HVRP using WPW. |
| End of the week | HVRP using WPW — for new content since the mid-week session, if one was done. For additional revision, answer the test questions created earlier (or share them with a study group). |
| Approximately 3 to 4 weeks later, and again before the test or exam (if time allows) | HVRP via brain dump (linear or mind map) and practice papers. |

Flashcards should be completed continuously via microlearning in discrete pockets of time — they
should not need their own scheduled session.

## WPW technique

WPW involves looking at a topic as a **whole**, breaking it into **parts**, then returning to it as
a **whole**. It challenges: higher-order structures and clarity of prioritised relationships
(ability to explain the whole clearly); the strength of relationships between layers (ability to
move into parts and back to whole smoothly); and recall of detailed information (ability to explain
each part accurately).

### Meta-checklist

| How it can feel | Cognitive outcomes |
|---|---|
| Very slow to teach; heightened awareness of how the material is being spoken or which points are chosen to explain next. | Forces a deep, multi-order evaluation of the knowledge structure — the mental schema is tested against higher-order criteria like "What makes the most sense?" and "Why is this important?" |
| Anxious because gaps in knowledge are being found frequently. | A good sign — it allows identification of gaps that may have been hidden by the illusion of explanatory depth. |

## WPW tips

1. Use reverse explanations.
2. Always look for big-picture relevance.
3. Don't get locked into the way the topic was originally taught or learned — the ideal WPW flow
   may not match the GRINDE map's layers.
4. Teach based on memory/recall, not by reading.
5. Doodle a mind map while teaching.
6. Speak out loud to slow down — don't rush.
7. Give it at least **3 days** after finishing the GRINDE map before attempting WPW.
8. Use full recall to find gaps, then revise them to perfect recall.
9. If struggling at first, keep trying — struggle means gaps are being found.

## General teaching flow (two stages)

### Stage 1: Layman's terms

Layman's terms means common terminology that most people can understand, even without prior
knowledge of the topic. Four parts, covered in order, using only layman's terms (no terminology):

1. Explain what the topic is generally about (big picture).
2. Explain why this is important to know.
3. Explain how the topic divides into major components.
4. Explain how the components relate to each other.

This stage tests understanding of concepts and relationships at the first and second layers of
learning, and protects against conceptual questions, difficult short-answer questions, and
curveball questions. By the end of Stage 1, the "listener" should have a solid conceptual
understanding of the overall topic, why it matters, its major components, and how those components
relate.

### Stage 2: Technical explanation

Introduces terminology. Two parts:

1. **Labelling** — add technical terminology to the components already explained.
2. **Technical elaboration** — using the terminology, explain each component in full detail.

Ineffective teaching introduces terminology first and explains it later; WPW deliberately inverts
this. Forcing an explanation without relying on terminology first challenges genuine understanding
of the concepts and relationships. During technical explanation, details and specific information
must be recalled, protecting against detail-oriented questions, definitions, and pure-recall
questions. By the end of Stage 2, the technical names of concepts/components should be known and
the details should make sense.

### Terminology crutching

The most common WPW mistake: relying on jargon instead of a genuine reverse explanation. It makes
the process significantly easier, but by removing desirable difficulty.

### Teaching to the pen

Pacing teaching to match drawing/mindmap speed. The pace of teaching should not be limited by the
pace of drawing — this is a common mistake even among learners with high levels of knowledge
expertise when first learning the method.

## Worked example dissections

Four worked examples, each demonstrating a distinct WPW failure mode or principle (the underlying
per-example dissections are largely video-only; the captured "key principles" for each are recorded
below):

- **Economics** — demonstrates reverse explanation vs. terminology crutching, and the instruction
  to do as much as possible from memory (testing retrieval, not short-term memory or the ability to
  read a mind map). Noted as a good technique for end-of-week revision, being both broad and
  detailed, covering a wide scope with multiple forms of interleaving, and helping target
  weaknesses quickly.
- **Equilibrium** — demonstrates continual relevance-building and a reverse explanation for why the
  entire topic is important, used to bridge naturally into components and major chunks. Notes that
  WPW may be the first time chunks are actively challenged, especially in topics with more
  "obvious" chunk structures — the most fundamental logical flow found during WPW may differ from
  the original logic layer, which is not necessarily a problem but should prompt evaluating whether
  the initial chunking was as intuitive as possible. Difficulty fluently explaining the importance
  of a topic or concept may signal that chunks were information-based rather than importance- or
  logic-based.
- **Electromagnetism** — demonstrates a conciseness failure (traced to unclear communication of
  importance, leading to over-explanation and poor transitions to more detailed parts) and "teaching
  to the pen" (see above). Both mistakes are noted as common even among learners with high levels of
  knowledge expertise first learning WPW.
- **Biodiversity** — demonstrates uncertainty about whether depth of detail and breadth of topic
  coverage is sufficient, posed as a reflective question rather than a directive worked correction.
