## Overview

ReCOVer is a four-step method for structuring essay writing during assessments and examinations: **Recall,
Chunk, Order, Verbalise**. It is positioned as the direct essay-writing analogue of the Bear Hunter System's
Skin step and of WPW: the Chunk step is described as akin to Skinning a mind map, and the overall approach as
structuring and linearising writing similarly to performing WPW.

Two problems are named as the most common causes of poor essay writing: ideas communicated out of an order
and flow that is easy to understand (new perspectives/concepts introduced randomly, conclusions not
well-justified, points lacking examples or clarity, overall progression hard to follow without deep
concentration), and insufficient time spent planning structure and flow before writing (most people spend far
too little time on this, then waste time mid-writing trying to work out the flow). Fundamental principles of
good writing: language appropriate to the expected standard but simple and concise; arguments and
perspectives represented logically; ideas developing in a way that is easy to follow. "Place more emphasis on
crafting the logical flow so it is as simple and understandable as possible. This is one of the biggest
separators of good writing vs. mediocre writing."

## Procedure

### 1. Recall (≤10 minutes in exam)

After applying BHS, the learner should have deep, heavily connected knowledge of the topic. Recall all
relevant points concerning the essay question stem, and craft a flow of main points and chunks exactly as for
a logic/concepts layer — but use the question stem or essay prompt as the guideline for which chunk structure
is most relevant.

**Difficulty at this step likely indicates a lack of higher-order learning of the topic during preparation.**

### 2. Chunk (≤5 minutes in exam; 3-4 main chunks)

Remove weaker or less relevant points that do not support the main argument, and evaluate whether the
resulting flow of ideas is the most easily understandable. This step is explicitly akin to Skinning a mind
map. The target output is a clear logic layer with:

1. A one-directional flow and progression of ideas (this is what allows linearisation into the essay).
2. Supporting concepts and arguments that clearly support each chunk of the logic layer.
3. A total of **three to four main chunks** to keep the argument concise for most essays (this count may
   change with the level of writing required — see the essay-length table below).

### 3. Order (≤5 minutes in exam)

Linearise the structure: arrange the biggest chunks and any major sub-chunks into a linear order, e.g.:

- Introduction — overview of Main point 1, Main point 2, Main point 3
- Main point 1 — Supporting concept 1, Supporting concept 2, Bridging concept 3
- Main point 2 — Supporting concept 1, Supporting concept 2
- Main point 3 — etc.

This is the order in which the essay will be written, starting with an introduction that cleanly covers the
main ideas and how they will flow between each other; each chunk is then unpacked with supporting evidence
and statements. A messy chunk organisation makes a good essay unlikely; if even the plan looks bad, the
execution is unlikely to be better — and if it somehow is, it would undoubtedly be slower and less accurate.

### 4. Verbalise (remaining exam time, excluding review time)

Expand the skeleton outline into full sentences and paragraphs. This should be the easiest step, since what
to say and how supporting statements fit the bigger picture is already known. For essays with many points,
draft bullet points in the planned order first, then convert bullets into sentences (in a timed exam this
extra sub-step is often skipped, since exam essays tend to be shorter with fewer points). Difficulty at this
step specifically (with no other problems present) indicates a lower level of language mastery, requiring
more practice and interleaving to reach the desired fluency.

## Essay length by chunk count

| Level | Chunks | Sub-chunks | Points per chunk | Total discussion points | Essay length |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| High school | 3-5 | 0 or 1 | 2-4 | 12-20 | ~2,000 words |
| Early university | 5-7 | 1-2 | 3-5 | 20-40 | ~3,000 words |
| Late university | 5-7 | 1-3 | 4-7 | 40-70 | ~10,000 words |
| Academic publication or thesis | 5-10 | 2-4 | 5-10 | 50-100 | ~30,000-100,000+ words |

## Diagnostic table: problem felt → likely cause

| Problem felt | Likely cause(s) |
|---|---|
| Lacking evidence to support points, or feeling the need to waffle due to inadequate meaningful statements | Recall step inadequate, or the material was learned too superficially initially |
| Unsure how points recalled support major themes or conclusions | Inadequate chunking, or lack of relationship-based learning initially |
| Unsure how to navigate from point to point, or unclear on the essay's direction | Inadequate ordering of chunks or sub-chunks (can itself stem from inadequate chunking) |
| Sentences don't flow together naturally despite none of the above issues being present | Inadequate language mastery |

## BHS / WPW parallels

- **Chunk** step ≈ Skinning the mind map (see bear-hunter-system): same evaluate-level pruning and flow-check
  operation, applied to essay content instead of a map.
- **Order/linearisation** ≈ structuring and linearising WPW's teaching flow (../03-details/techniques/wpw.md):
  both convert a non-linear relational structure into a one-directional sequence for output (spoken teaching
  vs written essay).
- The whole four-step sequence assumes the deep, connected knowledge that BHS is intended to produce during
  preparation; ReCOVer does not build that knowledge, it structures its retrieval and expression under time
  pressure.
