# CLEAR feedback framework

## Purpose

CLEAR balances two competing needs in a feedback request: enough context for the situation to be
understood, and enough focus that the response is actionable rather than overwhelming. A quadrant
model captures the failure modes on either axis: narrow focus + low information = precise but
vague; narrow focus + high information = targeted skill improvement (the goal); broad focus + low
information = general overload; broad focus + high information = detailed but unfocused.

## The five steps

**Context.** Relevant background about the current situation, including the stage currently
reached in the program. Examples of relevant context: special scenarios likely to be encountered
(an upcoming exam or assessment) or personal difficulties (a hectic professional schedule, a
learning disability). This lets the feedback provider tailor their response to the actual
circumstances.

**Learning Goal.** The specific skill or process being aimed at, kept relevant to the current
stage. Example: "My goal is to practice interleaving skills and understand how I can improve my
teaching skills."

**Evaluate Your Work.** The longest and most detailed step. Self-reflection acts as a mirror,
making the eventual request more specific and focused. Four sub-foci: what was tried; what went
well vs. what was challenging; how this compared to expectations; where the single most important
area for improvement seems to be. **"I don't know" is flagged as an insufficient answer** — even
when the process feels confusing, a detailed reflection attempt is required, because self-
evaluation quality is itself a signal of insight and metacognition that can be more valuable to a
coach than the underlying work. This kind of self-evaluation is what research calls "sustainable
assessment": learning to judge one's own work rather than depending entirely on an external judge.

**Ask Targeted Questions.** Limited to **1–2 specific areas** — it is impossible to improve on
too many things at once, so this narrowing is necessary regardless of how many concerns actually
exist. If several areas feel like they need feedback, identify which are likely highest-yield.

**Review & Follow-up.** After receiving feedback, revisit and absorb the response; use this stage
to ask any clarifying follow-up questions.

## Closing the feedback loop

Receiving feedback is only the first step — real improvement comes from analysing feedback and
acting on it to produce new work ("closing the feedback loop": receive → analyse → implement →
produce new work → receive more feedback, cyclically).

3-step procedure:
1. **Read objectively** — read carefully without rushing to conclusions, especially when initial
   disagreement is felt; clarify confusions; stay emotionally detached (the feedback concerns the
   work, not the person's worth).
2. **Make an action plan** — once the feedback is understood, identify and prioritise key areas,
   and build a plan with specific steps.
3. **Test and review** — follow the action plan and run experiments on the changes made. Each
   round of feedback can generate multiple experiment ideas before more feedback is needed.

Follow-up cadence: complex situations may need **3–4 more experiments** before the next round of
external feedback is useful; simple situations can go **weeks** with independent marginal
improvement and no further feedback needed at all.

## Feedback sources

| Type | Source | Reliability / use case |
|---|---|---|
| Finder AI search | In-platform AI search, returns the best matching lesson/resource plus a summary | ~90% accuracy on the most common learner questions; recommended before seeking coach feedback |
| Coach feedback | Feedback from a learning coach on the platform | Highly reliable; requires acting on the advice to see improvement; can be time-consuming |
| Self-feedback | Self-reflection and self-evaluation of one's own performance | Reliability depends on the skill of the evaluator; once developed, the most overall valuable source, since it enables rapid, externally-independent improvement cycles; recommended as the first response in every situation |

## Detailed worked example

**Context.** Preparing for a second-year undergraduate history exam due in 3 months, using
interleaved retrieval, currently at the Fundamentals 2 stage.

**Learning Goal.** Improve retrieval skills for the upcoming exam.

**Evaluate Your Work.** Tried a combination of re-reading notes, creating summary pages, doing
practice questions from a textbook, and quizzing friends. Generally strong on big-picture ideas,
struggling with small details (specific dates). Some concepts and details are forgotten
inconsistently — sometimes recalled, sometimes not. Overall retention is about 70%, with most
forgetting concentrated in the details. Unsure which part of the retrieval practice is the
bottleneck, but suspects too much time is spent re-reading notes, leaving too little time for
practice questions or methods like flashcards.

**Ask Targeted Questions.** "What can I do to improve my recall of fine details without
compromising my big-picture understanding?" "Do you think my self-assessment is correct, or is
there something I'm missing?"

**Closing the loop (after receiving feedback).** Read objectively: carefully reviewed the
feedback and asked clarifying questions where unclear. Stayed detached and focused: despite the
volume of feedback and the challenge of learning that some methods had been mistaken, focused on
turning suggestions into an action plan rather than treating the feedback as a judgment of
ability. Made an action plan: removed re-reading; set aside 30 minutes/day for high-yield
flashcards; replaced summary pages with summary mindmaps to preserve the big-picture benefit in
less time. Reflected and experimented over the following week: found 30 minutes insufficient
(extended to 45) and the mindmaps too difficult on their own (switched to a hybrid of linear
summary notes plus mini-mindmaps) to balance benefit against time-efficiency ahead of the exam.
