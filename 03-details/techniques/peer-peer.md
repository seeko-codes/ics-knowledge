## Overview

PEER-Peer is a core-pillar habit-creation system, built directly on the cue-response-reward anatomy of a habit (see `habit-formation.md`). It has a low learning curve, is quick to set up, and produces visible benefits immediately. It is described as capable of removing procrastination entirely when applied consistently.

## The five steps

### P — Prep

Cue optimisation: identify the cues that trigger the undesired response, remove them, and adopt/integrate cues that trigger the desired response instead.

- **Bad-cue removal examples**: deleting a social-media app icon or browsing in incognito mode to remove the "seeing the icon" cue; placing a phone at the bottom of a drawer or handing it to someone else to remove the "seeing the phone" cue.
- **Good-cue examples**: leaving books open to the correct page (arranged the night before) as a study cue; hanging running shoes on a doorknob as an exercise cue.
- **Iteration criterion**: cue optimisation is rarely ineffective when repeatedly optimised — if a first attempt does not produce the desired behaviour, optimise again rather than concluding the approach doesn't work. This step draws on nudge theory: environmental cues influence behaviour more than willpower, and successful, consistently productive people rely on environment design over willpower as the primary lever.

### E — Easy

Set a **minimum viable goal (MVG)** — the smallest possible action step, with motivation cost as close to zero as possible.

- **Criterion for correct sizing**: if procrastination is a real problem, even "stand up" or "sit at the desk" is an acceptable MVG. Students commonly set MVGs too difficult; the fix is to shrink the MVG further, not to add willpower.

### E — Exit

At each MVG, give explicit permission to quit at any point. This keeps the brain focused on the immediately achievable MVG, rather than being scared off by the size of the eventual full goal.

### R — Reward

Set a reward for attaining a goal.

- **Criteria**: the reward should require a minimum amount of work to grant, and the delay before granting it should be maximised — the closer to the final goal before rewarding, the better. What counts as "good enough" to earn the reward is left to individual judgment.
- **Failure mode and remedy**: relying on willpower alone to avoid taking the reward prematurely tends to fail. Physically locking the reward away (with the help of others if needed) is more reliable than willpower-based restraint.

### Peer

External accountability, using another person (family, friend, or a community member) as a check — the same mechanism used in the burnt ships strategy (see `beds-m.md`).

- **Automatisation note**: once a behaviour has become a fully automatic habit, the Peer step is no longer necessary. Accountability usage decreases as behaviours automatise, meaning the system requires progressively less external scaffolding over time — this is described as the best feature of the system.

## Sequencing note

The steps are named in P-E-E-R-Peer order but are not a strict linear checklist executed once — Prep is iterated until effective, and Peer is the step most likely to be phased out first as a given habit stabilises.
