## Beginner's Mindmap Guide (build procedure)

1. Identify the biggest chunks. These will be hypothetical at first. There are usually 3 to 7 for
   a single topic.
2. Arrange the chunks and their most important contained concepts so the relationship and flow
   between them is expressed.
3. Identify sub-concepts and sub-chunks and add them to the map. Always think about how each new
   chunk or concept fits into the big picture.
4. Add more details until the topic is complete. Information that is highly specific and not
   related to other parts of the map goes to memorisation techniques such as flashcards, instead
   of the map.

General tips: (a) when arranging the first chunks, leave big spaces between them so there is room
for sub-chunks and other concepts; (b) make the main flow of relationships between the largest
chunks — the "backbone" — visually very clear; (c) relate each new item to the bigger picture as
it is added; (d) constantly refine and revise the structure; do not over-commit to the first
attempt — "everything is just a hypothesis."

## Correctness self-check (not a build step — a diagnostic)

A mind map is being built correctly when the learner:

1. finds it easier to think of new relationships and chunks by seeing what has already been
   written down;
2. is able to reflect on how each individual piece connects to the big picture;
3. finds it feels messy and confusing at first, but over time — as the mind map becomes more
   organised and accurate — the underlying knowledge also feels more organised and accurate;
4. continuously refines and updates the map (indicating sustained big-picture thinking and
   hypothesis-holding rather than premature commitment);
5. constantly engages in higher-order thinking: analysing (finding similarities and differences)
   and evaluating (prioritising and making value judgments).

## Chunking-by-importance worked example (cell biology)

Sample source excerpt: epithelial **cell types** (Squamous, Cuboidal, Columnar — each with a
description and function) crossed with **cell arrangements** (Stratified, Simple,
Pseudostratified — each with a description and function), yielding four **important epithelial
linings** (Stratified Squamous; Simple Cuboidal; Simple Columnar; Pseudostratified Columnar),
each already given a function in the source text.

- **Initial mindmap attempt** (figure: image-1.png, "red mind map") — chunked by textbook order
  (types vs. arrangements), showing "slight spiderwebbing" where multiple relationships between
  multiple concepts look messy and hard to follow.
- **Alternative mindmap** (figure: image_0.png, "blue mind map with pictures") — re-chunked by
  *why* the cell types and arrangements are important: since the important linings are simply
  examples of different type × arrangement combinations, chunking by shared **function** produces
  a clearer, easier-to-follow, more intuitive structure while representing the same underlying
  information.

Key principle: often, the best chunk structure comes from seeing the topic from a different
perspective to how it was originally taught. As a general rule, a missing perspective or simpler
way is always assumed to exist; persistence until it is found is part of the technique, however
difficult. Domain-dependence: in this biology example, function came first; but
function is not always the right axis — in chemistry, importance is more often reaction mechanism
or reactivity, not function; in English, importance ties to the author's perspective, background,
or worldview. "This is why the question is 'Why is it important?' and not 'What are their shared
functions?'"

## Immediate vs. root reason, and the "So what?" approach

A *root reason* is the fundamental reason something is important, as distinct from the
*immediate* (first, surface-level) reason. Illustrated with Seth Godin's drill-bit story: people
buying drill bits don't ultimately want the hole in the wall (immediate reason) — they want the
screw that goes in the hole, and beyond that the picture that hangs on the screw, and beyond that
the feeling of seeing that picture on the wall (root reason). Figure: pexels-pixabay-47091.jpg
(drill-bit close-up, illustrating the analogy).

Applied to learning: for genetic diversity, the immediate reason given is usually "it leads to
variation." Asking "why is variation important?" surfaces the root reason: variation is essential
for the adaptability, resilience, and long-term survival of species and ecosystems. The resulting
chunk should therefore be renamed "survival," not "variation" — root-reason chunks are broader,
encompass more sub-concepts, and are easier to form relationships to and from than immediate-
reason chunks. Figure: pexels-google-deepmind-18069421.jpg (DNA double-helix, illustrating the
genetic-diversity example).

**"So what?" approach**: after finding a reason for importance, repeatedly ask "So what?" until
the question no longer makes sense to ask — at that point, the root reason has presumably been
reached.

## 2-4 Rule application procedure

The 2-4 Rule is an intermediate-level process promoting higher-order (Evaluate-level) thinking
while chunking:

1. Always aim to have two branches coming off each node, and never have more than four.
2. When four or more branches come out of a node, form a new (sub-)chunk.
3. When fewer than two concepts come from a node, merge or re-chunk (**concatenate**).

Definitions: a **single-node chain** is a chunk or concept linking from exactly one chunk/concept
to exactly one other chunk/concept (one inbound, one outbound connection) — single-node chains
indicate the structure can usually be optimised, since these nodes carry fewer relationships and
are weak points at risk of lower retention. A **terminal node** is a single-node chain's endpoint
with no further outbound connections — "like the leaves of a tree."

### Worked example: "Consequences in India" (British colonisation)

Four iterations, each with a before/after figure pair (India chunking example 1.jpeg through
7.jpeg):

1. **More than four**: six points originally branch off "Consequences in India" (figure: India
   chunking example 1.jpeg) → re-chunked into two sub-chunks of three points each (figure: India
   chunking example 2.jpeg), which also surfaces a new lateral relationship between the
   sub-chunks that would otherwise have caused spiderwebbing.
2. **Fewer than two / single-node chain**: "British colonisation" links only from one chunk to
   one chunk — "Consequences in India" (figure: India chunking example 3.jpeg) → removed
   entirely, replaced with a direct "Drivers" → "Consequences" flow, since the whole topic is
   already about British colonisation and drivers/consequences are unambiguous without the
   intermediate node (figure: India chunking example 4.jpeg); "economic gain" is re-chunked to
   become one of the "Drivers."
3. **Rule reapplied dynamically**: once multiple additional drivers (political, religious) are
   found, "economic gain" is reintroduced as a sub-chunk alongside them under "Drivers" (figure:
   India chunking example 5.jpeg) — demonstrating the rule is applied dynamically in both
   directions, not just as a one-way collapse.
4. **Fewer than two, with a terminal node**: "Famine" → "malnourishment" is a single-node chain,
   and "malnourishment" has no further outbound connections, making it a terminal node (figure:
   India chunking example 6.jpeg, boxed in red) → concatenated by merging "malnourishment"
   directly under "Famine," with "Poverty" and "working conditions" moved to give more space, and
   a visualisation of rotting meat added at the concatenation point as a memory anchor (figure:
   India chunking example 7.jpeg).

Key message ("Persistence is crucial"): rechunking to abide by the 2-4 Rule can involve
substantial cognitive effort; giving up because it is difficult sacrifices the quality of
learning; persistence gets easier and faster with practice. General effect: continual chunking,
sub-chunking, and re-chunking frees cognitive and physical map space for additional
relationships, improving the spatial arrangement of the map — more chunks means fewer isolated
points, which means fewer arrows and a less complicated map, without sacrificing the underlying
connections, since the chunks remain connected.

## Intuitive chunking

Chunks are "intuitive" when they leverage prior knowledge — the more a chunk relies on prior
knowledge, the more logical it feels and the less memorisation is required. Comparing chunk
structures and deciding which one makes the most sense is itself an act of higher-order learning.
There is no single "right answer" to search for; the goal is the answer that best fits the
learner's own knowledge schema. Rather than settling on a chunk structure without evaluating
alternatives, look for opportunities to **reverse-chunk** so the chunking stays genuinely
importance-based. Diagnostic relationship: correctly performed importance-based chunking produces
intuitive chunks, so checking a chunk structure's intuitiveness is a way of checking whether
importance-based chunking was actually performed.

Meta-checklist (feeling → cognitive outcome):

| How it can feel | Cognitive outcome |
|---|---|
| Overwhelmed, similar to first learning the Aim step, because of having to think about the best way to represent and label chunks | Forces prioritisation and evaluation at a higher level than previous versions of the technique; creates deeper, more memorable structures |
| Suddenly slower and less fluent than before | The standard for high-quality chunking and encoding has risen; this eases with practice |
