## Key principles (inquiry-quality checklist)

Correct inquiry-based learning, at any stage (TLS or Aim), must satisfy all of the following:

- Questions asked must help find relationships.
- Answering a question must force evaluation of the importance of information and value
  judgments.
- The process must require genuine mental effort.
- Information must always be connected to a big picture.
- Information must always form a network.
- The network must be logical and make sense — being connected is not sufficient; the
  connection must be meaningful.

This checklist can be applied directly against an inquiry session to test whether it is
functioning as intended. Anything that prevents multiple relationships from being found, or that
avoids the need to make a value judgment about relationship importance, reduces the quality of
learning — this is named as one of the most common causes of ineffective chunking or reduced-
quality encoding.

## Question-yield ranking

Question types are not interchangeable; they differ systematically in how much relational,
higher-order processing they force.

1. **"Why is X important?" — highest yield.** Forces examination of a fact or concept in
   relation to other facts and concepts *and* forces a judgment on how important that
   relationship is. This dual demand (find a relationship, then evaluate it) is what facilitates
   higher-order learning and improves retention and depth of understanding. The question's value
   diminishes sharply if answered with only a single relationship: the purpose of the question is
   to find *multiple* relationships and judge the importance of each; needing to rationalise and
   justify that judgment is what forces the higher-order work and produces a robust knowledge
   network. "What is the purpose of X?" is treated as the same question reworded.
2. **Relationship/dependency questions ("how does X affect Y?", "how are X and Y different?") —
   medium yield.** These directly probe relationships, interactions, dependencies, and
   influences between concepts, and are effective at building relationships. They are weaker than
   "why is it important?" because the resulting relationship is built between only two (or maybe
   three) concepts, whereas "why is it important?" tends to invoke multiple concepts
   simultaneously as a big picture.
3. **"How does X occur?" / process questions — low yield, near "what."** This is equivalent to
   "what is the process?" and does not put the information in context with other concepts. It
   risks collapsing into pure process memorisation.

## Examples-not-sufficient rule

Asking for examples of a concept is usually not a sufficient substitute for asking why the
concept is important — even when the examples given happen to touch on reasons for importance.
Examples tend to be lower-order and do not reliably improve the relationships formed between
other concepts; they also may not help chunk information well, because they do not sufficiently
surface the various similarities between concepts that chunking depends on.

## Emotional/curiosity binding

Questions that are emotional, personal, or that spark genuine curiosity connect information much
more strongly than neutral questions. Since the objective is to enter a problem-solving mindset,
questions should not be asked about problems whose answer is already known — this is not inquiry.
Genuine curiosity about the topic generates more knowledge than manufactured or rhetorical
questioning.

## Multi-relationship requirement

Answering a question with a single, simple, direct answer closes off the multi-relational,
higher-order evaluation that questioning exists to produce. This failure pattern is named "simple
question and answer": a common way learners avoid the discomfort of deeper processing by subtly
modifying their inquiry-based-learning technique into something that only superficially resembles
it. Correct execution requires generating multiple possible relationships per question before
judging which are worth keeping.

## Purpose ≈ importance equivalence

"What is the purpose of X?" and "Why is X important?" function as the same question — different
wording of the same high-yield prompt, both pushing toward evaluation of importance against the
big picture.

## How-occur ≈ process warning

"How does X occur?" is functionally identical to "What is the process?" — neither question
requires the information to be considered in context with other concepts, and both risk becoming
exercises in pure process memorisation rather than relationship-building.

## Common issues (operational checklist for diagnosing weak inquiry)

- Questions are answered with a simple, direct answer instead of being used to identify multiple
  relationships for evaluation.
- Questions are too "what"-based: isolating, and not requiring examination of relationships
  between pieces of information.
- Questions neglect to ask "why is it important?" — named as one of the most critical questions
  for rapid chunk formation and key-relationship identification.
- Questions are asked only of individual concepts, never of the chunks that have already been
  identified.

## Signs and symptoms of incorrect inquiry

- Information is retained less and recall is slower.
- Curveball exam questions become harder to answer, because ideas were not related to other
  ideas during study — variations are harder to synthesise and conceptualise, which is exactly
  what curveball questions test.
- Mindmaps appear disorganised, because the backbone of concepts and logic was not formed
  strongly — either because questions were not asked of the largest chunks, or for the reasons
  above.

Note on scope: identifying precisely where an individual's inquiry-based learning is going wrong
is often nuanced and subtle, with many possible variations and solutions; a single article cannot
enumerate all of them (source: ics-learning-support/encoding/90.md).

## Seven-question troubleshooting template (subject-agnostic; surfaced in the mathematics clinic)

A concrete question template for learning any pair of related items A and B (crowd-sourced in
Live Clinic 41, preserved in its chapter record; the surrounding troubleshooting session is
[video-only: not captured] — ics-course/04-supplementary/01-live-clinics/35-lc41.md):

1. Why is A important?
2. What functions does A serve?
3. When is A used?
4. Why is B important?
5. What functions does B serve?
6. When is B used?
7. How does B relate to A?

The template operationalises the question-yield ranking above for procedural-heavy subjects
(mathematics being the clinic's case): importance, function, and use-condition questions build
the higher-order declarative context that pure procedure practice lacks.
