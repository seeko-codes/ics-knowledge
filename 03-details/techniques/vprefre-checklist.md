# VPReFRE Checklist

VPReFRE is the six-component quality checklist for note-taking: Visual, Processed, Relational,
Freehand, Reflective, Efficient. A mind map done correctly will satisfy this checklist.
Key principles stated across the lesson: the method used to take notes matters less
than how the learner is thinking while taking them; good note-taking reduces unhelpful and
extraneous cognitive load; bad note-taking causes avoidance of thinking; wordier notes are
usually less processed and less effective, and typing exacerbates this because it is easier to
produce high volumes of unprocessed notes than freehand writing allows.

## V — Visual

Reduce the amount of words used and increase visuals: pictures, symbols, doodles, shorthand,
diagrams. Rationale: visuals greatly help retention because they incorporate multiple modes of
learning, and they act as landmarks in memory; they also make notes more personal and engaging.

Key principle: as with all note-taking, what is produced is less important than the processes
used to produce it. Even if other people would not understand a given doodle, its creator
retains a deep and meaningful connection to it because of the cognitive process of imagining and
creating it.

## P — Processed

Process information as a **problem**, by **asking questions** (this is the direct tie to the Aim
step of the Bear Hunter System). Visuals also aid processing.

## Re — Relational

Do not focus on remembering individual pieces of information in isolation; always relate
information to other information, via mind maps. Reading linearly means the brain does not
process the information as effectively, and anything that goes in quickly goes back out.
Retention dramatically increases when the relationships *between* pieces of information are
strengthened, rather than when individual pieces are strengthened in isolation.

## F — Freehand

Typing is "too single-minded" and does not encourage much thinking. Freehand writing forces more
processing of the information and allows more visual representation; typed documents represent
relationships poorly by comparison.

## R — Reflective

Take time to reflect on notes and check that they are not laid out in the same order as the
source resource (e.g. a textbook). Learners are used to working start-to-finish in presented
order, but this is not how the brain learns; reflect to check whether notes are ordered in a way
that makes sense to the learner's own brain, not linearly.

## E — Efficient

A check for understanding: does the note-taking allow a lot of learning within the amount of
study time spent? The explicit efficiency threshold: **forgetting more than 30% of the
information written in one's notes indicates something wrong with how the previous points of
VPReFRE are being applied.** Key principle: effective note-taking should help the
brain *learn*, not help it *avoid* learning — the final check is whether the information is being
retained, used, and applied; if not, something in the note-taking process upstream (V, P, Re, or
F) needs to change.

## Evidence references

- **Freehand**: Oppenheimer's "The Pen is Mightier Than the Keyboard" is cited as one of the most
  famous supporting studies, with the explicit caveat that it does not conclusively explain *why*
  freehand offers its advantages; cognitive load theory and multimodal learning theory are
  offered as more robust explanatory frameworks. An additional reference block (~9 studies)
  supports freehand expression combined with visualisation (Fuson 2019; Jaeger, Velazquez,
  Dawdanow & Shipley 2018; Kantrowitz, Fava & Brew 2017; Schwamborn, Thillmann, Leutner, Mayer &
  Leopold 2010; Sun, Xiang, Yang, Yang & Lou 2014; Tadayon & Afhami 2017; Wammes, Jonker &
  Fernandes 2019; Wu & Rau 2019).
- **Visual / learning-styles myth**: a separate reference block establishes that learning-styles
  theory (matching instruction to a visual/auditory/kinesthetic preference) is largely
  unsupported or mythical (APA press release 2019; The Atlantic 2018; Conyers & Wilson 2020;
  Deale 2019; Kraemer, Rosenberg & Thompson-Schill 2009; Moussa-Inaty, Atallah & Causapin 2019;
  Nancekivell, Shah & Gelman 2020; Tovar-Moll & Lent 2016), while multimodal learning itself
  remains evidence-supported.
- **Hemisphere-myth correction** ("Lesson correction," appended to the Visual sublesson): the
  accompanying video's claim that the dominant hemisphere handles rational thinking and the
  non-dominant hemisphere handles abstract thinking is flagged as not well supported by current
  research. What is established: different brain regions have different functions (large body of
  imaging studies); hemispheres show no relationship to personality; sensory and motor
  hemispheric dominance is well established, but cognitive/information-processing dominance is
  not; a general hemisphere preference has been demonstrated, but other brain areas also activate
  in ways inconsistent with a strict lateralisation theory. Practical conclusion: it may not
  matter whether the hemispheric explanation is correct, since multimodal learning has a
  demonstrated benefit regardless of the underlying mechanism.

## Note-taking app criteria (mechanism-relevant only)

A note-taking app should: (1) facilitate the correct cognitive processes for learning (most
important) — concretely, an infinite canvas plus convenient, fast freehand drawing with a digital
stylus; (2) provide good organisational features for managing multiple notes (less important).
Most commercial note-taking apps were designed based on what would sell rather than on learning
research, which is why many of them are restrictive for this purpose. (The source includes an
extensive per-app product comparison — Evernote, Notion, Notability, OneNote, Freeform, Concepts,
Squid, Leonardo, Explain Everything — which is commercial/product-recommendation content, not
mechanism, and is not reproduced here.)
