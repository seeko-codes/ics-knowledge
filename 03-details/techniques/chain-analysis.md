## Overview

Chain analysis is a clinical-psychology technique for finding the causes of a response or emotion, used by clinical psychologists to address entrenched behaviour and neuroticism, and described as a versatile life skill worth mastering generally. The version below is one simple, effective form a psychologist might use.

## The six steps

### 1. Target behaviour

What is the specific behaviour to change? Examples given: procrastinating, over-eating, spending too much time on social media, binge-watching, or emotional-coping behaviours such as getting angry and yelling at someone.

### 2. Prompting event

What event sparked the chain leading to the target behaviour? Be as specific as possible.

### 3. Vulnerability factors

What made the individual vulnerable to the prompting event? This may include state of mind, mood, preceding events, or habits that caused the event to have more effect than it would have on another day or for someone else.

### 4. Links

What happened between the prompting event and the target behaviour — the links in the chain?

- What happened? How did the individual behave? How did they react?
- How did they feel at each point? How did the feelings evolve across the links?
- What thoughts occurred after the prompting event and before the target behaviour?

Be as specific as possible.

### 5. Consequences

What were the short-term and long-term consequences of the behaviour?

### 6. Solutions

Four sub-categories of potential solution:

- **Exposure**: how can exposure to the prompting event, or to similar events, be limited? If that isn't feasible, how can exposure to the vulnerability factors be limited instead?
- **Skills**: what skills can be developed or used to mitigate vulnerability factors, reduce exposure, modify/influence the events, behaviours, emotions and thoughts, or manage the consequences?
- **Cognitive modifications**: how can the mindset or perspective on the event be changed so it has less emotional/mental impact? How will this be remembered the next time a similar trigger arises? Even without direct skill to resolve a problem, changing how it is perceived can substantially reduce its impact. Example: anxiety often worsens when a person is actively focused on *not* being anxious — an anxiety spiral that can trigger panic attacks. A cognitive modification that acknowledges the anxiety, appreciates the brain's protective intent, but reassures that there is no real present danger, is an effective technique for interrupting this spiral.
- **Contingency management**: if all previous steps fail, what can reduce the short- and long-term consequences? How can future attempts at the earlier steps be made more successful — e.g., stronger reminders, or a clearer structure/system?

## Application note

Chain analysis is presented as optional rather than mandatory: it is recommended specifically for learners who struggle with neuroticism, and its use is left to individual judgment rather than assigned as a universal task.
