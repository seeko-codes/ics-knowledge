## Overview

An intermediate time-management and prioritisation technique combining a values/vision-level layer (Priority 0) with a daily/weekly task-level layer (Priority 1, via a modified Eisenhower Matrix).

## Priority myths corrected first

- **Myth 1 — working faster increases productivity**: the brain is not a factory; forcing faster performance decreases quality and increases errors. Some error from genuine learning is unavoidable, but unnecessary/unproductive errors from rushing should still be minimised.
- **Myth 2 — trying harder increases productivity**: intense effort often leaves no mental space, time, or perspective to check whether the actions taken are actually correct — effort is spent without noticing it isn't moving toward the goal. Activities that look unproductive (e.g., relaxing to maintain mental health) can in fact improve productivity.
- **Key principle**: hard work can prevent smart work. Working smarter requires time and mental freedom; if a performance standard is only reachable through high effort, the only option is to accept a short-term loss in order to create room to work smarter.

## Procedure

1. Look at current goals and identify the top priority — use reverse goal-setting principles to identify this (see `reverse-goal-setting.md`).
2. Block out time to accomplish the top priority.
3. Fill the rest of the timetable according to lower priorities.
4. In the case of unforeseen events, shuffle lower-priority items to protect the highest-priority task.

## Eisenhower Matrix use

A modified Eisenhower Matrix categorises tasks by importance and urgency to determine priorities. Recommended cadence: prioritise and schedule tasks **the day before**; on the day itself, keep a running list of newly emergent tasks and feed them into the matrix for the next day's scheduling.

Key prioritisation principles:

- Decide whether a task is important based on the **consequence of failure**, not an intuitive feeling of importance (which is often actually urgency).
- A task with a low, acceptable, manageable consequence of failure is not important, even if it "feels" important.
- Avoid scheduling urgent tasks first — this perpetuates urgency trapping, where time is constantly consumed by urgent tasks and the future self is never better equipped to handle them or remove the workload at its root cause.

## Priority 0

Priority 0 refers to core **values and long-term vision**, reassessed once every **6 months**. Regular reassessment ensures Priority 1 tasks stay aligned with the underlying values — a step described as low-cost and high-payoff for staying on track.

## FAQ content

**What counts as urgency trapping?** A common student pattern: filling all available time with urgent homework and never developing underlying study skills. If academic challenges grow faster than skills, the gap between skill and challenge widens continuously — by the time the gap is felt as urgent, it is often already too late to close in time. The remedy is not to schedule urgent tasks later in the day; it is to schedule important-and-non-urgent tasks *first* and fit urgent tasks around them (example: a fixed 20-minute evening meditation block scheduled first, with the rest of the afternoon filled around it, so the urgent tasks never crowd out the important one).

**How is Priority 0 identified?** Priority 0 comes down to core values (fundamental beliefs dictating behaviour and action) and long-term vision (an open, abstract idea of the kind of person and life one wants).

**What distinguishes a long-term vision from a long-term goal?** A long-term vision is *not* a SMART goal. Setting goals that are too long-term risks tunnel vision, since personality and priorities are near-guaranteed to change over time — especially before age 30, when many transformative first experiences occur. A vision is instead an open, abstract idea of the person and life wanted, functioning as a broad calibration checklist for medium-term goals: broad enough to avoid closing off opportunities, but specific enough to keep direction consistent. Example vision: financial freedom, an intellectually stimulating job with enough free time for multiple hobbies, and being patient, calm, confident, and able to adapt to and overcome challenges enjoyably. A medium-term goal that doesn't serve this vision (e.g., relying on tutoring to raise grades, which builds no independent challenge-overcoming skill and no additional free time) is unlikely to be a good decision under this vision, even if it produces a short-term result.

**How often does Priority 0 change?** It should always be evaluated open-mindedly rather than assumed unchanged — it is tempting and comfortable to believe a long-term vision never changes, but this is unlikely for a living, dynamic person continually taking in new information about the world and themself. The recommended approach is to deliberately look for reasons, thoughts, or emotions indicating the vision *has* changed, rather than assuming continuity by default; this counters the natural cognitive bias toward assuming stability. Recommended review cadence: every 6 months.

**How often does Priority 1 change?** Repeated once every day or week depending on schedule variability — busier people with more moving parts need to repeat it daily.

**How can one be confident enough to delete a task?** If a task is truly important, it will return to the list on its own. Barring ample free time and willingness to spend it on the task over relaxation, sleep, or other non-urgent-important work, it is not advisable to spend time on it now.
