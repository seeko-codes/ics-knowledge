## The 2-4 Rule

An intermediate-level structural discipline that promotes evaluate-level thinking while
chunking. Full statement:

1. Always aim for two branches coming off each node; never have more than four.
2. When four or more branches come out of an information node, form a new (sub-)chunk from the
   excess.
3. When fewer than two concepts come from a node, merge or re-chunk it (concatenate — see
   below).

The rule is applied continuously and dynamically, not once: a chunk that was correctly merged
away can be legitimately reintroduced later if enough new material accumulates under it to
justify a new chunk again (e.g. an "economic gain" chunk removed for being a single-node chain
can return once political and religious drivers are also found, since "Drivers" would then
exceed four branches without it — see the worked India/British-colonisation sequence in
chunking-worked-examples.md).

**Rechunking operation — sub-chunk at ≥4.** When a node has four or more branches, group the
excess branches into one or more new sub-chunks. This can surface a new lateral relationship
between the resulting sub-chunks that would otherwise have created spiderwebbing if left as
loose individual branches.

**Rechunking operation — merge/concatenate at <2 (single-node chains and terminal nodes).** A
node with exactly one inbound and one outbound connection is a single-node chain: it clutters
the map without adding organisational value and is a weak point at elevated risk of low
retention. If a single-node chain's outbound connection leads nowhere further, its endpoint is a
terminal node (a leaf). Concatenation procedure: remove the intermediate single-node chain from
the flow and connect its neighbours directly to each other (e.g. "British colonisation," a
single-node chain between "Drivers" and "Consequences," is removed so the map reads "Drivers →
Consequences" directly — justified because the whole topic is already about British colonisation,
so nothing is lost by not stating it explicitly on the map). Where the chain ends in a terminal
node, concatenate the terminal node directly into its predecessor instead of leaving it as a
separate point (e.g. "malnourishment," a terminal node under "Famine," is folded directly under
"Famine"). A visual memory anchor can be added at the concatenation point to compensate for the
detail no longer having its own dedicated node (e.g. an image of rotting meat added at the
Famine → malnourishment concatenation).

**Rechunking operation — re-chunk around a high-connection node.** When many new lateral
relationships have been found and the map has become disorganised (spiderwebbing), identify
whichever chunk or concept has accumulated the highest density of connections and hypothesise a
new overall structure organised around that node instead of around the original topic name (the
retinal-detachment example re-chunks around "Types" once it becomes the highest-connection node;
see chunking-worked-examples.md).

**Persistence requirement.** Re-chunking to satisfy the 2-4 Rule can require substantial
cognitive effort. Giving up because it is difficult sacrifices learning quality; the rule
explicitly expects this to become easier and faster with continued practice, not to stay easy
from the start.

**Effect on spatial structure.** Correct application produces fewer isolated points and
therefore fewer required arrows, without sacrificing any of the relationships found — they end
up expressed through the organisational structure itself (chunk placement and hierarchy) rather
than through extra lines.

## Reverse-chunking procedure

Reverse-chunking tests whether a chunk structure is genuinely importance-based (see
importance-based-chunking.md, Intuitive and reverse chunking) rather than merely correct-looking.

1. Look across the existing chunk structure for a specific feature, characteristic, or fact that
   **repeats** across multiple different chunks in layer 2 or 3.
2. Hypothesise an alternative structure in which that repeated element becomes the **name of a
   new chunk**.
3. Move the concepts that previously held the repeated element under the new chunk, as
   sub-chunks or details of it.
4. Evaluate whether the resulting flow is simpler and more intuitive than the original. If it is,
   adopt it — the new structure often expresses a more original, unique perspective on the
   material while retaining accuracy.

**When not to reverse-chunk.** Repetition sometimes persists no matter how the material is
chunked. Reverse-chunking is not mandatory in that case; what matters is that alternative
structures were genuinely *evaluated*, not that one structure is assumed best without the
comparison being made.

## Prechunking conditions

Prechunking is forming chunks before inquiry begins. It is hypothetical-only by design; the
corpus states three explicit conditions governing when it is acceptable to use at all:

1. Acceptable only if the resulting chunks remain hypotheses and continue to be actively modified
   through inquiry — never if they are assumed correct/final.
2. Avoid it if there is any difficulty breaking away from chunking in the same way information is
   presented by the source (i.e. weak order control) — prechunking will reinforce, not correct,
   that weakness.
3. Avoid it entirely if spiderwebbing is occurring, or if the Aim step generally feels
   ineffective — both are signs the chunking process needs more, not less, ground-up inquiry.

A stronger, later restatement of condition 2: because framing bias can be powerful, learners who
specifically struggle to break free from a prechunked structure once it exists are advised to
avoid prechunking altogether for that material and form chunks only after deliberate inquiry.
