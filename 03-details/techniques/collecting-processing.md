## Framing

"Collecting and processing is a transitionary technique, meaning we use it to transition between
different strategies. In this case, we use collecting and processing to transition from heavily
linear notes to processed notes. It helps learners spend more time thinking and analysing the
information to break the habit of passive, mindless note-taking." Rationale: writing information
down is not the same as learning it — the brain does not learn simply because something was
transcribed, which is why a separate processing step is required.

## Step 1: Collecting

Make the information as concise as possible. Include only keywords; remove words that are not
needed (e.g. "the," "and"). Use symbols such as → or + to represent words or meanings. This step
is easy and "does not activate any learning" — it is purely a setup step for processing.

## Step 2: Processing

Think about the information to make sense of it and simplify it. Represent the information in the
simplest way possible, bringing together as many concepts as possible. Move from collecting to
processing after every concept, so a more extensive network is progressively built — as
processing continues, the "web" or "network" of knowledge should grow through more relationships,
arrows, and groups. Do not be afraid to oversimplify: the details always remain available on the
left in the collection column; the processing side should focus on representing the main ideas as
effectively as possible.

Important note: processing notes is a core learning skill, and the skills/methods learned here
are expected to carry over to all later note-taking, including future "mindmaps (or more
accurately, chunkmaps)" — those are still expected to be processed, not just drawn.

## Grouping and creating relationships

Grouping and creating relationships is described as one of the most important skills for
improving learning efficiency and deep processing — the thought processes involved are
crucial for high-quality encoding.

Baseline self-questions: How can I group this information? Does it still make sense? What does it
relate to? Why is this important? How does it fit into the big picture?

More advanced prioritising self-questions, used once grouping is under way: What are other ways
that these concepts are similar to each other? What wider purpose do these concepts have? Why do
I need to know this — why is this relationship important? What is the simplest way I can group
these ideas together while keeping the relationships accurate?

Grouping activates the *evaluate* level of Bloom's revised taxonomy (figure: Bloom's simplified
version). Making a value judgment on the importance/relevance of information is ultimately
subjective, but "the time and effort involved in making this judgment directly contributes to
effective learning." Uncertainty about which grouping makes the most sense is normal; the
resulting back-and-forth process — think, question, seek answers, consolidate — *is* what
higher-order learning feels like, and can differ greatly from the feel of prior, mostly
lower-order habits.

## Groups-superior-to-images correction

This "Notes and Processing" sublesson explicitly supersedes the same file's own earlier
visualisation-tips sublesson: "creating groups and relationships is superior to simply creating
an image." The point is demonstrated with a chemistry example: converting reaction equations
(Metal + acid = salt + hydrogen gas; Metal + water = metal hydroxide + hydrogen gas) into repeated
1:1 word-substitute images is "hieroglyphics" — no more memorable than the words themselves,
because repetition strips the images of distinctiveness (compared to trying to remember every
streetlamp in a city, rather than a landmark). The corrective is that such information "is better
learned through good logic and a deeply processed understanding of the concepts. Relying on a
superficial memory cue like this image is less efficient." Visualisations and memory cues can
still *reinforce* information — but only once it has already been processed and grouped; they are
not a substitute for grouping.

## Adjustment timeframe

For learners new to non-linear note-taking, gradually changing note-taking style over roughly 2–3
weeks is the recommended pace, alongside practising other overlapping techniques (particularly
in-class technique) in the meantime.

## Relation to the Cornell method and the sophistication ladder

Collecting/Processing "shares similarities with the well-known Cornell Note-Taking Method." The
Cornell method is also a beginner-level technique, but is more limited and does not itself
transition to more efficient note-taking; it reflects a more outdated approach to note-taking —
superior to the typical linear methods that most learners still use but less effective than
the non-linear methods taught later in the program. Recorded sophistication ladder: linear (worst) <
Cornell (beginner) < Collecting/Processing (transitionary) < non-linear processed note-taking
(canonical, VPReFRE) < GRINDE (map-specific final form, at the Skin step).
