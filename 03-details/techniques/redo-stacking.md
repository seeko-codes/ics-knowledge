## Stacking, defined

Stacking means making improvements that **add value and build on existing progress**. A gain
"stacks" if it contributes productively to skill development in a way that carries forward what
was already achieved; a gain that does not build on prior progress does not stack, even if it is
itself a genuine 1% improvement in isolation.

## The REDO framework

1. **Reflect** — What was learned from previous attempts? Look for trends across multiple
   attempts. Ask: "What might the next 1% gain look like?"
2. **Evaluate** — Prioritise a direction: *unblock* (switch to a different skill) or *unlock*
   (more experimentation, theory, or reflection on the current skill).
3. **Define** — Identify what a 1% improvement in the chosen direction would look like. Early
   improvements often mean simply finding mistakes, before performance gains become visible.
   Estimate how long results might take.
4. **Optimise** — Build a specific plan, try it, and reflect again from Step 1.

## Three requirements of efficient skill development

Stacking fulfils all three simultaneously (Venn-diagram relationship — missing any one limits
progress):
1. Focus on important skills that make a real difference to ability.
2. Persist at improving the skill long enough to find mistakes, overcome them, and eventually
   reach habitual competence.
3. Recognise that performance depends on multiple processes working together as a system — the
   best marginal gain for a given moment evolves and changes as the learner evolves; thinking in
   systems, not isolated techniques.

When all three are met, stacking unlocks **exponential growth** (each attempt builds on the prior
one, compounding the 1% improvement and making further improvement progressively easier — see the
additive-vs-compounding distinction in ../../02-concepts/marginal-gains.md, flagged there as an
unresolved point of learner confusion) and **new habit formation** (without new habits, further
change requires too much ongoing effort and concentration).

## Good example: identifying the right kind of skill (Rayla)

Rayla finds note-taking time-consuming and targets a 1% improvement: "processing difficult
concepts into fewer keywords." Retention improves, but the process remains time-consuming. Two
candidate next gains are considered: focusing on BEDS-M (procrastination reduction) or focusing on
Prestudy (big-picture identification). BEDS-M does not build on the prior gain — reducing
procrastination does not itself enhance the deep processing and higher-order thinking that
effective note-taking depends on. Prestudy does build directly on it, since quickly understanding
key ideas strengthens the same deep-processing and higher-order-thinking skills note-taking
relies on. Prestudy is therefore the gain that stacks.

## Good example: persisting on a current skill (Rayla)

After two weeks working on non-linear note-taking and continuing to make mistakes, Rayla considers
switching to a new skill versus persisting. With a growth-mindset framing (finding mistakes is a
sign of progress), she assesses non-linear note-taking as a core skill worth mastering and,
because many mistakes have already been found (meaning significant progress has already been
made), chooses to persist. After another week, the remaining mistakes are found and the skill can
be performed correctly; the next marginal gains identified are accuracy, consistency, and
eventually speed and overall efficiency.

## Bad example: switching between skills too frequently (Rayla)

Rayla's improvement attempts, in sequence: tries non-linear note-taking and struggles → switches
to flashcards, makes some mistakes → decides procrastination is the bigger problem, tries
procrastination techniques → these do not work as well as hoped after one or two attempts →
switches to time management/scheduling instead → fails to follow the new schedule for a few days →
returns to note-taking, which has not improved. By attempting many things at once and switching
often, none of them is given sufficient experience and reflection to surface mistakes, be
understood, and be built on to a meaningful level — potential gains are lost, and weeks or months
can pass with very little real progress. This pattern is described as common and capable of
self-sabotaging progress.

## Detailed worked example: Sarah (essay writing)

Sarah has already made a 1% gain: faster brainstorming.

- **Reflect**: brainstorming helps generate ideas faster, but presenting them clearly in essays is
  still a struggle. Idea generation has improved over the last two weeks, but essay structure
  remains disorganised, and essays now take longer overall because more time goes into planning.
- **Evaluate**: idea-generation competence is improving; competence with organising/presenting
  ideas is still low, and Sarah is not aware of what specific mistakes she makes there. Speed and
  efficiency are deprioritised (expected to come with time); organisation of ideas is prioritised.
- **Define**: since the specific mistakes in organisation/planning are not yet known, the 1%
  improvement identified is simply becoming more self-aware of current tendencies, habits, and
  mistakes in this area. Estimated time: at least 10 hours over the next week to surface the major
  mistakes (these are long-standing habits), with another one to two weeks to experiment with
  possible fixes.
- **Optimise**: adopts the PEEL (Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link) essay-structuring technique.
  Trying it surfaces a different set of issues and mistakes — read as a sign that awareness of
  what "good" looks like for this skill is growing — and the cycle reflects again from this new
  starting point.
