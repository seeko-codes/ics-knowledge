## What to cover

### Correct framing (prestudy-guide)

Correct prestudying involves framing the material and creating organisational structure; this
should always be the first step of learning a topic, even when prestudy is later done so
comprehensively that classes or lectures become unnecessary. Correct framing means:

- Avoiding smaller details.
- Identifying the main ideas or groups of ideas.
- Identifying the key relationships between these ideas and groups of ideas.
- Gaining a big-picture understanding of the topic.

If comprehensive prestudy is not possible, doing only this framing step is still highly
beneficial for overall efficiency.

**Signs prestudy has been done correctly:**

- A clear big-picture understanding of the topic exists.
- The entire topic or sub-topic has been covered, not just a single class or lecture.
- No details or facts are memorised yet — focus was exclusively on the big picture.
- The major ideas of the topic are seen to relate to each other and could be grouped.
- Some hypotheses exist about how the concepts might relate to each other.
- There is a feeling of readiness to learn more detail.

Committing to details early activates isolated, lower-order learning processes that waste time
and make the content harder to understand — most learners misunderstand this and feel insecure
about not memorising details during prestudy, when omitting detail is in fact correct execution.

### Six principles (must-dos / basics version)

1. Group information based on how it is related.
2. Focus on the big picture; details come later.
3. Spend time on *why* something is important or relevant, rather than trying to remember or
   understand it directly.
4. Create analogies and connect the information to what is already known.
5. Study the entire topic, not individual classes or lectures, to preserve the full scope of
   relationships.
6. Aim to be shallow and broad, rather than deep and narrow.

This is where the program's chunking terminology is introduced: the process of taking
information and putting it into groups is called **chunking**; "chunks" are the resulting big
groups, "sub-chunks" are groups within groups.

If writing notes during prestudy, relational and non-linear note-taking (e.g. mindmaps) is
preferred, since it expresses complex relationships far more accurately than linear note-taking;
if writing is not viable, the mental exercise of correct priming and framing remains beneficial
on its own.

## Timing

Prestudy can happen at any point before the main learning event, depending on time availability.
Common patterns: prestudy the coming week's material during the prior weekend, or prestudy the
day's material the same morning. With moderately efficient technique, prestudying 4-6 weeks of
content in a single weekend is achievable, allowing a learner to move fully beyond class pace and
escape the exam study grind; where no dedicated time exists, even a 10-minute skim before class
is beneficial provided the framing principles above are still followed.

Skill-acquisition timeline: with daily practice, sufficient prestudy skill typically develops
after approximately 1-2 weeks — this range is approximate and self-paced.

## Snowballing schedule integration

General scheduling guidelines for sustaining prestudy alongside encoding and retrieval:

1. Use a simple 1-day / 1-week / 1-month revision spacing rule (or an existing compatible
   spacing schedule already in use).
2. Schedule retrieval sessions in the calendar and adhere to that schedule.
3. Prestudy material before class — this reduces the amount of revision needed later.
4. Schedule and protect prestudy time specifically, especially when busy.
5. **Snowball**: learn the topic superficially but broadly during prestudy, and add more in-depth
   knowledge on each subsequent pass through it.

### Weekly schedule template

| Day / time | Activity | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Sunday, anytime | Prestudy the week's material | Learn only the concepts and how they relate to each other. Do not memorise processes, steps, or definitions. Make a very basic mindmap. |
| End of every weekday (after class) | Revise the day's material | Build a more detailed understanding. Create flashcards and elaborate the mind map. Do not spend time memorising definitions or highly detailed information yet; do not make flashcards for conceptual information. |
| End of every week (e.g. Friday evening or Saturday) | Revise the entire week's material | Use an interleaved retrieval method; return to weaknesses and gaps. Begin memorising details. Teaching and creating challenge questions are a good initial technique here. |
| During the week, any available pocket of time | Go through flashcards | Getting through all of them is not required — use whatever pockets of time are available. |
| End of the month, on a weekend | Revise the entire month's material | Should be surprisingly easy, since forgetting has been kept low. Use another interleaved retrieval method; by this point most details should already be memorised via the ongoing flashcards. |
| Two to three weeks before a test or exam | Do more retrieval practice | Use another interleaved revision technique such as practice papers, challenge questions, or peer quizzing; brush up on persistently-forgotten details. |

If content volume is high, an additional revision session can be added mid-month (a fortnightly
review) or mid-week (a 2-day review). The exact days are adjustable (prestudy could move to
Saturday or Monday morning, or cover two weeks at a time instead of one). A more advanced version
of this schedule — prestudying an entire topic (4-6 weeks' worth) in one go — is developed at a
later stage; this template is the basic starting version.

**Preview over review**: prestudying saves revision time because less is forgotten going forward;
as prestudy technique improves, revision time decreases. Given a choice between good prestudy and
reviewing previous material, prestudy is chosen — review can always happen later, but prestudy
reduces how much there is to catch up on in the first place.

Difficulty following this schedule typically traces to one of two causes: (1) basic time-
management skill gaps, or (2) task-prioritisation errors, such as prioritising retrieval sessions
over quality prestudy sessions. Schedule competence is typically reached within a couple of weeks
of use, though fitting the schedule to individual needs takes some experimentation and weekly
reflection.

## In-class pairing

### Live-session guidelines

Live learning events (lectures, workshops, webinars) are synchronous and cannot be paused, which
makes them especially challenging — the learner is forced to think and learn at someone else's
pace. Guidelines for these sessions:

1. Always prestudy — prestudy improves retention, engagement, and enjoyment in class.
2. Focus prestudy on big-picture concepts, not details.
3. Always paraphrase and process information before writing notes.
4. Do not avoid feeling challenged — effective learning feels uncomfortable because the brain is
   actively working.
5. Write non-linear notes — mindmaps and spatial representations are more effective than linear
   notes.
6. Limit word-count — the more written, the less is being processed; learning happens in the
   brain, not on the page.

**Signal check — prestudy is being done correctly if it produces:**

- Less overwhelm.
- Greater ability to consolidate and make sense of information from class or lectures.
- General awareness of the main ideas that will be covered.
- Ability to ask deeper or more interconnected questions (where asking questions is possible).

Absence of these signals indicates an execution problem, to be corrected against the correct-
framing checklist above.

**Delaying note-taking**: difficulty thinking and processing before writing notes indicates a
weakness in deep processing rather than a fixed limitation. It is trained by delaying note-taking
for as long as possible — until information would otherwise be forgotten — and gradually
extending that delay over time. This is easier when information is constantly related to the big
picture and new information is simplified and grouped as it arrives.

### Training auditory-processing capacity during live sessions

A separate cause of in-class overwhelm is an as-yet underdeveloped capacity to process auditory
information efficiently; the fix is diligent training over time, run alongside prestudy rather
than instead of it. Procedure:

1. Listen, and connect new information to prior knowledge as much as possible — simplify,
   group/chunk, and find similarities/differences while the speaker is talking.
2. When no more information can be held mentally, quickly map all current thoughts onto notes.
3. Repeat from step 1, now referencing the mapped notes occasionally to find further connections,
   simplifications, and groupings.
4. Continue this cycle, aiming to process approximately 1% more information mentally per attempt
   before needing to offload it to notes.

**Benchmark**: at university level, being able to hold 10-15 minutes of dense-lecture content
mentally before needing to offload it signifies a high level of processing ability.

### Last-resort tactic: deliberately shallow prestudy

Where a mandatory class cannot be avoided, used discreetly, or replaced with self-study (see the
school-context workaround options), and would otherwise be wasted time, prestudy depth itself can
be reduced as a deliberate tactic: doing a less comprehensive, shallower prestudy than usual
increases the cognitive load and confusion experienced in class, which makes the otherwise-wasted
class time more engaging and challenging. The prestudy time saved by shortening prestudy this way
is then redirected to interleaved retrieval practice instead.
