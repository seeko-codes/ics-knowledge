## Why it exists

The full interleaving table is extensive, and it can take a long time to find the right
combination of methods within it and master them. Rather than halting progress while that
mastery develops, test-target-teach introduces high-yield techniques that work well for many
learners across most disciplines and levels of learning, as an interim measure.

## Test (practice instructions)

1. Apply existing knowledge.
2. Create original questions and also use premade questions.
3. Self-made questions can be shared with a small study group.

**Why use self-made *and* premade questions?** Creating a challenge or exam question can be more
effective than answering an existing past-paper or premade question — even without fully knowing
the correct answer, more is learned from creating the question and attempting it than from
answering a pre-existing one.

Use premade questions to: save time; familiarise with the assessment's style of questioning
(without guarantee that style won't change); find gaps in knowledge by testing in ways not
otherwise thought of.

Use self-made questions to: strengthen understanding of already-learned material from a different
angle; find knowledge gaps in already-learned content; practise retrieval across all the orders and
types of knowledge needed, since it is easier to target weaknesses when the type of retrieval
challenge is self-controlled.

> Over-reliance on premade questions is one reason curveball questions throw students off so much.
> Creating one's own questions is especially useful in subjects like maths and physics, which can
> trip up students with real-world applications of formulae and equations not usually considered.
> Good learners always use a combination of self-made and premade questions.

There is significant overlap between this Test step and the advanced group study method (see
03-details/techniques/advanced-group-method.md); a learner confident with the advanced group method
can use that instead.

## Target (practice instructions)

1. Look for areas of lower confidence, or areas not yet used/tested.
2. Start with areas of weakness even if it feels uncomfortable.

## Teach (practice instructions)

1. Some methods of teaching are more effective than others.
2. Don't just "explain" — teach. Change the order and approach used to present the information so
   it makes sense to someone who has not learned it before.

> An effective way to find weaknesses is to explain why a concept is important *before*
> introducing any terminology — which requires finding a way to explain it in lay terms. For
> example, imagine teaching the concept to a 10-year-old.

## Knowledge gap redefinition

A knowledge gap, for the purposes of this framework, is any of:
1. A part struggled to recall.
2. A part answered incorrectly.
3. A part felt unconfident about.

**Knowledge gaps are not only when something is gotten incorrect.**

Whenever a knowledge gap is found, review the relevant material and re-encode it using the methods
already taught — whichever method(s) were used to learn it initially can be reused, but try to
connect the information more deeply, or make sense of it from a different perspective, to improve
retention and depth.

## Competence criteria

Attempt all three steps (test, target, teach) at least once before moving on to the next set of
lessons. The skill can continue to be refined over time, but the gaps in knowledge found through
this technique will change how future learning is interpreted — it is beneficial to have this
experience at least once or twice before moving on.

Two signs indicate more practice time is needed before moving on:
1. **Struggling to apply these methods** — spend a couple more weeks practising to reap the
   benefits. This prevents overload and maximises learning efficiency.
2. **Finding it easy but not finding any knowledge gaps** — most likely indicates the technique was
   performed incorrectly. Unless knowledge is truly pristine, gaps should be found; retrieval
   techniques exist to find gaps, and finding none most likely means the attempt was ineffective.
   If the technique felt easy *and* was ineffective, it may indicate that not all necessary orders
   of learning or types of knowledge testing were covered — a signal to check whether the
   appropriate levels are being tested (see 02-concepts/revision-strategy.md's four gap types, and
   02-concepts/knowledge-types.md).
